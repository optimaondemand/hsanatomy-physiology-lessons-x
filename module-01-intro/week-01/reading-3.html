Why chemistry matters for biology
You are made of atoms. About 99 percent of your body’s mass comes from just six of them — oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, calcium, and phosphorus. The remaining one percent is a long list of trace elements (sodium, potassium, iron, zinc, and many more) that, despite tiny quantities, are essential for the body to function.
Elements of the Human Body. The main elements that compose the human body, shown from most to least abundant. (Image credit: OpenStax)
But atoms alone do not make life. Atoms become biology when they bond together into molecules, and the chemistry of those bonds — what holds, what breaks, what reacts — is what your cells, tissues, and organs are doing every second. Three chemical realities especially shape how the body works: water, pH, and the four families of biological macromolecules.
Water
The human body is roughly 60 percent water by mass. Inside cells, water is the medium in which biochemistry happens; outside cells, water carries blood, lymph, and urine.
Water has unusual chemical properties for a molecule its size. It is polar: the oxygen end pulls electrons more strongly than the hydrogen ends, leaving the molecule slightly negative on one side and slightly positive on the other. This polarity means water molecules attract each other (cohesion) and stick to other polar surfaces (adhesion), which is why water rises through plant stems, why your tears spread across your eye, and why blood does not just sit in your veins but flows.
Polar Covalent Bonds in a Water Molecule. (Image credit: OpenStax)
Hydrogen Bonds between Water Molecules. The bonds occur between the weakly positive hydrogen atoms and the weakly negative oxygen atoms. (Image credit: OpenStax)
Water is also an excellent solvent. Polar substances and most ionic compounds — salt, glucose, amino acids, ions like sodium and potassium — dissolve in it readily. Most of biochemistry happens in solution, which means most of biochemistry happens in water.
Water as a solvent. (Image credit: NurseHub)
Finally, water has high heat capacity — it absorbs and releases heat slowly. This buffers your body against temperature swings, and it is why sweating works as a cooling system: as water evaporates from your skin, it pulls a relatively large amount of heat with it.
pH and acid–base balance
pH measures the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in a solution. A low pH (lots of H⁺) is acidic; a high pH (few H⁺) is basic, or alkaline. The pH scale runs from 0 to 14, with 7 being neutral. Each step on the scale represents a tenfold change in H⁺ concentration — a pH of 4 has ten times the H⁺ of pH 5, and a hundred times the H⁺ of pH 6.
The pH reference scale. (Image credit: OpenStax)
The body holds the pH of blood between 7.35 and 7.45. Outside that narrow window, proteins begin to lose their shape, enzymes stop working, and the chemistry of cells breaks down. To stay inside the window, the body uses buffers: chemical systems that absorb excess H⁺ when fluids become too acidic, and release H⁺ when fluids become too basic. The most important blood buffer is the bicarbonate system, which you will meet again in Module 9 (respiratory) and Module 11 (urinary).
Blood pH regulation. (Image credit: Scoolam Foundation)
Different parts of the body operate at very different pH values. The stomach holds a pH near 1.5 — powerful acid, for digesting proteins. The small intestine sits closer to 8 — slightly basic, matching the enzymes that work there. The skin’s surface is mildly acidic (around 5.5), which discourages many bacteria from growing on it. Each value is matched to the chemistry happening at that location.
The four biological macromolecules
Almost everything inside your cells is a member of one of four molecular families. Each family is defined by a shared kind of building block.
Carbohydrates
Made from sugar units. Mono- means “one,” di- means “two,” poly- means “many,” and sacchar- (as in saccharide) comes from the Greek for “sweet” — the same root, glyco-, that gives us “sugar.” So a single sugar (glucose, fructose) is a monosaccharide; two sugars linked together (sucrose, lactose) form a disaccharide; long chains are polysaccharides (glycogen, starch, cellulose). You may notice that sugars typically end in -ose — glucose, fructose, sucrose, lactose. Carbohydrates serve two main roles in the body: short-term energy (glucose burned in cells; glycogen stored in muscle and liver) and, less commonly in animals, structure (cellulose in plant cell walls is critical as dietary fiber even though humans cannot digest it).

Lipids
A chemically diverse family that share one trait: they do not dissolve well in water. Triglycerides (fats and oils) are the body’s long-term energy storage — the name comes from tri-, “three,” because each one is a glycerol molecule bonded to three fatty acid chains. Phospholipids form the membranes that wrap every cell and every organelle. Steroids — including cholesterol and the sex hormones you will meet in Modules 6 and 12 — are made of four interlocked carbon rings.
(a) Saturated vs. (b) unsaturated fatty acid chains — the double bond in the unsaturated chain creates the “kink” that keeps unsaturated fats liquid at room temperature. (Image credit: OpenStax)
Formation of a triglyceride: three fatty acid chains bond to glycerol by dehydration synthesis, releasing three water molecules. (Image credit: OpenStax)
Lipid structures: (a) phospholipids, (b) sterols (e.g. cholesterol), and (c) prostaglandins. (Image credit: OpenStax)
Proteins
Made from amino acids — twenty different ones — strung together in chains and folded into specific three-dimensional shapes. The shape determines what the protein does. Some proteins act as enzymes (the topic of next lesson) — you can often spot one by name, since enzymes typically end in -ase (lactase, amylase, proteinase). Some are structural (collagen in connective tissue, keratin in hair and skin). Some transport (hemoglobin carries oxygen; albumin carries fats). Some signal (many hormones are proteins). Almost everything that does work in your body is a protein.
The general structure of an amino acid, built around a central (alpha) carbon bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, and a variable side chain (R group). (Image credit: OpenStax)
Levels of protein structure: (a) primary, (b) secondary, (c) tertiary, and (d) quaternary, shown for hemoglobin, a globular protein. (Image credit: OpenStax)
Nucleic acids
Made from nucleotide units. There are two of them: DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the storage molecule, holding the genetic instructions that make you you; RNA (ribonucleic acid) carries those instructions to where they are read and turned into proteins. Nucleic acids are the information layer of the body.
Nucleotide units — a nitrogenous base (purine or pyrimidine), a pentose sugar, and a phosphate group — the building blocks of DNA and RNA. (Image credit: OpenStax)
DNA. In the DNA double helix, two strands attach via hydrogen bonds between the bases (adenine–thymine, guanine–cytosine) of the component nucleotides. (Image credit: OpenStax)
These four families do not work in isolation. A single ribosome — the cellular machine that builds proteins — is itself made of RNA bound to proteins, fueled by ATP (a nucleotide), embedded in a cell whose membrane is a phospholipid bilayer. Biology is the chemistry of these four families interacting.
📊 CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH — SEEING THE MOLECULES
Most of what we used to know about the shape of large biological molecules came from X-ray crystallography, a technique that requires the molecule to be coaxed into a crystal first. Many biological molecules refused to crystallize. They were studied indirectly, or not at all.

In recent years, a method called cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has changed this. The molecule is flash-frozen in a thin layer of ice and imaged with an electron beam at very low temperatures. Many individual particles are imaged at slightly different angles, and computers reconstruct the three-dimensional shape from the images. Researchers are now imaging the structures of viral capsids, ribosomes, ion channels, and entire cellular machines that were beyond reach a decade ago. The technique earned a Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2017, and the scientific community is still working out the limits of what it can resolve.
The chemistry you have just read is the foundation of everything that follows in this course. You do not need to memorize every detail — but you need to remember that every cell, every tissue, every organ, every system is, at bottom, four families of molecules cooperating in water at a tightly controlled pH.
