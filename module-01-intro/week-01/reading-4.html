Enzymes — Biological Catalysts
Reading
📖 READING
What an enzyme is
An enzyme is a biological catalyst — a molecule that speeds up a chemical reaction without being consumed by it. The vast majority of enzymes are proteins. (A small minority are RNA molecules called ribozymes; they are exceptions.) When you eat starchy food, an enzyme in your saliva called amylase begins breaking the starch into smaller sugars before you have even swallowed. When your blood carries carbon dioxide back to your lungs, an enzyme called carbonic anhydrase converts CO₂ and water into bicarbonate ion thousands of times per second. Without enzymes, the chemistry of your body would happen so slowly that you could not exist.
The number of reactions an enzyme can catalyze per second is staggering. Carbonic anhydrase can process about a million CO₂ molecules per second. Catalase, an enzyme that decomposes hydrogen peroxide, can process tens of millions per second. The body’s chemistry runs at the speed of life because enzymes make it run fast.
How enzymes work
A reaction usually requires energy to start — an “energy hill” called the activation energy that the reactants must climb before they can convert into products. The higher the hill, the slower the reaction. Enzymes lower the hill.
Enzymes decrease the activation energy required for a given chemical reaction to occur. (a) Without an enzyme, the energy input needed is high. (b) With an enzyme, less energy is needed. (Image credit: OpenStax)
They do this by binding to their substrate — the molecule the reaction acts on — at a specific pocket called the active site. The active site is shaped to fit the substrate the way a glove fits a hand. When substrate enters the active site, the enzyme typically shifts shape slightly to clamp around it (a phenomenon called induced fit). The chemistry happens inside this confined space, where the enzyme can stress, position, or stabilize the substrate — dramatically lowering the activation energy. The product is then released, and the enzyme — unchanged — moves on to its next substrate.
Enzyme–substrate complex. (Image credit: TimVickers/Fvasconcellos, Wikimedia Commons, public domain)
Most enzymes are highly specific. Lactase breaks down lactose. Sucrase breaks down sucrose. They will not interchange. The active site shape is the reason — only the matching substrate can enter, and only the matching reaction can happen.
Lactase enzyme function. (Image credit: LabXchange)
Factors affecting enzyme activity
Enzymes work best within a narrow set of physical conditions.
Temperature
As temperature rises, molecules move faster and reactions speed up — until the temperature gets too high and the protein denatures, losing the precise shape its active site depends on. Most human enzymes have an optimum around 37°C (body temperature). At 41°C — the temperature of a high fever — many enzymes are already losing efficiency. This is one reason high fevers are medically dangerous.
pH
Each enzyme has an optimum pH. Pepsin, the protein-digesting enzyme in the stomach, works best around pH 1.5–2 — exactly the acid environment the stomach maintains. Trypsin, a protein-digesting enzyme in the small intestine, works best around pH 8 — exactly the slightly basic environment there. Move pepsin to the small intestine and it stops working; move trypsin into the stomach and it stops working too.
Enzyme activity across different pH values. (Image source: Save My Exams)
Substrate concentration
Reaction rate rises as substrate concentration rises — until all the active sites are occupied. Beyond that point, adding more substrate does not speed anything up. The enzyme is “saturated.”
Enzyme concentration
With substrate plentiful, more enzyme means a faster reaction.
Inhibition and regulation
The body needs to control when reactions happen, which means it needs to slow enzymes down or shut them off. Competitive inhibitors are molecules that resemble the substrate enough to enter the active site, blocking the real substrate without being processed. Noncompetitive inhibitors bind elsewhere on the enzyme, distorting its shape and disabling the active site indirectly. Many medications and many natural toxins work as enzyme inhibitors.
Normal enzyme activity compared with competitive inhibition (blocks the active site directly) and noncompetitive inhibition (binds elsewhere and distorts the active site).
A particularly elegant form of regulation is feedback inhibition: when the end product of a multi-step pathway accumulates, it binds to and slows down an enzyme earlier in the same pathway. The cell stops producing what it already has enough of. You will see this pattern throughout the course.

📊 CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH — ENZYMES DESIGNED IN THE LAB
Most enzymes used in industry — the enzymes in laundry detergent, the enzymes that convert corn into syrup, the enzymes that make leather and cheese — are not the enzymes nature provides. They are engineered.

The technique is called directed evolution. Researchers introduce random mutations into the gene that codes for an enzyme, generating thousands of slightly different versions. They then screen the versions for whichever performs best at the task they care about — stability at high temperature, faster reaction rate, tolerance for industrial chemicals. The best performers are mutated again, and the cycle repeats. Over many rounds, the enzyme has effectively been “evolved” in the lab to do something it never did in nature.

The 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry recognized this technique. Researchers continue to apply it to design enzymes for everything from carbon-capture chemistry to plastic-degrading reactions.
Every reaction your body needs in order to live is sped along by an enzyme. Understanding what controls enzyme activity is understanding what controls the body’s chemistry.
