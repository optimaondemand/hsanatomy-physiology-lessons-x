📖 READING
Anatomical position
Before anatomy can have a vocabulary, it needs a starting pose. Anatomical position is the universal reference posture for describing the human body: the body stands upright, faces forward, with the feet flat on the floor and slightly apart, the arms hanging at the sides, and the palms facing forward. Every anatomical direction we are about to define assumes the body is in this position. When you read that the heart is “deep to the sternum,” that statement is true with the body in anatomical position, regardless of whether the actual person is currently lying down or standing on their head.
This convention exists because the body can twist, bend, and rotate into nearly any shape, and a description that depended on the body’s current posture would constantly contradict itself. Anatomical position is the agreed-upon zero — the pose from which every direction is measured.

💼 CAREER CONNECTIONS
Body planes are not just a classroom exercise — they are how medical imaging is organized. A radiologic technologist positions patients and equipment to capture images along a specific plane. A radiologist reads CT and MRI scans stacked as transverse sections, essentially flipping through the body slice by slice. An MRI technologist chooses which plane to scan in based on what a physician needs to see — a sagittal scan of the spine looks nothing like a transverse scan of the abdomen, even though both describe the same body. Every one of these careers depends on the same vocabulary you are learning this week.
Directional terms
Anatomical directions come in pairs of opposites.
Superior (toward the head) / Inferior (toward the feet). The skull is superior to the pelvis. The pelvis is inferior to the lungs.
Anterior (toward the front) / Posterior (toward the back). The sternum is anterior to the spine. The spine is posterior to the heart. (In humans, ventral is roughly equivalent to anterior and dorsal to posterior. In four-legged animals, those terms become more useful.)
Medial (toward the body’s midline) / Lateral (away from the midline). The nose is medial to the eyes. The shoulder is lateral to the sternum.
Proximal (toward the trunk — used on limbs) / Distal (away from the trunk — used on limbs). The shoulder is proximal to the elbow. The hand is distal to the elbow.
Superficial (closer to the body’s surface) / Deep (farther from the surface). The skin is superficial to the muscles. The bones of the wrist are deep to the tendons of the forearm.
Directional terms applied to the human body: superior/inferior, anterior/posterior, medial/lateral, and proximal/distal. (Image credit: OpenStax)
Superficial and deep, illustrated. (Image credit: LabXchange)
These pairs are tools. Every anatomical description in this course will use them, and they are how clinicians describe injuries, surgeries, and medical findings. Learn them now and you will save yourself confusion every week of the year.
Body planes
A plane is a flat slice through the body, used to view its internal anatomy. Three planes are standard.
Sagittal plane — a vertical plane that divides the body into left and right portions. If the cut runs exactly down the midline, splitting left and right symmetrically, it is the midsagittal (or median) plane. If it runs parallel to the midline but off-center, it is a parasagittal plane.
Frontal plane (also called the coronal plane) — a vertical plane that divides the body into anterior (front) and posterior (back) portions. CT scans of the chest, taken to view the lungs from the front, are commonly displayed in the frontal plane.
Transverse plane (also called the horizontal plane or cross-section) — a horizontal plane that divides the body into superior (upper) and inferior (lower) portions. A standard CT scan of the abdomen takes transverse sections, stacking them like slices of bread.
The sagittal, frontal (coronal), and transverse body planes. (Image credit: OpenStax)
Each plane has clinical use — different organs reveal different features depending on the angle of the slice. You will practice this directly on the figure you build this week.
Body regions
The body is broadly divided into two regions: the axial part (head, neck, and trunk — the body’s central axis) and the appendicular part (the four limbs, which append to the axial body). You will return to these terms in Module 3 with the skeletal system.
The axial and appendicular regions of the body. (Image credit: RegisteredNurseRN.com)
Within these regions, anatomy uses a thick vocabulary of named subregions — cervical (neck), thoracic (chest), abdominal (between chest and pelvis), pelvic (hip area), brachial (upper arm), antebrachial (forearm), crural (lower leg), and many more. You do not need to memorize them all this week. You will learn each as it becomes relevant.
Body cavities
The body’s organs sit inside two large cavities, each containing smaller subdivisions.
The dorsal cavity runs along the body’s posterior surface and is divided into the cranial cavity (inside the skull, holding the brain) and the vertebral cavity (inside the vertebral column, holding the spinal cord).
The ventral cavity runs along the body’s anterior surface and is divided by the diaphragm into the thoracic cavity (above the diaphragm) and the abdominopelvic cavity (below).
Dorsal and Ventral Body Cavities. The ventral cavity includes the thoracic and abdominopelvic cavities and their subdivisions; the dorsal cavity includes the cranial and spinal cavities. (Image credit: OpenStax)
The thoracic cavity contains the pleural cavities (around each lung), the pericardial cavity (around the heart), and the mediastinum (the central region holding the heart, esophagus, and major vessels). The abdominopelvic cavity contains the abdominal cavity (digestive organs, kidneys, spleen) and the pelvic cavity (bladder, reproductive organs, and the end of the digestive tract).
Due to its size, the abdominopelvic cavity is further subdivided for clinical description — into either nine regions (for precise anatomical reference) or four quadrants (for quick clinical shorthand). Two of the nine regions borrow directly from Greek: the epigastric region (epi-, “upon” or “above,” + gastric, “stomach”) sits above the stomach, and the paired hypochondriac regions (hypo-, “below,” + chondro-, “cartilage”) sit below the rib cartilage on either side.
Regions and Quadrants of the Peritoneal Cavity. (a) Nine abdominal regions and (b) four abdominal quadrants. (Image credit: OpenStax)
These cavities are lined by thin double-layered serous membranes that secrete a slippery fluid, allowing organs to slide against the cavity walls without friction. You will meet specific examples — pleura, pericardium, peritoneum — in their respective modules.
A first look at homeostasis
The body is constantly under threat: ambient temperature changes, food intake fluctuates, oxygen demand rises during exercise, water is lost through breathing and sweating. And yet inside the body, conditions stay remarkably constant. Body temperature stays close to 37°C (98.6°F). Blood pH stays in the narrow range 7.35–7.45. Blood glucose fluctuates, but is held to a stable working range. The maintenance of these stable internal conditions in the face of external change is called homeostasis — from homeo (“similar”) and stasis (“standing”).
Every homeostatic mechanism shares the same four parts. A variable is the condition being regulated (body temperature, blood pH, blood glucose). A sensor (or receptor) detects when the variable drifts from its set point. An integrating center (or control center) — often a region of the brain — compares the sensor’s signal to the set point and decides on a response. An effector carries out that response, pushing the variable back toward normal. You will see this same four-part loop — variable, sensor, integrating center, effector — again and again this year, in nearly every organ system.
(a) A negative feedback loop and (b) its application to body-temperature regulation. (Image credit: OpenStax)
Homeostasis is the master organizing principle of physiology. Every organ system, in some way, contributes to it. The thermoregulation that keeps you near 37°C is mostly cardiovascular and integumentary. The maintenance of blood pH is mostly respiratory and renal. The control of blood glucose is mostly endocrine. As you walk through the eleven systems this year, ask of every organ: what variable does this help to keep stable?
💡 HENRY GRAY AND THE BOOK THAT NEVER WENT OUT OF PRINT
Henry Gray (1827–1861) was a British surgeon and anatomist who, at the age of 31, published Anatomy: Descriptive and Surgical — the textbook now universally known as Gray’s Anatomy. He had been working at St. George’s Hospital Medical School in London, where he taught dissection. His goal was a textbook that pruned the bloated, ornamental anatomy literature of his era into something a medical student could actually use: clear descriptions, accurate engravings, and direct surgical relevance.

The first edition appeared in 1858. It was 750 pages and contained 363 illustrations engraved by his colleague Henry Vandyke Carter. It was an immediate success. Gray died of smallpox just three years later, at age 34, having only seen the second edition through to publication. He never saw what his book would become. Gray’s Anatomy has been continuously revised for over 165 years, has gone through more than 40 editions, and is still standard reference reading in medical schools around the world.

The book remains in print partly because Gray and Carter understood something important: anatomy is best learned not from prose alone, but from prose paired with careful, accurate, beautiful images. The principle still holds. It is why this course pairs every reading with drawing, labeling, and building tasks of your own.
You will return to homeostasis in detail in Module 6 (Endocrine), where the body’s most carefully regulated variables become most clearly visible. For this lesson, treat it as the question you carry forward: what is being held steady, and which systems are doing the holding?
