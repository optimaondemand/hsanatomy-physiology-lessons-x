Coronary Circulation: The Heart That Gives, Fed By Itself
Reading
📖 READING
A pump that cannot use the blood it pumps
The chambers of the heart are filled with blood, but the muscle of the heart wall cannot get its oxygen from that blood. The wall is too thick — oxygen can only diffuse a few cell layers from the chamber lining (endocardium). Like every other organ in the body, the heart needs its own dedicated arterial supply and its own venous drainage. This is the coronary circulation — the heart’s personal circuit.
The myocardium is one of the most metabolically demanding tissues in the body. At rest it takes about 5% of total cardiac output for itself; during heavy exercise that can climb to over 10%. The cells need oxygen continuously — even brief interruptions cause symptoms within seconds and irreversible damage within minutes. The story of coronary circulation is the story of how this need is met, and what happens when it is not.
The coronary arteries
The coronary arteries are the first branches off the aorta, just above the aortic valve. Their openings — the coronary ostia — are positioned so that during ventricular diastole, when the aortic valve closes and pressure in the aorta is highest, blood pours into them. The heart, paradoxically, feeds itself when it is relaxing.
A. Anterior view of coronary circulation, showing the right coronary artery, the path of the left coronary artery, the circumflex artery, and the anterior interventricular artery, along with the great and small cardiac veins.
Left coronary artery (LCA)
The left coronary artery emerges from the left side of the aorta. It is short — usually no more than 2 cm — before it splits into two major branches:
Left anterior descending (LAD) artery — travels down the anterior interventricular sulcus toward the apex. It supplies the front of the left ventricle, the apex, and the front of the interventricular septum (including most of the conduction system). The LAD is famously called the “widow-maker” in clinical medicine: a blockage in its proximal segment is one of the most lethal heart attacks because it deprives so much working myocardium of oxygen at once.
Circumflex artery — wraps around to the left side and back of the heart, traveling in the coronary sulcus. It supplies the left atrium and the lateral and posterior walls of the left ventricle.
Right coronary artery (RCA)
The right coronary artery emerges from the right side of the aorta and travels in the coronary sulcus toward the back. It supplies the right atrium, the SA node and the AV node in most people, the right ventricle, and (in about 85% of people) the back of the left ventricle through a terminal branch called the posterior descending artery (PDA). Whether the PDA arises from the RCA or the circumflex defines right-dominant vs. left-dominant coronary anatomy.
The coronary veins
Blood drained from the heart muscle returns through veins that run alongside the coronary arteries. The largest is the great cardiac vein, which travels with the LAD up the anterior interventricular sulcus. It joins other cardiac veins to form the coronary sinus, a wide vein on the back of the heart that empties directly into the right atrium. The coronary venous return therefore goes back into the heart that pumped it — the only major venous return that does not arrive via the SVC or IVC.

What can go wrong — coronary artery disease
Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the slow narrowing of one or more coronary arteries by plaques of cholesterol, calcium, and fibrous tissue — a process called atherosclerosis. The arteries become rigid and the opening shrinks. Blood flow to the downstream myocardium falls. In the early stages this causes angina — chest pain on exertion that goes away with rest, because the heart at rest can still meet its reduced supply, but at higher demand it cannot.
Coronary artery disease: plaque narrows a coronary artery, reducing blood flow to the myocardium it supplies and producing the chest pain of angina on exertion.
A heart attack (myocardial infarction, MI) happens when a plaque suddenly ruptures, a clot forms on top of it, and blood flow to a region of myocardium stops. The deprived muscle starts to die within minutes. The location of the dead muscle — the infarct — depends on which artery was blocked. A proximal LAD occlusion infarcts the entire anterior left ventricle and is among the most dangerous. A right-coronary occlusion can take out the SA node and produce severe bradycardia or complete heart block in addition to wall damage.
Heart attack (myocardial infarction): plaque and a blood clot block a coronary artery, cutting off blood flow and causing the myocardium downstream to die. (Cleveland Clinic)
CAD is the leading cause of death in the developed world. The major modifiable risk factors — high blood pressure, high LDL cholesterol, diabetes, smoking, obesity, sedentary lifestyle — have been understood for decades, and the public-health interventions that target them have dramatically reduced CAD mortality in the past forty years. They have not eliminated it.
🔬 CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH — LVADs AND THE TOTAL ARTIFICIAL HEART
For patients whose hearts have failed beyond what medication or stents can fix, the options are a heart transplant or a mechanical assist device. Transplants save lives but are limited by the number of donor hearts available — only about 3,500 happen worldwide each year.

A left ventricular assist device (LVAD) is a small pump implanted in the chest that takes over for a failing left ventricle. The patient’s native heart still beats, and blood from the left ventricle is routed through the pump on its way to the aorta. LVADs were originally meant as a bridge to transplant — keeping a patient alive long enough for a donor heart to become available. Increasingly they are used as destination therapy: long-term support for patients who will never get a transplant. Some patients have lived with an LVAD for more than ten years.
LVAD (pump): a tube carries blood from the left ventricle to the aorta through the implanted pump, which is powered through a driveline connected to an external controller and power source.
A simpler, openly licensed illustration of the same LVAD setup. (WikiMedia Commons)
A total artificial heart (TAH) replaces both ventricles entirely. The two TAH systems with current FDA approval can keep a patient alive for months while awaiting transplant. They are large, noisy, and require an external pump console; the engineering challenges of a fully implantable, durable, biocompatible replacement heart remain unsolved despite decades of work. Scientists are currently exploring smaller continuous-flow pumps, biological-mechanical hybrids, and even tissue-engineered hearts grown from a patient’s own stem cells on a decellularized scaffold. None of these is yet a routine clinical reality.
Total artificial heart: in cardiomyopathy, the diseased ventricles are removed; the total artificial heart replaces them with mechanical right and left ventricles. (Cleveland Clinic)

▶  Self-check: name the three named coronary arteries and which part of the heart each supplies
Left anterior descending (LAD): front of the left ventricle, apex, front of the interventricular septum (including most of the conduction system).
Circumflex: left atrium, lateral and posterior walls of the left ventricle.
Right coronary artery (RCA): right atrium, SA + AV nodes (in most people), right ventricle, posterior wall of the left ventricle (via the PDA in right-dominant hearts).

Bonus: why does the heart receive most of its blood during diastole, not systole? During ventricular systole, the contracting myocardium compresses the small arteries that supply it. Blood can only flow freely through the coronaries when the muscle relaxes.
