📖 READING
A self-firing pump
A muscle that needs a nerve impulse to contract is called neurogenic. The skeletal muscle in your biceps will not move unless a motor neuron fires. The heart is different. It is myogenic: the contraction begins inside the heart itself, in specialized cardiac muscle cells that fire rhythmically on their own. Cut the heart out of the body, give it nutrients and oxygen, and it will keep beating in a dish. The nervous system can speed it up or slow it down — we will see how next module — but it cannot start or stop it.
This rhythm comes from the heart’s conduction system: a small population of cardiac cells, not contractile but electrically active, that generate the heartbeat and broadcast it through the rest of the muscle.
The conduction pathway
An electrical signal travels through the heart in a fixed sequence at every beat. The order matters: it ensures that atria contract before ventricles, and that the ventricles squeeze from the apex upward rather than from the top down.
1. The SA node — the pacemaker
The sinoatrial (SA) node is a cluster of about 10,000 specialized cells in the upper wall of the right atrium, near where the SVC enters. SA nodal cells are leaky — sodium and calcium drift in slowly between beats, so the cell’s membrane voltage drifts upward until it triggers an action potential, all by itself, about 60–100 times per minute in an adult at rest. Each time it fires, the SA node sends an electrical wave outward through both atria, causing them to contract together. The SA node is the heart’s natural pacemaker.
2. The AV node — the pause
The electrical signal converges on the atrioventricular (AV) node, a smaller cluster of cells in the floor of the right atrium near the tricuspid valve. The AV node deliberately slows the signal — for about 0.1 seconds. This pause is functionally essential: it gives the atria time to finish contracting and pushing their blood into the ventricles before the ventricles themselves squeeze.
3. The bundle of His — the cable
After the AV node delay, the signal enters the bundle of His (the AV bundle), a tract of conducting fibers that runs down the interventricular septum. The bundle of His is the only electrical connection between the atria and the ventricles — everywhere else, a non-conducting fibrous ring (the cardiac skeleton) separates the two.
4. The bundle branches — the split
Roughly halfway down the septum, the bundle of His splits into left and right bundle branches, which travel toward the apex along each side of the septum.
5. The Purkinje fibers — the spray
The bundle branches give way to a fine network of Purkinje fibers that spread across the inner surface of each ventricle, carrying the signal to every contractile cell. Because Purkinje fibers conduct very fast and reach the apex first, the ventricles contract from the bottom up — which squeezes the blood toward the great vessels at the top.

Memorize the order, because it appears in every cardiology textbook and on every cardiac exam: SA node → AV node → Bundle of His → Bundle branches → Purkinje fibers.

The cardiac cycle
One full heartbeat — one cardiac cycle — is roughly 0.8 seconds at a resting heart rate of 75 bpm. The cycle has two main phases:
Systole — the contraction phase. Ventricular systole is the phase that ejects blood into the pulmonary trunk and aorta. It lasts about 0.3 seconds in a resting heart.
Diastole — the relaxation/filling phase. The ventricles relax, the AV valves open, and blood pours in from the atria. Diastole lasts about 0.5 seconds in a resting heart. The heart spends more time relaxing than working — otherwise the muscle could not recover.
When blood pressure is reported as “120 over 80,” the 120 is the systemic arterial pressure during ventricular systole, and the 80 is the lowest pressure between beats (diastole). The two numbers are the high and low of the same pressure wave.
Figure 19.19 — Cardiac Conduction. (1) The sinoatrial (SA) node and the remainder of the conduction system are at rest. (2) The SA node initiates the action potential, which sweeps across the atria. (3) After reaching the atrioventricular node, there is a delay of approximately 100 ms that allows the atria to complete pumping blood before the impulse is transmitted to the atrioventricular bundle. (4) Following the delay, the impulse travels through the atrioventricular bundle and bundle branches to the Purkinje fibers, and also reaches the right papillary muscle via the moderator band. (5) The impulse spreads to the contractile fibers of the ventricle. (6) Ventricular contraction begins.
The two heart sounds
A normal heart makes two sounds per beat, traditionally written “lub-dub.” They are not the muscle contracting — they are the valves closing.
S1 (“lub”) — the longer, lower-pitched first sound. It is the closure of the AV valves (tricuspid + mitral) at the start of ventricular systole.
S2 (“dub”) — the shorter, higher-pitched second sound. It is the closure of the semilunar valves (pulmonary + aortic) at the start of ventricular diastole.
An abnormal whooshing sound between or during S1/S2 is a heart murmur, usually caused by turbulent flow through a stenotic or regurgitant valve. The pattern of which murmur occurs at which point in the cycle is one of the central diagnostic skills of cardiology.

💡 RENÉ LAENNEC AND THE INVENTION OF THE STETHOSCOPE
In 1816, in a hospital in Paris, the French physician René Laennec (1781–1826) had a patient with suspected heart disease — a stout young woman whose chest he felt it would be improper to press his ear against. Searching for an alternative, he recalled a children’s game in which a long piece of wood transmitted scratches from one end to the other. He rolled up a notebook, placed one end against the patient’s chest and the other against his own ear, and was astonished. He could hear the heart sounds more clearly than he ever had directly.

Within months he had built a wooden cylindrical “chest examiner” — the first stéthoscope, from the Greek stethos (chest) and skopein (to look). He published his book De l’auscultation médiate in 1819, cataloguing the heart and lung sounds his stethoscope could distinguish — including normal S1 and S2, the murmurs of valve disease, and the crackles of pneumonia. He died of tuberculosis at 45, having heard the disease in his own lungs through his own instrument.

The stethoscope did something no medical tool had ever done: it let a physician hear an organ working from the outside. It is the reason every doctor still wears one around their neck. For a hundred and fifty years it was the single most important diagnostic instrument in medicine. Even today, with ultrasound and echocardiography available in every clinic, the stethoscope remains the first tool reached for when a patient walks into an examination room.
The ECG — a recording of the cycle
An electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) is a recording of the heart’s electrical activity as picked up by electrodes on the skin. A normal ECG shows three named waves per beat:
P wave — atrial depolarization. The atria are about to contract.
QRS complex — ventricular depolarization. The ventricles are about to contract. The brief atrial repolarization happens during this window and is hidden by the larger QRS signal.
T wave — ventricular repolarization. The ventricles are about to relax.

Figure 19.24 — ECG Tracing Correlated to the Cardiac Cycle. This diagram correlates an ECG tracing with the electrical and mechanical events of a heart contraction. Each segment of an ECG tracing corresponds to one event in the cardiac cycle.
Abnormalities in shape, timing, or order of these waves are the language of clinical electrocardiography. Heart attacks, arrhythmias, electrolyte disturbances, and conduction blocks all leave characteristic signatures.
Three of the most common rhythm anomalies are worth knowing by name. Tachycardia is a resting heart rate above 100 bpm — the SA node is still setting the pace, just too fast for the heart to fill completely between beats. Bradycardia is a resting heart rate below 60 bpm; this is normal in well-conditioned athletes (a stronger heart ejects more blood per beat, so it needs fewer beats per minute), but in someone else it can signal a conduction problem. An ectopic beat is a heartbeat triggered by an electrical signal that starts somewhere other than the SA node — a patch of atrial or ventricular muscle firing on its own, out of sequence with the normal SA → AV → Purkinje pathway. Ectopic beats are usually felt as a “skipped” or extra beat and are common and often harmless, though frequent or sustained ectopic activity can disrupt the heart’s pumping efficiency. All three show up on an ECG as a departure from the normal, evenly spaced P-QRS-T pattern — which is exactly why the ECG is the tool clinicians reach for first.
