Reading 7.06
Inside the Heart: Chambers, Valves, and the Septum
Reading
📖 READING
A house with four rooms
If you cut a heart open along a vertical plane from base to apex, you see four chambers separated by a continuous wall down the middle. The two right chambers handle deoxygenated blood. The two left chambers handle oxygenated blood. Between them, valves open and close to keep the flow strictly one-way. The whole structure is built so that on every beat, the blood goes through the same path in the same direction with as little leakage as possible.
The three layers of the heart wall
Before we go inside the chambers, look at the wall itself. From outside in:
Epicardium — the outermost layer, also called the visceral pericardium. The thin glistening surface you saw last lesson.
The heart wall’s three layers — epicardium, myocardium, and endocardium — and their continuity with the surrounding serous and fibrous pericardium.
Myocardium — the thick middle layer of cardiac muscle. This is the muscle that contracts. It is built of cardiac muscle cells (cardiomyocytes) joined end-to-end at intercalated discs — specialized junctions that allow electrical signals to spread rapidly cell-to-cell across the entire chamber. The myocardium is much thicker in the left ventricle than in the right (about 10–15 mm vs. 3–5 mm), reflecting the pressure each must generate.
Figure 10.22 — Cardiac Muscle. Intercalated discs are part of the cardiac muscle sarcolemma and they contain gap junctions and desmosomes.
Endocardium — the innermost layer, a smooth simple-squamous epithelium continuous with the lining of all the blood vessels. It exists for the same reason vessel endothelium does: a smooth lining minimizes turbulence and friction. The valves are folds of endocardium reinforced with connective tissue.
The interventricular septum
The wall between the right and left ventricles is the interventricular septum. It is built almost entirely of myocardium, with a small upper segment that is membranous. The septum keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood from mixing. A hole in the septum — a ventricular septal defect, the most common congenital heart defect — allows the higher-pressure left ventricle to push blood into the right ventricle, eventually overloading the lungs. Some VSDs close on their own; others require surgical repair.
There is also an interatrial septum between the right and left atria. Before birth, this septum has a flap valve called the foramen ovale that allows blood to bypass the non-functional lungs. It normally closes at birth; we will return to this in Lesson 7.12 (fetal circulation).
The four chambers from the inside
Right atrium. Receives the SVC, IVC, and the coronary sinus (which drains the heart’s own veins). The wall is thin — just enough muscle to push blood across into the right ventricle.
Right ventricle. Pumps blood into the pulmonary trunk under modest pressure (the lungs are right next door, with low-resistance vessels). The wall is moderately thick. Its inner surface shows trabeculae carneae (literally “meaty ridges”) and rope-like structures called chordae tendineae that anchor the tricuspid valve to small muscle bumps called papillary muscles.
Left atrium. Receives the four pulmonary veins. Like the right atrium, it has a thin wall — just enough to deliver blood into the left ventricle.
Left ventricle. Pumps blood into the aorta under high pressure to reach the entire body. Its wall is dramatically thicker than the right’s, with the same trabeculae carneae, chordae tendineae, and papillary muscles — just more of them, and bigger. The left ventricle is the workhorse of the heart.

The four valves
Four one-way valves keep blood flowing forward. Two are between the atria and the ventricles — the atrioventricular (AV) valves. Two are between the ventricles and the great arteries — the semilunar valves.
Tricuspid valve (right AV)
Between the right atrium and right ventricle. Has three cusps (flaps), hence tricuspid. Each cusp is anchored downward by chordae tendineae that attach to papillary muscles in the ventricle wall. When the ventricle contracts, the rising pressure slams the cusps shut; the chordae tendineae prevent the cusps from being blown backward into the atrium.
Mitral valve (left AV)
Between the left atrium and left ventricle. Has two cusps — that is why it is also called the bicuspid valve. (It is called “mitral” because the two cusps together resemble a Catholic bishop’s mitre hat.) Otherwise it works identically to the tricuspid: chordae tendineae tether it down so that the high pressure of left-ventricular contraction does not blow it open backward.
Pulmonary semilunar valve
At the base of the pulmonary trunk, where it leaves the right ventricle. Three pocket-shaped cusps. When the right ventricle relaxes, blood that would otherwise fall back fills the pockets and forces them shut. No chordae tendineae are needed because the pockets are self-locking under back-pressure.
Aortic semilunar valve
At the base of the aorta, where it leaves the left ventricle. Three pocket-shaped cusps, identical mechanism to the pulmonary valve. The aortic valve is the most often diseased valve in adult medicine because of the high pressures it endures across decades.

A mnemonic for valves
“Try Before You Buy” works in many anatomy classes: Tricuspid (Right AV) → Pulmonary → Bicuspid/Mitral (Left AV) → Aortic. Or, in the order a drop of blood would encounter them traveling through the heart: tricuspid → pulmonary semilunar → mitral → aortic semilunar.
Why this matters — valve disease
A valve can fail in two ways. It can become stenotic (stiff and narrow, restricting forward flow) or insufficient/regurgitant (failing to close fully, allowing backflow). Aortic stenosis is the most common adult valve disease — the cusps stiffen and calcify with age, and the left ventricle has to work much harder to push blood through a narrowed opening. Mitral regurgitation is the second most common — a damaged mitral valve allows blood to leak back into the left atrium during ventricular contraction. Both produce characteristic heart murmurs, which is what physicians listen for with a stethoscope (and what René Laennec was first to amplify systematically — we meet him next lesson).
▶  Self-check: name all four valves in the order a drop of blood encounters them, and which use chordae tendineae
In order: tricuspid → pulmonary semilunar → mitral → aortic semilunar.

Only the two AV valves (tricuspid and mitral) have chordae tendineae — they need them because the rising ventricular pressure would otherwise blow the cusps backward into the atria. The semilunar valves are self-locking pocket valves; they need no chordae.

Bonus: which valve is most often diseased in older adults? Answer: the aortic valve — especially aortic stenosis from age-related calcification.
