The Heart from the Outside: Pericardium, Auricles, and the Great Vessels
Reading
📖 READING
A muscle the size of a fist
The heart is a hollow muscular organ about the size of an adult fist, weighing roughly 250–350 grams. It sits in the middle of the chest, behind the sternum, with about two thirds of its mass to the left of the midline. The pointed end — the apex — points down and to the left, and the broader top — the base — faces up and slightly to the right. (Note the counterintuitive naming: the “base” is on top.) From the base emerge the great vessels that connect the heart to the rest of the circulation.
A heart specimen with its base (at the level of the 2nd rib) and its apex — the distal, pointed end — labeled.
The heart sits inside the mediastinum, the central compartment of the thoracic cavity, between the two lungs and above the diaphragm. It is enclosed in a tough fibrous sac and pumps continuously without rest from before birth until the moment of death. In a 75-year lifetime, an average heart beats more than 2.7 billion times.
Figure 19.2 — Position of the Heart in the Thorax. The heart is located within the thoracic cavity, medially between the lungs in the mediastinum. It is about the size of a fist, is broad at the top (called the base), and tapers toward the apex.
A cadaver dissection showing the heart and great vessels in place within the opened thorax.
The pericardium — the heart’s protective sac
The pericardium is a triple-layered membrane that surrounds the heart. From outermost to innermost:
1. Fibrous pericardium. A tough, inelastic outer sac of dense connective tissue. It anchors the heart to the diaphragm below and the sternum in front, and it prevents the heart from over-expanding. If you have ever pulled a folded paper bag from inside a backpack, you know how dense fibrous pericardium feels.
2. Parietal layer of the serous pericardium. A thin, glistening membrane that lines the inside of the fibrous pericardium.
3. Visceral layer of the serous pericardium (epicardium). The same membrane, continued around the surface of the heart itself, becoming the outermost layer of the heart wall.
Between the parietal and visceral layers is the pericardial cavity, a slim space filled with about 15–50 mL of clear pericardial fluid. The fluid is the heart’s lubricant: it lets the two serous layers slide against each other with almost no friction every time the heart beats. If too much fluid accumulates — from infection, trauma, or cancer — it can compress the heart in a condition called cardiac tamponade, which is a medical emergency.
Figure 19.5 — Pericardial Membranes and Layers of the Heart Wall. The pericardial membrane that surrounds the heart consists of three layers and the pericardial cavity. The heart wall also consists of three layers. The pericardial membrane and the heart wall share the epicardium.
The four chambers — from the outside
Looking at the front of the heart, you see four chambers organized in two stacked pairs.
Atria (singular: atrium) are the two upper chambers. They receive blood returning from the body or lungs. From the outside, the most distinctive feature of each atrium is its auricle — an ear-flap-like extension that wrinkles outward over the front of the heart. Auricle literally means “little ear.” The right auricle sits over the right atrium; the left auricle sits over the left atrium.
Ventricles are the two lower chambers. They pump blood out of the heart. The right ventricle takes up the front of the heart and pumps blood to the lungs; the left ventricle wraps around the left side and back and pumps blood to the entire body. The left ventricle’s wall is dramatically thicker than the right’s, because it has to generate roughly five times the pressure. You will see this from the inside next lesson.
Surface landmarks — sulci
On the heart’s surface are two grooves that mark the boundaries between chambers underneath. The coronary sulcus (also called the atrioventricular groove) runs roughly horizontally around the heart and marks where atria meet ventricles. The anterior interventricular sulcus runs down the front from base to apex and marks the boundary between the two ventricles. A similar groove runs along the back. Both sulci carry the coronary blood vessels — we will trace these in Lesson 7.08.
The great vessels — where blood enters and leaves
Before naming the six great vessels, three rules make the whole list predictable instead of a memorization exercise. First, arteries always carry blood away from the heart, and veins always carry blood toward the heart — that is what the words mean, regardless of which gas the blood is carrying. Second, the atria only ever receive blood, so they are always connected to veins; the ventricles are the muscular pumps that push blood out, so they are always connected to arteries. Third, each of the four chambers connects to exactly one type of vessel: the right atrium to the venae cavae, the right ventricle to the pulmonary arteries, the left atrium to the pulmonary veins, and the left ventricle to the aorta. Hold onto that chamber-to-vessel pairing — it is the key that unlocks the rest of this section.
Six large vessels enter or leave the base of the heart. Knowing them on the outside, in order, is the spine of cardiac anatomy.
Vessels entering the right atrium — deoxygenated blood returning from the body
Superior vena cava (SVC). Drains the head, neck, upper limbs, and upper chest. Enters from above.
Inferior vena cava (IVC). Drains everything below the diaphragm — abdomen, pelvis, lower limbs. Enters from below.
Both empty into the right atrium, where the body’s used blood waits to be pumped to the lungs.
Vessel leaving the right ventricle — toward the lungs
Pulmonary trunk. Exits the right ventricle, then splits almost immediately into the left and right pulmonary arteries, which carry deoxygenated blood to each lung. The pulmonary trunk is the only major artery in the body that carries deoxygenated blood — an exception to remember.
Superficial Heart Anatomy (Anterior). Note that only the left pulmonary veins are labeled here — the right pulmonary veins enter the back of the left atrium and are out of view from this angle.
Vessels entering the left atrium — oxygenated blood returning from the lungs
Four pulmonary veins — two from each lung — empty into the back of the left atrium. The pulmonary veins are the only major veins in the body that carry oxygenated blood — the other exception.
Vessel leaving the left ventricle — toward the body
Aorta. The largest artery in the body, originating at the top of the left ventricle. The first portion (ascending aorta) goes up; it arches over (aortic arch); then it descends behind the heart (descending aorta) all the way to the pelvis. We trace its branches in Week 3.
Figure 19.6 — External Anatomy of the Heart. Inside the pericardium, the surface features of the heart — and all six great vessels — are visible from both the anterior and posterior views.
The two circuits
The six great vessels together define two circulatory loops powered by a single heart.
The pulmonary circuit carries deoxygenated blood from the right side of the heart to the lungs and back: right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary trunk → pulmonary arteries → lung capillaries (pick up O₂, drop off CO₂) → pulmonary veins → left atrium.
The systemic circuit carries oxygenated blood from the left side of the heart to the rest of the body and back: left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → systemic arteries → capillary beds throughout the body → systemic veins → venae cavae → right atrium.
The two circuits are in series — every drop of blood that completes one is then handed off to the other. The right heart and the left heart beat simultaneously, but they pump into different circuits at very different pressures. We will see why next lesson.
Figure 19.4 — Dual System of the Human Blood Circulation. Blood flows from the right atrium to the right ventricle, where it is pumped into the pulmonary circuit. Following exchange in the lungs, blood enters the left ventricle, which pumps it into the systemic circuit. Following exchange in the systemic capillaries, blood returns to the right atrium and the cycle is repeated.
▶  Self-check: name the two vessels that are exceptions to the “arteries carry oxygenated blood, veins carry deoxygenated blood” rule
The two exceptions are the pulmonary trunk / pulmonary arteries (the only major arteries carrying deoxygenated blood — from right ventricle to lungs) and the pulmonary veins (the only major veins carrying oxygenated blood — from lungs to left atrium).

The rule that artery means “carries blood away from the heart” (regardless of oxygenation) and vein means “carries blood toward the heart” (regardless of oxygenation) is the only rule with no exceptions.
