Blood Flow, Pressure, and Resistance: The Physics of Circulation
Reading
📖 READING
A fluid in a tube
Blood is a fluid, and the rules that govern fluids in tubes — pressure, resistance, viscosity, diameter — govern blood flow as well. The cardiovascular system is built from these few simple physical principles, and so are most of its diseases. This reading is the physics half of the cardiovascular module.
The fundamental equation
For any fluid flowing through any tube, the volume flowing per second depends on two things: the pressure pushing the fluid forward and the resistance opposing it. The equation that links them is the cleanest piece of physics in physiology:
Q = ΔP / R
Where Q is flow rate (volume per unit time), ΔP is the pressure gradient (the difference between two points in the tube), and R is resistance. For the whole circulation, Q is cardiac output, ΔP is the difference between aortic pressure and right-atrial pressure, and R is total peripheral resistance. Every other cardiovascular fact in this lesson is a corollary of this one equation.
What determines resistance?
Resistance to flow has three main contributors. Two of them barely change. The third — vessel diameter — is the body’s main control knob.
Blood viscosity. Blood is about five times more viscous than water. Viscosity is set mainly by hematocrit, and outside of disease (severe anemia or polycythemia) it does not change much from day to day.
Total blood vessel length. Tends not to change in adults except slowly (e.g., growing new vessels through obesity adds vessel length).
Vessel radius. The dominant factor by far. Resistance is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the radius. This is the most important consequence in all of cardiovascular physiology:
R ∝ 1 / r⁴
Reduce a vessel’s radius by half, and resistance rises by a factor of 16. Reduce by a third, and resistance rises by a factor of about 5. This is why a small amount of arterial constriction can produce large changes in blood pressure, and why even modest atherosclerotic narrowing matters clinically.
How the three vessel types handle pressure differently
A drop of blood leaves the left ventricle at about 120 mmHg and arrives at the right atrium at near 0 mmHg. That entire pressure drop happens across the systemic circuit, but it does not happen evenly. Different vessels handle different portions of the pressure profile.
Figure 20.16 — Capillary Exchange. Net filtration occurs near the arterial end of the capillary since capillary hydrostatic pressure (CHP) is greater than blood colloidal osmotic pressure (BCOP). There is no net movement of fluid near the midpoint since CHP = BCOP. Net reabsorption occurs near the venous end since BCOP is greater than CHP.
Large arteries (aorta and major branches). Walls are stiff with elastin. They stretch during systole, recoil during diastole. The stretch-and-recoil smooths the pressure wave into a steady-but-still-pulsing flow. Pressure drops only modestly across them.
Arterioles. The body’s main resistance vessels. Their thick smooth-muscle walls dilate and constrict in response to nerve signals, hormones, and local metabolites. Most of the pressure drop in the systemic circulation happens here. Tighten the arterioles and you raise blood pressure throughout the body; relax them and you lower it.
Capillaries. The actual exchange surface. Walls are one cell thick. Pressure has dropped to about 35 mmHg at the arterial end and 15 mmHg at the venous end. This pressure window is what drives water and small molecules out at the start of the capillary bed and pulls them back in at the end — the cycle that delivers oxygen and removes waste at every tissue.
At the arterial side of a capillary bed, blood pressure exceeds blood osmotic pressure, so plasma consistently leaks out through the capillary walls, forming the interstitial fluid that bathes tissues. Plasma proteins and blood cells are too large to leak out and stay in the capillary.
Venules and veins. Pressure is very low (under 10 mmHg in most veins). Wall structure handles capacitance, not pressure.
Cardiac output
Cardiac output (CO) is the volume of blood pumped per minute by one ventricle. At rest, both ventricles pump about 5 L/min — nearly your entire blood volume every minute. Cardiac output is the product of two things:
CO = HR × SV
Where HR is heart rate (beats per minute) and SV is stroke volume (volume ejected per beat — about 70 mL at rest). During heavy exercise, both factors rise: heart rate can triple (to 180+ bpm) and stroke volume can roughly double (to 150 mL+), so cardiac output can climb fivefold or more — from 5 L/min at rest to 25 L/min or higher in elite athletes during peak effort.
Congestive heart failure: a failing left ventricle pumps a reduced volume of oxygen-rich blood to the body, producing an enlarged heart, fluid buildup around the lungs, and swelling in the legs and feet.
Blood pressure regulation
Mean arterial pressure (MAP) — the average pressure across the cardiac cycle — can be approximated as:
MAP = CO × TPR
Where TPR is total peripheral resistance — the combined resistance of all the systemic vessels, dominated by the arterioles. To raise blood pressure, the body can raise heart rate, raise stroke volume, or constrict arterioles. To lower it, the opposite. Three regulatory systems work together: baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch detect pressure and trigger fast nerve responses; the kidneys regulate blood volume more slowly (Module 11); and hormones like ADH, aldosterone, and the renin-angiotensin system fine-tune the long-term setpoint (Module 6 and Module 11).
Hypertension — the silent killer
Hypertension is chronically elevated arterial pressure — usually defined as repeated readings above 130/80 mmHg in clinical practice. Most cases (about 90%) have no single identifiable cause; they emerge from a combination of genetic, dietary, and lifestyle factors. The other 10% are secondary hypertension caused by an identifiable problem (renal artery stenosis, an adrenal tumor secreting cortisol or aldosterone, certain medications).
Hypertension is dangerous because it is silent — patients feel fine for years — and because the damage accumulates everywhere blood is flowing. Three of the most important consequences:
Heart. The left ventricle thickens to push against the elevated pressure (left ventricular hypertrophy). Over time it stiffens and stops filling properly — heart failure.
Brain. Sustained high pressure damages cerebral arterioles, increasing the risk of stroke (both ischemic, from atherosclerotic narrowing, and hemorrhagic, from vessel rupture).
Kidneys. Glomerular capillaries are exquisitely sensitive to pressure. Chronic hypertension damages them, leading to chronic kidney disease (Module 11).
Atherosclerosis progresses from normal endothelial function through dysfunction, plaque formation, and thrombosis — the vessel damage that chronic hypertension accelerates throughout the body.
🔬 CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH — WEARABLE CONTINUOUS BP MONITORING
For most of medical history, blood pressure was a snapshot — one or two readings at a doctor’s office, sometimes with a 24-hour ambulatory monitor for serious cases. Because pressure varies minute by minute and rises during stress, those snapshots can miss the average behavior of a person’s pressure across the day.

Several research teams are working on cuffless continuous BP monitoring using sensors that fit on the wrist, finger, or ear. The leading approaches use pulse-wave analysis: a small optical or pressure sensor measures the pulse’s waveform and time-of-arrival, and a calibrated algorithm infers blood pressure from those features. Some smartwatches already provide approximate readings; medical-grade devices have begun to receive regulatory clearance in several countries.

Whether these devices will be accurate enough to replace cuff measurements is still being worked out. Calibration drift is a major challenge — the algorithms typically require periodic recalibration with a real cuff measurement. But if the technology matures, hypertension could be identified earlier and treatment titrated more precisely against a continuous record of how the patient’s pressure actually behaves across a day, a workout, or a poor night’s sleep.
▶  Self-check: state the two flow equations and explain why a small change in vessel radius matters so much
Q = ΔP / R — flow rate equals pressure gradient divided by resistance.
R ∝ 1 / r⁴ — resistance is inversely proportional to the fourth power of vessel radius.

Because radius is raised to the fourth power, halving the radius produces a 16-fold increase in resistance. The body uses this leverage at the arterioles: small smooth-muscle adjustments in arteriole diameter cause huge changes in systemic resistance and therefore in blood pressure. It is also why narrowing of large arteries by atherosclerosis is more harmful than its modest-looking diameter change would suggest.
