Fetal Circulation: A Different Circuit and What Changes at Birth
Reading
📖 READING
A circulation built for two
For nine months before you breathed your first breath, your cardiovascular system worked very differently from the way it works now. The fetus has no functioning lungs — the lungs are filled with amniotic fluid and the pulmonary vessels are tightly constricted. The fetus has no functioning digestive system — it does not eat. The fetus does not get oxygen, nutrients, or rid of waste in the way an adult does. All of those exchanges happen at the placenta: an organ that grows together with the fetus from both fetal and maternal tissue, and dissolves at birth.
This means the fetal circulation has to do something the adult’s does not: it has to bring oxygenated blood in from outside the body (from the placenta, through the umbilical cord) and send deoxygenated blood out the same way. It also has to bypass the lungs, since blood that goes through them gets nothing.
The placenta and the umbilical cord
The placenta is the organ where fetal blood meets maternal blood — not by mixing, but by lying close enough that oxygen, glucose, and waste products can diffuse across a thin barrier between them. The umbilical cord connects the placenta to the fetus and contains three vessels:
One umbilical vein — carries oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood from the placenta to the fetus.
Two umbilical arteries — carry deoxygenated, waste-laden blood from the fetus back to the placenta.
This is one of two places in the body where the artery/vein naming follows the standard rule (artery away from heart, vein toward heart), even though the oxygenation is “backwards.” The umbilical vein carries oxygenated blood; the umbilical arteries carry deoxygenated blood. (The other exception is the pulmonary circulation, which you met in Week 2.)
Three fetal shunts
The fetus has three special pathways — shunts — that exist only before birth. Each routes blood around an organ that is not yet doing its adult job.
Figure 20.44 — Fetal Shunts. The foramen ovale in the interatrial septum allows blood to flow from the right atrium to the left atrium. The ductus arteriosus is a temporary vessel, connecting the aorta to the pulmonary trunk. The ductus venosus links the umbilical vein to the inferior vena cava largely through the liver.
1. Ductus venosus — bypass the liver
When the oxygenated umbilical vein reaches the fetus, most of its blood needs to get to the heart quickly — not detour through the maturing liver. The ductus venosus is a small vessel that shunts about 80% of umbilical-vein blood directly into the inferior vena cava, bypassing most of the liver. The liver still gets some blood for development, but the main supply goes straight to the heart.
2. Foramen ovale — bypass the lungs (option 1)
When oxygenated blood arrives at the right atrium via the IVC, it should not flow through the right ventricle and then to the lungs (which are not working). Instead, most of it crosses to the left side immediately. The foramen ovale is a flap-valve opening in the interatrial septum that allows blood to pass directly from the right atrium into the left atrium. From there it follows the normal path: left ventricle → aorta → body. This is the first of two lung-bypass shunts.
3. Ductus arteriosus — bypass the lungs (option 2)
Some blood does pass into the right ventricle and out the pulmonary trunk. Instead of going to the lungs, however, it takes a second shortcut: the ductus arteriosus, a short vessel connecting the pulmonary trunk directly to the aortic arch. Blood ejected from the right ventricle therefore mostly bypasses the lungs by flowing from pulmonary trunk → ductus arteriosus → aorta → body.
Together, the foramen ovale and the ductus arteriosus deliver almost all of the cardiac output away from the lungs and into the systemic circulation. The fetal pulmonary vessels stay constricted and the lungs receive only a small fraction of cardiac output for their own growth and development.
What happens at the first breath
When the baby takes its first breath after birth, the cardiovascular system reorganizes in seconds. Three changes happen almost simultaneously:
1. Pulmonary resistance drops. The lungs fill with air. Oxygen in the alveoli dilates pulmonary blood vessels. Blood that used to bypass the lungs now flows through them, and a huge volume of oxygenated blood begins returning to the left atrium.
2. Left-atrial pressure rises above right-atrial pressure. The increased pulmonary venous return raises pressure in the left atrium. This pressure shift forces the flap of the foramen ovale closed against the interatrial septum. Over weeks the flap fuses, leaving a small depression called the fossa ovalis — an adult landmark you can still see in any anatomical drawing of the right atrium.
3. The ductus arteriosus constricts. Rising arterial oxygen and a drop in prostaglandin levels cause the smooth muscle in the wall of the ductus arteriosus to contract. Within hours the duct is functionally closed; within weeks it fibroses into the ligamentum arteriosum — a small fibrous cord still visible between the pulmonary trunk and the aortic arch.
4. The umbilical vessels close. Once the umbilical cord is cut, the umbilical vein and arteries close off and eventually become fibrous ligaments. The ductus venosus closes shortly after for the same reason.
Congenital persistence — when shunts don’t close
Sometimes the fetal shunts do not close after birth. Each failure mode is its own congenital heart defect:
Patent foramen ovale (PFO) — the most common, present in about 25% of adults to some degree. Usually benign, but in some patients allows small clots to bypass the lungs (which normally filter them) and reach the brain, causing certain unexplained strokes.
Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) — when the ductus arteriosus stays open. Allows abnormal flow between the aorta and pulmonary trunk, with consequences depending on size. Sometimes closes on its own; sometimes treated with medication (indomethacin, which blocks prostaglandin synthesis) or surgical/catheter closure.
▶  Self-check: name the three fetal shunts and what each bypasses
Ductus venosus — bypasses the liver. Routes umbilical-vein blood directly to the IVC.
Foramen ovale — bypasses the lungs (option 1). Routes blood from right atrium to left atrium.
Ductus arteriosus — bypasses the lungs (option 2). Routes blood from pulmonary trunk to aorta.

Bonus: what is the umbilical vein doing that is “backwards”? It is a vein carrying oxygenated blood — like the pulmonary veins. The two are exceptions to the “arteries oxygenated, veins deoxygenated” rule.
