The Major Arteries: From the Aorta to Every Limb and Organ
Reading
📖 READING
A tree with one trunk
Every artery in the systemic circulation traces back to a single vessel: the aorta. Branching from it, again and again, smaller arteries fan out into every tissue of the body. The whole system has the geometry of a tree — one trunk, large branches, smaller branches, and finally microscopic capillaries at the leaves. This lesson is the named portion of that tree, the part anatomists and physicians keep at their fingertips.
The three layers of an arterial wall
Every artery is built from three concentric layers:
Tunica intima — the smooth inner endothelium that contacts the blood. Identical material to the lining of every vessel; minimizes turbulence.
Tunica media — the thick middle layer of smooth muscle and elastic fibers. This is where arteries differ most from veins. Large arteries (like the aorta) have abundant elastin, which lets them stretch on each ventricular contraction and recoil between beats, smoothing the pressure pulse. Medium-sized arteries have more smooth muscle, allowing them to constrict or dilate — the basis of blood-pressure regulation we will meet in Lesson 7.11.
Tunica externa (adventitia) — the outer layer of connective tissue that anchors the vessel to surrounding structures.
The Structure of an Artery Wall — tunica externa, tunica media, and tunica intima, with the smooth muscle and elastic membranes that let large arteries stretch and recoil.
An alternate labeled cross-section of an artery, including the vasa vasorum and nervi vasorum — the vessel's own tiny blood supply and nerve supply.
The aorta and its three regions
The aorta is divided into three sequential regions that you should learn as a sequence:
1. Ascending aorta. Rises from the left ventricle. Its only branches are the left and right coronary arteries (from Lesson 7.08).
2. Aortic arch. Curves backward and to the left, then becomes the descending aorta. The arch has three named branches in a specific order (left to right when looking at a textbook drawing — but right to left when looking at a real person from the front): the brachiocephalic trunk, the left common carotid artery, and the left subclavian artery.
3. Descending aorta. Passes down through the chest (thoracic aorta) and then through the diaphragm into the abdomen (abdominal aorta), branching to supply chest, abdominal, and pelvic organs before splitting into the two common iliac arteries at the level of the pelvis.
The aorta's three regions — ascending aorta, aortic arch, and descending (thoracic then abdominal) aorta — with the arch's three named branches and the coronary arteries at its base.
Arteries of the head and neck
Each side of the head and neck is supplied by a common carotid artery. On the left, it branches directly off the aortic arch. On the right, it branches off the brachiocephalic trunk. Each common carotid divides at about the level of the larynx into:
External carotid artery — supplies the face, scalp, and structures outside the skull.
Internal carotid artery — ascends through the skull and supplies most of the brain.
The brain also receives blood from the two vertebral arteries, which branch off the subclavian arteries on each side, ascend through small holes in the cervical vertebrae, and join inside the skull to form the basilar artery. The vertebral and internal carotid systems meet in a ring at the base of the brain called the Circle of Willis — a redundant connection that allows blood to flow through alternative paths if one vessel is blocked. This is one of the body’s most elegant pieces of engineering.

Arteries of the upper limb
Each upper limb is supplied by a single sequence of arteries that change names as they descend:
Subclavian → Axillary → Brachial → Radial + Ulnar. The name changes whenever the artery passes a major anatomical boundary — the first rib, the lower border of the teres major, the elbow.
The brachial artery is the artery you press on when taking a blood pressure with a cuff and stethoscope. The radial artery is the artery you feel at the wrist when taking a pulse. The radial and ulnar arteries form anastomotic arches in the palm, supplying the digital arteries that go to each finger.
Figure 20.31 — Major Arteries Serving the Thorax and Upper Limb. The arteries that supply blood to the arms and hands are extensions of the subclavian arteries.
Abdominal aorta and its main branches
In the abdomen, the descending aorta sends out three large unpaired branches to the gut and a series of paired branches to the kidneys, adrenals, and gonads:
Celiac trunk — the first major abdominal branch. Splits into three: the left gastric (to the stomach), the splenic (to the spleen and pancreas), and the common hepatic (to the liver).
Superior mesenteric artery (SMA) — supplies the small intestine and the first half of the large intestine.
Renal arteries — one to each kidney. They are paired (one left, one right). The kidneys collectively receive about 20% of the cardiac output at rest, which is why the renal arteries are so large despite the kidneys’ modest size.
Inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) — supplies the second half of the large intestine.
At the level of the pelvis, the abdominal aorta bifurcates into the left and right common iliac arteries, which divide into internal iliac (pelvic organs) and external iliac (continuing into the lower limb).
Figure 20.28 — Arteries of the Thoracic and Abdominal Regions. The thoracic aorta gives rise to the arteries of the visceral and parietal branches.
Arteries of the lower limb
Just as in the upper limb, the lower limb has a single artery that changes name as it descends:
External iliac → Femoral → Popliteal → Anterior tibial + Posterior tibial + Fibular (peroneal). The femoral artery passes anteriorly through the thigh; it becomes the popliteal artery behind the knee. Below the knee, the popliteal splits into three arteries that supply the leg and the foot.
The femoral artery is large, superficial, and accessible — it is the standard access point for cardiac catheterization (a catheter is threaded retrograde all the way up to the heart).

▶  Self-check: name the arterial path from the aortic arch to the brain, and from the aortic arch to your fingertip
Aortic arch → brain: aortic arch → common carotid (right via brachiocephalic, left directly off arch) → internal carotid → brain. Plus the vertebral arteries via the subclavians, joining the internal carotids at the Circle of Willis.

Aortic arch → fingertip: aortic arch → subclavian (right via brachiocephalic, left directly off arch) → axillary → brachial → radial or ulnar → palmar arch → digital artery → fingertip.

Bonus: name the three abdominal aorta branches that supply the gut. Answer: celiac trunk, superior mesenteric, inferior mesenteric.
