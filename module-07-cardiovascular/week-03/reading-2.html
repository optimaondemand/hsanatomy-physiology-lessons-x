The Major Veins and the Problem of Venous Return
Reading
📖 READING
Returning what the arteries delivered
Every artery delivers blood to a capillary bed. The capillary bed is where the actual work of the cardiovascular system happens — oxygen unloads, nutrients diffuse, waste loads up — and on the other side of the capillary the flow gathers into the smallest venules, which join into ever-larger veins as they march back toward the heart. The venous system is the return half of the circuit.
Two facts make veins a different engineering problem from arteries. First, the blood in veins is no longer under the high pressure the heart generated — most of that pressure has been dissipated by the time blood crosses the capillary bed. Second, the body must move blood up from the feet, against gravity, all the way to the heart. The vein wall and its surrounding structures have evolved an elegant set of solutions.
Figure 20.6 — Capillary Bed. In a capillary bed, arterioles give rise to metarterioles. Precapillary sphincters located at the junction of a metarteriole with a capillary regulate blood flow. A thoroughfare channel connects the metarteriole to a venule. An arteriovenous anastomosis, which directly connects the arteriole with the venule, is shown at the bottom.
How a vein differs from an artery
A vein has the same three wall layers as an artery (intima, media, externa), but in different proportions:
Thinner walls overall. Veins do not need to withstand high pressure, so the smooth-muscle media is much thinner.
A medium-sized vein's three wall layers, with a pair of one-way valves shown closed.
Wider lumens. The inside diameter of a vein is typically two to three times larger than the corresponding artery. Veins hold most of the body’s blood volume at any moment (about 60–70% of total blood volume is in the venous system at rest).
One-way valves. Medium-sized veins, especially in the limbs, have one-way venous valves — flap-like extensions of the intima that allow blood to flow only toward the heart. The valves close behind every contraction of the surrounding muscle, preventing backflow.

How blood returns against gravity
If you simply stood still, blood would pool in your feet within minutes and you would faint. The body uses three coordinated mechanisms to push venous blood back toward the heart:
1. The skeletal-muscle pump. When the muscles of your legs contract, they squeeze the deep veins running through them, pushing blood upward. The one-way valves prevent it from sliding back. Walking is literally pumping; soldiers passing out at attention are familiar examples of what happens when the pump stops.
Figure 20.15 — Skeletal Muscle Pump. The contraction of skeletal muscles surrounding a vein compresses the blood and increases the pressure in that area. This action forces blood closer to the heart where venous pressure is lower. Note the importance of the one-way valve.
2. The respiratory pump. Each inhalation lowers pressure in the thoracic cavity and pulls blood upward from abdominal veins into the chest.
3. Vasoconstriction in response to sympathetic activity. When the body needs more venous return (during exercise, blood loss, or standing up), sympathetic nerves narrow the venous walls, “squeezing the reservoir” back into the circulation.
The named veins of the upper body
All venous return from above the diaphragm eventually drains into the superior vena cava (SVC), which empties into the right atrium. Walking backwards along the return path:
Head and neck — internal jugular vein (drains the brain via the dural sinuses) and external jugular vein (drains the face and scalp). Both empty into the subclavian or brachiocephalic veins.
Upper limb — two parallel systems. The deep veins follow the arteries with the same names (radial vein, ulnar vein, brachial vein, axillary vein, subclavian vein). The superficial veins — the cephalic vein on the lateral side and the basilic vein on the medial side — run just under the skin and are the veins phlebotomists draw blood from when they put a needle in your antecubital fossa (the bend of the elbow). The median cubital vein connects the cephalic and basilic veins across the elbow.
Subclavian + internal jugular → brachiocephalic vein (one per side) → the two brachiocephalics join to form the SVC → right atrium.
Figure 20.38 — Veins of the Upper Limb. This anterior view shows the veins that drain the upper limb.
The named veins of the lower body
All venous return from below the diaphragm eventually drains into the inferior vena cava (IVC), also into the right atrium. The path from foot to right atrium:
Foot and leg — deep veins parallel the arteries (anterior tibial, posterior tibial, fibular). Two superficial veins matter clinically: the great saphenous vein (the longest vein in the body, running up the medial side of the leg from foot to groin — the vein most often harvested for coronary bypass grafts) and the small saphenous vein (the back of the calf to the popliteal vein).
Thigh and pelvis — popliteal vein → femoral vein → external iliac vein. The external and internal iliac veins join to form the common iliac vein, and the two common iliacs join to form the IVC at the level of the pelvis.

A special detour — the hepatic portal system
Most veins go directly to the heart. The veins from the gut do not. Instead, blood drained from the small intestine, large intestine, stomach, pancreas, and spleen first travels through the hepatic portal vein to the liver. There it passes through a second capillary bed (the liver sinusoids), where the liver inspects, modifies, stores, or detoxifies whatever the gut has just absorbed. Only then does the blood enter the hepatic veins and finally drain into the IVC.
This system — two capillary beds in series, with a portal vein between them — is called a portal system. It is essential to digestive physiology and we will return to it in Module 10 when we study the digestive system in detail. For now: know that every nutrient absorbed from your gut passes through the liver before it reaches anywhere else in your body, and the hepatic portal vein is what makes that detour possible.
Figure 20.43 — Hepatic Portal System. The liver receives blood from the normal systemic circulation via the hepatic artery. It also receives and processes blood from other organs, delivered via the veins of the hepatic portal system. All blood exits the liver via the hepatic vein, which delivers the blood to the inferior vena cava.
