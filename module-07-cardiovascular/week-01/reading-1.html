What Is Blood? Plasma and the Formed Elements

📖 READING
A river that visits every cell
You are made of roughly 30 to 40 trillion cells, and almost none of them live next to the surface where oxygen and food enter the body, or near the lungs and kidneys where waste leaves it. The body solves this problem with a river: about five liters of blood carried in a closed loop of vessels, driven by a single pump. Every cell in the body lives within a fraction of a millimeter of a capillary — the smallest blood vessel — so that the river can drop off what the cell needs and pick up what it makes.
Module 7 is the body’s transport system: the blood that does the carrying, the heart that does the pumping, and the vessels that route every drop where it needs to go. We begin where the system itself begins — with the fluid.
Blood is a connective tissue
When you first hear that blood is a tissue, it sounds wrong — blood pours. Tissues are supposed to hold their shape. But the formal definition of a tissue is “cells of a similar type and function arranged in an extracellular matrix.” In bone, the matrix is hard and crystalline. In tendon, the matrix is dense and fibrous. In blood, the matrix is liquid. The classification has nothing to do with how stiff the tissue feels — it has to do with the relationship between the cells and the substance they sit in.
An adult body holds about 4–6 liters of blood, scaling with body size and sex. That is roughly 7–8% of total body weight. About 55% of that volume is liquid matrix — plasma — and about 45% is the cells and cell fragments suspended in it — the formed elements. The percentage of blood that is red cells is called the hematocrit, and it is one of the first numbers a physician orders on any blood test.
What blood does
Blood has three large jobs — transport, regulation, and protection — each of which decomposes into several specific tasks.
Transport. Blood carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue and carbon dioxide back. It carries glucose, amino acids, fats, and ions absorbed from the small intestine to the cells that use them. It carries hormones from the endocrine glands you sculpted last module to their distant targets. And it carries metabolic waste — urea, creatinine, ammonia — to the kidneys and liver for disposal.
Regulation. Blood distributes heat. When your core is too warm, vessels in the skin dilate and the bloodstream becomes a radiator. Blood also buffers pH, holding the body within a narrow window (about 7.35 to 7.45). And blood maintains osmotic balance with the surrounding tissues: the proteins dissolved in plasma create a pressure that pulls water back into the vessels at every capillary.
Protection. Blood defends the body. White blood cells patrol the tissues, swallow invaders, and produce antibodies. Platelets and clotting proteins seal a damaged vessel within minutes — without them, even a paper cut would not stop bleeding. We will spend the rest of this week on both of those functions.
💡 WILLIAM HARVEY AND THE CIRCULATION OF BLOOD
For more than fourteen centuries, every European physician was taught that blood was made fresh in the liver, traveled outward through the veins, and was consumed by the tissues like firewood. This view came from the second-century Greek physician Galen, and it was unquestioned. The heart, in Galen’s scheme, was a heating organ. The pulse you feel in your wrist was the artery wall’s own contraction, not a wave from the heart.

William Harvey (1578–1657), an English physician trained in Padua, suspected that the math did not work. He measured the volume the heart could hold and the rate at which it beat, and computed that the heart had to pump roughly 250 liters of blood per hour. The liver could not possibly make that much. The only explanation was that the same blood went around in a loop — out the arteries, through the tissues, back the veins. He showed it experimentally by tying ligatures on arms to interrupt either the arterial or the venous flow, by valving veins to demonstrate one-way return, and by tracing the heart in living animals.

Harvey published De Motu Cordis (“On the Motion of the Heart”) in 1628. He was ridiculed for years. He could not see the capillaries that closed the loop — the microscope had not yet been turned to anatomy — and his contemporaries demanded the connecting vessels. Marcello Malpighi finally saw them, in a frog’s lung, in 1661 — four years after Harvey’s death. By the end of the seventeenth century, every European medical school had thrown out Galen on this question. Harvey’s correction is the foundation of every cardiovascular fact you will learn in this module.
Plasma — the liquid matrix
Plasma is a pale yellow fluid. About 92% of it is water. The remaining 8% is a remarkable cargo: dissolved gases, ions, nutrients, hormones, wastes, and three families of plasma proteins.
The plasma proteins
Albumin is the most abundant plasma protein, accounting for about 58% of total plasma protein. It is made in the liver. Its main job is to hold water inside the bloodstream by osmotic pull, called colloid osmotic pressure. Without enough albumin, water leaks out of the capillaries and pools in the tissues — the swelling we call edema.
Globulins are a family of about a third of the plasma proteins. Some are transport proteins (carrying iron, copper, lipids, or hormones), and some are antibodies — the immunoglobulins — produced by white blood cells. We will return to immunoglobulins in Module 8.
Fibrinogen is the smallest plasma-protein fraction by mass (about 4%), but it is essential. Fibrinogen circulates dissolved in plasma until vessel damage occurs, and then it is enzymatically converted to fibrin — the protein threads that build a blood clot. You will trace this conversion in Lesson 7.04.
The formed elements — an introduction
The cells and cell fragments suspended in plasma are called the formed elements. There are three families, and the proportions are striking.
Erythrocytes — red blood cells — account for more than 99% of the formed-element count. A single drop of blood holds about 5 million of them per microliter (the volume of a pinhead). They carry oxygen, and they are the next lesson in this module.
Leukocytes — white blood cells — are less than 1% of the formed-element count but are the entire reason your immune system can function. There are five named types: neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils. We meet them in Lesson 7.03.
Platelets are not whole cells. They are fragments shed from much larger bone-marrow cells called megakaryocytes. About 150,000 to 400,000 of them sit in every microliter of blood. When a vessel is damaged, platelets are the first responders — we will see how in Lesson 7.04.

Where blood comes from
Blood cells live short lives. A red blood cell lasts about 120 days; most white cells live days to weeks; platelets last roughly ten days. To keep the river stocked, the body makes new blood cells continuously — about two million per second. The process is called hematopoiesis, and it happens almost entirely in the red bone marrow of certain bones: the sternum, the ribs, the vertebrae, the pelvis, the proximal femur, and the proximal humerus. You met red bone marrow back in the skeletal module; this is what it does. Every formed element in your bloodstream began as a stem cell in one of those bones, divided through several intermediate types, and was released into the circulation when it was finally ready to work.

A few measurable properties
Blood is denser than water and noticeably more viscous — about five times as thick. It feels heavier in the test tube than its volume suggests. Its color depends on its oxygen state: bright red when oxygenated (in the arteries that leave the left heart), darker red when deoxygenated (in the veins returning to the right heart). The blue-purple color of veins on the back of your hand is an optical effect of the skin filtering certain wavelengths; the blood inside is still red, just a darker shade.
Figure — The difference between oxygenated blood (left) and deoxygenated blood (right) is surprisingly minor — no blue blood here. (WikiMedia Commons.)
Blood pH sits between 7.35 and 7.45 — slightly alkaline. A small shift outside this window is medically serious. We will see why when we discuss the bicarbonate buffer in Module 9 (Respiratory).
▶  Self-check: what is the rough proportion of plasma to formed elements, and what fraction of formed elements are red cells?
By volume, blood is about 55% plasma and 45% formed elements. Of the formed elements, more than 99% are red blood cells — the white cells and platelets are a tiny minority by count, even though they are essential by function.
