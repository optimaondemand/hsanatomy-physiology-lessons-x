Red Blood Cells: Hemoglobin and Oxygen Transport
Reading
📖 READING
A cell that gave up everything to carry oxygen
An erythrocyte — a red blood cell — is one of the strangest cells in your body. It has no nucleus. It has no mitochondria. It has no ribosomes, no endoplasmic reticulum, no Golgi apparatus. It cannot divide. It cannot make new proteins. It has no way to repair itself. In the strictest sense, it is barely a cell.
What it has is hemoglobin — about 250 million molecules of it in every cell — and the geometry to put that hemoglobin within a fraction of a micrometer of the cell membrane, so that oxygen can rush in or out at every spot on the surface. Every “missing” organelle is the price of that geometry. The red blood cell is form following function in its purest expression in the human body.
The numbers
In each microliter of blood there are roughly 4.7 to 6.1 million red cells in adult males and 4.2 to 5.4 million in adult females. A single drop of blood holds about 250 million. Your whole bloodstream holds 20 to 30 trillion red cells — comparable in number to every other cell in your body combined.
Each red cell lives about 120 days. Without a nucleus or repair machinery, the cell’s membrane proteins slowly wear out, and once they do, the spleen and the liver recognize the worn-out cell and destroy it. The bone marrow replaces lost cells at roughly two million per second — the most prolific tissue in the body.
The biconcave disc
Each red blood cell is shaped like a flattened doughnut without the hole — a biconcave disc. It is about 7–8 micrometers across and only 2 micrometers thick, dipping to about 1 micrometer in the middle. That shape is no accident:
Figure 18.6 — Shape of Red Blood Cells. Erythrocytes are biconcave discs with very shallow centers. This shape optimizes the ratio of surface area to volume, facilitating gas exchange, and also enables them to fold up as they move through narrow blood vessels.
1. Maximum surface area for diffusion. A flattened disc has more surface area per unit volume than a sphere of the same size. More surface means more places for O₂ and CO₂ to cross the membrane.
2. Short diffusion path. No hemoglobin molecule sits more than about a micrometer from the cell’s outer surface. Oxygen does not have to travel far to reach a binding site or escape from one.
3. Flexibility through narrow capillaries. The narrowest capillaries are about 5 micrometers across — smaller than the red cell’s diameter. A red cell deforms into a parachute or a torpedo to squeeze through, then springs back. A rigid disc would jam.

Hemoglobin — the cargo molecule
Hemoglobin is one of the most studied proteins in biology. Each molecule is built from four protein subunits — two alpha chains and two beta chains in adult hemoglobin (HbA). Each of the four chains cradles a small ring-shaped pigment called a heme group, and at the center of each heme sits a single iron atom (Fe²⁺). That iron atom is the actual binding site for oxygen.
Figure 18.7 — Hemoglobin. (a) A molecule of hemoglobin contains four globin proteins, each of which is bound to one molecule of the iron-containing pigment heme. (b) A single erythrocyte can contain 300 million hemoglobin molecules, and thus more than 1 billion oxygen molecules.
Each hemoglobin molecule can therefore bind four O₂ molecules — one per heme. With 250 million hemoglobin molecules per red cell, a single cell can carry about a billion molecules of oxygen at once. Multiply by 20 trillion cells and you get the carrying capacity of your bloodstream: enough oxygen, at any given moment, to keep every tissue in your body alive for the next four minutes.
Hemoglobin is red, but it is two shades of red. Oxygenated hemoglobin (oxyhemoglobin) is the bright cherry-red of arterial blood. Deoxygenated hemoglobin is the darker, more burgundy red of venous blood. The color change is not a chemical change in the iron — the iron stays Fe²⁺ the entire time — but a subtle shift in the electron environment around it when O₂ binds.
Cooperative binding — why hemoglobin loads and unloads where it does
If you plotted “how much oxygen is bound to hemoglobin” against “how much oxygen is in the surrounding fluid,” you would not get a straight line. You would get a curve that is shallow at low oxygen levels, then suddenly steep through a middle range, then flat at the top. This is the famous oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve — an S-shape that you will meet again in Module 9 when we study the lungs.
Hemoglobin: Your Body’s Oxygen Delivery Service — the 4-part structure, its four oxygen-binding sites, and cooperative binding, where loading the first O₂ molecule makes the next three easier to bind.
The shape comes from cooperative binding: when the first oxygen molecule binds, hemoglobin changes shape slightly and the other three sites become easier to fill. When the first oxygen leaves at a target tissue, the other three become easier to release. This means hemoglobin loads up fully when surrounded by the high-oxygen environment of the lungs, and dumps its cargo quickly when it reaches the low-oxygen environment of a working muscle. The curve is the cell’s physical answer to the question: how do I carry oxygen across a body where some places need it more than others?
Iron, erythropoietin, and where new red cells come from
Iron is the limiting raw material. The body recycles iron carefully when old red cells are broken down, but daily losses still require iron in the diet (about 8–18 mg per day, more for menstruating people). Iron-deficiency anemia — not enough iron to make functional hemoglobin — is the most common nutritional deficiency in the world.
Figure 18.2 — Composition of Blood. Depressed hematocrit (anemia, too few red cells) and elevated hematocrit (polycythemia, too many) show how erythrocyte production — regulated by iron supply and erythropoietin — can swing in either direction.
Red cell production is regulated by a hormone you met in Module 6: erythropoietin (EPO), released by the kidneys when blood oxygen falls. EPO travels to the bone marrow and tells stem cells to ramp up red-cell production. People who travel to high altitude make more EPO within hours; their hematocrit rises over weeks, which is why endurance athletes train at altitude. EPO is also given as a drug to patients with kidney failure (who cannot make enough of their own) and has been notoriously abused as a doping agent in endurance sports.
🔬 CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH — ARTIFICIAL BLOOD
Donated blood is a scarce resource. It must be type-matched, refrigerated, and used within weeks. In emergencies — combat trauma, rural hospitals, mass casualty events — type-matched whole blood is often not available in time. For decades, scientists have been working on hemoglobin-based oxygen carriers (HBOCs): engineered proteins or liposomes that can carry oxygen the way hemoglobin does, but without needing a red cell, a blood type, or refrigeration.

Several candidate products have reached human trials. Some have shown promise in livestock and short-duration trauma use. None has yet received broad regulatory approval in the United States, and several earlier products were withdrawn after concerns about cardiovascular side effects. Researchers are currently exploring perfluorocarbon emulsions (which dissolve O₂ the way carbonated water dissolves CO₂), polymerized hemoglobin from bovine sources, and recombinant human hemoglobin produced in genetically modified microbes. None of these can yet do everything a real red blood cell does — carry oxygen, modulate release through cooperative binding, and last 120 days — but the goal is a stable, universal, shelf-storable oxygen carrier for emergencies. Whether such a product will become routine is still an open question.
▶  Self-check: name three features of the red blood cell that all serve the same function before clicking
Several good answers, all pointing the same direction — maximizing oxygen-carrying capacity:

1. The biconcave-disc shape maximizes surface area and shortens the diffusion path.
2. No nucleus means more cytoplasmic volume for hemoglobin.
3. No mitochondria means the cell does not consume the oxygen it carries.
4. The flexibility of the membrane lets the cell pass through capillaries narrower than itself.

Every feature trades something away — the ability to divide, to repair, to make protein — in exchange for more oxygen capacity. This is form following function with no compromises.
