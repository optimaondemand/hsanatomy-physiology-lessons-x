📖 READING
Why a paper cut does not kill you
A typical adult has about five liters of blood circulating under pressure. Every centimeter of every vessel is a potential leak. Yet you lose almost no blood from minor cuts, scrapes, and bruises — usually a few drops, then nothing. The reason is hemostasis (literally “blood standing still”): a layered set of mechanisms that detect vessel damage and seal it within seconds, then build a permanent patch over the following minutes to hours. Hemostasis has three sequential phases.
Phase 1: Vascular spasm
The instant a vessel is cut, the smooth muscle in its wall contracts powerfully. This narrows the lumen of the vessel and reduces blood flow through the wound, sometimes by 50% or more. The spasm lasts for minutes to hours and is triggered by direct injury to the muscle cells, by chemicals released from damaged endothelium and from platelets, and by pain reflexes through the autonomic nervous system. Vascular spasm alone cannot close a wound, but it buys time for the rest of the response.
Phase 2: The platelet plug
A healthy endothelium — the smooth inner lining of every blood vessel — is non-sticky on purpose. Platelets glide past it without binding. When the endothelium is torn, however, the underlying collagen is exposed. Collagen is sticky to platelets, and within seconds of contact:
1. Adhesion. Platelets stick to the exposed collagen, anchored by a plasma protein called von Willebrand factor.
2. Activation. The adhered platelets change shape from smooth discs to spiked spheres and release the contents of their internal granules — ADP, serotonin, and thromboxane A₂. These chemicals act as a chemical recruiter, drawing more platelets in and making them sticky.
3. Aggregation. Newly arrived platelets bind to the activated ones, forming a soft, plug-shaped mass — the platelet plug — within a minute. The plug is enough to close a small wound. It is not enough to close a larger one, because it is held together by nothing more than platelet-to-platelet stickiness. The next phase reinforces it with a protein mesh.
Phase 3: Coagulation — the fibrin mesh
The blood plasma carries about a dozen clotting factors — proteins identified by Roman numerals (Factor I, Factor II, Factor III, and so on up to Factor XIII). Most are made in the liver. They circulate as inactive precursors, waiting. When vessel damage occurs, two starting paths — the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways — converge on a common pathway, and the cascade picks up speed at each step. The end of the cascade is the conversion of two specific factors:
Prothrombin (Factor II) → Thrombin. The active enzyme thrombin is the cascade’s output.
Fibrinogen (Factor I) → Fibrin. Thrombin then converts the soluble plasma protein fibrinogen into long, stringy fibrin threads, which weave a mesh around the platelet plug and trap red blood cells in the mesh. The whole structure is the blood clot — a fibrin-reinforced platelet plug studded with trapped cells.
Calcium ions and vitamin K are essential cofactors throughout the cascade. This is why anticoagulant medications like warfarin (which interferes with vitamin K) and why diseases of liver function or vitamin-K deficiency cause bleeding problems.

(b) The synthesis of fibrin in blood clots involves either an intrinsic or an extrinsic pathway, both of which lead to a common pathway. (credit: modification of work by Kevin MacKenzie)
After the clot — healing and dissolution
Once the wound is sealed, the underlying tissue rebuilds. Fibroblasts crawl in and lay down new collagen; the endothelium grows over the damaged area; and an enzyme called plasmin slowly digests the fibrin mesh from the inside out, dissolving the clot as the tissue heals beneath it. If clotting is too aggressive, a clot that forms inside an intact vessel — a thrombus — can block circulation. If a piece breaks loose and travels through the bloodstream, it becomes an embolus. We will return to this when we discuss strokes and heart attacks in the heart and vessel lessons.
💡 KARL LANDSTEINER AND THE ABO BLOOD GROUPS
In the 19th century, doctors tried again and again to transfuse blood from one human to another. They had no choice — severe hemorrhage from childbirth or trauma was unsurvivable without volume replacement. Some transfusions worked. Many killed the patient within minutes, the recipient’s blood collapsing into clumps in front of the surgeon’s eyes. No one knew why.

Karl Landsteiner (1868–1943), an Austrian-American immunologist, solved the mystery between 1900 and 1902. Working in a quiet laboratory in Vienna, he mixed the blood of different colleagues together drop by drop on glass slides and observed which combinations clumped. He found that human blood came in three patterns (he later named them A, B, and O; a fourth, AB, was identified by his students soon after). Each person made antibodies in their plasma against any of the “foreign” surface markers their own red cells did not carry. A person whose red cells were Type A made anti-B antibodies. A Type B person made anti-A. A Type O person made both anti-A and anti-B. A Type AB person made neither.

Landsteiner’s discovery made safe transfusion possible. By 1907, the first cross-matched transfusion was performed in New York. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1930. In 1937 he co-discovered the Rh factor, which explains why a previously transfused Rh-negative person can react against Rh-positive donor blood, and why Rh-positive babies can be at risk in Rh-negative mothers. The system of blood typing he uncovered is still the foundation of every hospital’s blood bank today.
The ABO blood group system
Every person’s red blood cells carry sugar-based surface markers called antigens. The two relevant ABO markers are A and B. A person can have one of four ABO types based on which antigens they carry:
Type A — carries A antigen on red cells; carries anti-B antibodies in plasma.
Type B — carries B antigen on red cells; carries anti-A antibodies in plasma.
Type AB — carries both A and B antigens; carries neither antibody. The universal recipient.
Type O — carries neither A nor B antigen; carries both anti-A and anti-B antibodies. The universal donor (for red cells — plasma transfusion rules are different).
Transfusion has one rule that follows from this scheme: never give a recipient red cells carrying an antigen they make antibodies against. If you give Type B blood to a Type A patient, the patient’s anti-B antibodies will attack the donor red cells, clump them, and trigger a life-threatening hemolytic transfusion reaction within minutes.

Rh factor
In addition to A and B, red cells may carry a third surface protein called the Rh antigen (named for the Rhesus monkey, in which it was first identified). About 85% of people have it (Rh-positive); the rest do not (Rh-negative). Unlike ABO, anti-Rh antibodies do not exist in the plasma at birth — an Rh-negative person only makes them after being exposed to Rh-positive blood (through a prior transfusion or pregnancy). This delay is the basis of hemolytic disease of the newborn: an Rh-negative mother who carries an Rh-positive fetus can become sensitized during the first pregnancy and attack the red cells of a subsequent Rh-positive fetus. The modern solution is a preventive injection of anti-Rh antibodies (RhoGAM) during and after the first pregnancy, which is one of the most successful interventions in modern obstetric medicine.

▶  Self-check: name the three phases of hemostasis in order, and explain why Type O is the “universal donor”
1. Vascular spasm → 2. Platelet plug formation (adhesion, activation, aggregation) → 3. Coagulation (the cascade that converts prothrombin to thrombin, and fibrinogen to fibrin, weaving a mesh around the plug).

Type O red cells carry neither A nor B antigens, so they do not provoke either kind of antibody attack. They can be given to any ABO recipient without triggering an ABO reaction — hence “universal donor” for red cells. (Plasma transfusion has the opposite rule because it is the antibodies that move, so Type AB is the universal plasma donor.)
