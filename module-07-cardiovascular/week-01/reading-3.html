Leukocytes and Platelets: The Defenders in the Bloodstream
Reading
📖 READING
The minority that defends the rest
If you stained a drop of blood and counted what you saw, you would count about a thousand red cells for every one white cell. Leukocytes — white blood cells — are a tiny fraction of the formed elements by number. But they are the entire reason your body can survive contact with bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites, cancer cells, and damaged tissue.
Unlike red cells, white cells have nuclei. They have mitochondria. They can crawl out of capillaries into the tissues. They can change shape, swallow things whole, release toxic chemicals, and make antibodies. They are full cells doing full-cell work — just with the bloodstream as their highway. We will meet the immune system in detail in Module 8. This reading is your first introduction.
The five named leukocytes
Leukocytes split first into two large families based on whether their cytoplasm contains visible granules under a microscope. The granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils) carry pre-loaded chemical weapons in their granules. The agranulocytes (lymphocytes and monocytes) carry their power in the receptors on their surface and in the proteins they make. There are five named types total; remember them in roughly the order of how common they are in blood.
Figure 18.11 — Granular Leukocytes. A neutrophil has small granules that stain light lilac and a nucleus with two to five lobes. An eosinophil’s granules are slightly larger and stain reddish-orange, and its nucleus has two to three lobes. A basophil has large granules that stain dark blue to purple and a two-lobed nucleus.
Neutrophils — the first responders (~50–70% of WBCs)
Neutrophils are the most abundant white blood cell. They are short-lived (hours to days), aggressive, and they arrive first. Their nuclei have a characteristic multi-lobed shape — usually three to five lobes connected by thin strands — which is why they are sometimes called “polys” (polymorphonuclear cells). Neutrophils are professional phagocytes: they crawl out of capillaries by squeezing between endothelial cells (a process called diapedesis), follow chemical trails to bacteria, swallow the bacteria whole, and digest them inside intracellular pouches called phagolysosomes. When neutrophils die in large numbers at a site of infection, the yellowish fluid you would call pus is mostly dead neutrophils.
Lymphocytes — specific immunity (~20–40%)
Lymphocytes are the second-most-common type, but they are the most important for long-term immunity. There are two big subfamilies, and you will meet both in detail in Module 8. B lymphocytes (B cells) make antibodies — soluble proteins that lock onto specific invaders and tag them for destruction. T lymphocytes (T cells) come in two important flavors: helper T cells coordinate the rest of the immune response, and cytotoxic T cells kill body cells that have been infected by viruses or have turned cancerous. A small lymphocyte looks unimpressive under a microscope — round, with a huge nucleus and almost no cytoplasm — but each one carries surface receptors capable of recognizing one specific molecular target out of millions.
Monocytes — the heavy clean-up crew (~2–8%)
Monocytes are the largest leukocytes — about two to three times the diameter of a red cell — with a kidney-bean-shaped nucleus. They circulate for a day or two and then leave the bloodstream to take up residence in the tissues, where they mature into macrophages. A macrophage is essentially a long-lived, heavy-duty phagocyte: it patrols a particular tissue for years, eats anything that looks foreign, and tells the lymphocytes what it found by presenting molecular fragments on its surface (we will return to antigen presentation in Module 8).
Agranulocytes: the lymphocyte and the monocyte, the two leukocyte types without visible cytoplasmic granules.
Eosinophils — parasite-killers (~1–4%)
Eosinophils have a bilobed nucleus and granules that stain bright pink with the dye eosin (hence the name). Their granules contain proteins that punch holes in the membranes of parasitic worms — the cells’ main job. They are also implicated in allergic inflammation: people with asthma and severe seasonal allergies often have elevated eosinophil counts because the cells are partly responsible for the inflammation that makes asthma airways constrict.
Basophils — the smallest crowd (<1%)
Basophils are the rarest leukocyte. Their dark-blue granules contain histamine and heparin. Histamine causes the local blood vessels to dilate and to leak, producing the redness, warmth, swelling, and itching of an inflammatory or allergic response. Heparin is a natural anticoagulant. Basophils circulate in the blood, while their close relatives in the tissues — mast cells — do most of the same job locally, and are the cells responsible for the worst of an anaphylactic reaction.
A mnemonic worth keeping
From most to least abundant: Neutrophils, Lymphocytes, Monocytes, Eosinophils, Basophils. The mnemonic “Never Let Monkeys Eat Bananas” is harmless and catches in the memory. Knowing the ranking matters in clinical contexts: a bacterial infection typically raises neutrophils first; a viral infection typically raises lymphocytes; a parasitic infection raises eosinophils. The pattern of a complete blood count tells the physician where to look.
Platelets — not cells, but essential
Platelets (also called thrombocytes) are not whole cells. They are small fragments — about 2 to 4 micrometers across — that bud off the cytoplasm of very large multinucleated cells in the bone marrow called megakaryocytes. A single megakaryocyte can shed about 4,000 platelets before exhausting itself.
A healthy adult has 150,000 to 400,000 platelets per microliter of blood. They circulate quietly for about ten days. When they encounter a torn blood-vessel wall — specifically, exposed collagen that should normally be hidden under the endothelium — platelets undergo a dramatic transformation. They become sticky, change from disc-shaped to spiked, release chemical signals that recruit more platelets, and aggregate at the wound to form a soft plug within seconds. We will work through the full clotting cascade in the next lesson; this lesson’s job is to introduce the platelet itself.
