📖 READING
Yesterday (Reading 2.03) covered skin’s functions and its three layers. Today finishes the integument with the structures embedded in it, what happens when it is damaged, and what happens when its cells lose control of division.
Accessory structures
Embedded in or extending from the skin are several structures that develop from epidermal tissue but reach down into the dermis.
Hair follicles are tubular structures that extend from the epidermis into the dermis, producing hair from a region called the matrix at the base of each follicle. Hair grows by cell division at the matrix, with each new cell pushing the previous ones upward and filling with keratin as it goes — the same process that builds the epidermis above, made into a thread.
Nails are dense plates of keratin produced by epidermal tissue at the nail root (technically called the nail matrix). The visible nail plate rests on the nail bed, is bordered by folds of skin called the nail cuticle, and shows a pale crescent near its base — the lunula (from lunul-, “crescent”), the visible tip of the nail matrix where new nail cells are still translucent. Nails protect the tips of fingers and toes and aid in fine manipulation.
Nails: the nail is an accessory structure of the integumentary system, produced by the nail matrix at the nail root.
Sweat glands — also called sudoriferous glands (from sudor, “sweat”) — come in two types. Eccrine sweat glands cover most of the body and produce a watery sweat used in thermoregulation. Apocrine sweat glands are concentrated in the armpits and groin and produce a thicker, oilier secretion.
Sebaceous glands (from seb-, “grease”) are usually associated with hair follicles and secrete an oily substance called sebum that lubricates the skin and hair and contributes to the skin’s slightly acidic pH (around 5.5), which discourages many bacteria.
Accessory structures of the skin: hair shaft, hair follicle, arrector pili muscle, sebaceous gland, and eccrine and apocrine sweat glands.

Burns: a quick clinical note
Burns are classified by which layers of skin they damage — the layers you read about yesterday. First-degree burns affect only the epidermis — sunburn is the most common example. The skin reddens and may peel, but it heals on its own within days because the stratum basale, where new keratinocytes are made, is untouched. Second-degree burns extend into the dermis. Blisters typically form, and healing takes weeks. The skin can usually still regenerate because the deepest stem cells of the epidermis are spared. Third-degree burns destroy both the epidermis and the dermis, including all the regenerative stem cells. Skin cannot heal itself from a third-degree burn — new skin must come from somewhere else. Historically this meant skin grafts from another part of the patient’s body. Recent research is changing what is possible.

📊 CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH — GROWING SKIN IN A LAB
For severe burn patients, the limiting factor in recovery has long been how much undamaged skin there is to harvest for grafts. Researchers have spent the past several decades exploring how to grow skin from a small biopsy outside the body and apply it to wounds.

Modern bioengineered skin products take a small sample of the patient’s own skin (a few square centimeters), isolate the stem cells from the epidermis, and culture them on a scaffold of collagen until they form sheets large enough to cover serious burn wounds. Some products now incorporate dermal cells alongside epidermal stem cells, attempting to recreate both layers in a single graft. Researchers are also exploring 3D-printed skin patches and skin grown from induced pluripotent stem cells (cells reprogrammed from any tissue back to a stem-cell-like state). The technology is real and being used in clinical practice; it is still being refined, particularly around restoring sweat glands and hair follicles in the regenerated tissue.
Cancer: cell division out of control
Recall from yesterday’s reading that the epidermis depends on constant, controlled cell division: keratinocytes are born in the stratum basale and pushed upward on a roughly month-long schedule. That division is normally regulated by a network of genes acting as accelerators and brakes. Proto-oncogenes push cells to divide; tumor-suppressor genes hold them back. In a healthy cell, these systems are balanced — cells divide when they need to, and stop when they should.
Cancer begins when mutations break this balance. A mutated proto-oncogene becomes an oncogene, jamming the accelerator. A mutated tumor-suppressor gene fails to put on the brakes. Often, several mutations in different genes are needed before a cell loses its controls completely. Once it does, the cell divides when it shouldn’t. Its daughters inherit the broken controls. The mass of dividing cells is a tumor. A tumor that stays in one place is benign. A tumor whose cells invade nearby tissue and spread through the bloodstream or lymph system is malignant — this is what we call cancer.
Skin is where you can see this most directly, because you can see the cell types involved. Basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma arise from mutated keratinocytes in the stratum basale and stratum spinosum — the same rapidly dividing cells that normally renew your epidermis every month, now dividing without stopping. Melanoma, the most dangerous skin cancer, arises from the melanocytes you read about yesterday — the same pigment-producing cells that protect you from UV damage can themselves become cancerous when that same UV radiation damages their own DNA. This is why sun exposure is the leading risk factor for skin cancer: the very radiation that melanin exists to block is also the radiation most likely to mutate the cell that makes the melanin.
The body has multiple layers of defense before mutations reach this point: DNA repair enzymes, cell-cycle checkpoints that halt division if damage is detected, and programmed cell death (apoptosis) that disposes of cells beyond repair. Cancer is what happens when enough of these layers fail in a single cell line at the same time. The good news: this is rare. The hard news: across a long lifespan in a body of trillions of cells, it does happen.
Skin and homeostasis
When the body needs to lose heat, blood vessels in the dermis dilate and sweat glands secrete water onto the surface, where it evaporates and pulls heat with it. When the body needs to retain heat, dermal vessels constrict, sweat production drops, and tiny muscles attached to hair follicles (the arrector pili) raise the hair to trap a layer of warmer air against the skin (the goosebumps you see). This is skin doing thermoregulation continuously and well. Burns and skin cancer are what it looks like when that regulation, or the tissue doing the regulating, breaks down — a burn victim with extensive skin loss cannot thermoregulate or hold in fluid properly, which is why third-degree burns are life-threatening even without infection. As you read about more body systems, watch for the same pattern: healthy tissue quietly maintaining homeostasis in the background, and disease as what happens when that maintenance fails.
