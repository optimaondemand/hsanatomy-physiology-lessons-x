Lesson focus: The genetics of cell division breaking down — and why it shows up first in skin
📋 ASSIGNMENT OVERVIEW
Cancer is not a single disease — it is what happens when the genetic controls on cell division fail. This assignment goes one layer deeper than the required Lesson 2.04 assignment: instead of asking what skin structures do, it asks what goes wrong at the cellular level when they stop working correctly, and why skin is the one organ where you can watch the connection between a specific cell type and a specific cancer play out in front of you.
🎯 FOCUS QUESTION
Melanin exists to protect skin from UV damage. Why does that same UV radiation make melanocytes — the cells that produce melanin — especially likely to become cancerous?
📝 STUDENT DIRECTIONS
1
In 2–3 sentences, describe the role of proto-oncogenes and tumor-suppressor genes in a healthy cell. (The accelerator/brakes analogy from the reading is fine to use.)
2
In a paragraph (4–5 sentences), trace the chain from a single mutation to a malignant tumor. Then connect it back to specific skin cells: name which epidermal cell type basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma arise from, and which epidermal cell type melanoma arises from.
3
Name at least two of the body’s defenses that normally prevent this chain from completing (e.g., DNA repair enzymes, cell-cycle checkpoints, apoptosis). Briefly describe what each one does.
4
In a final 3–4 sentence paragraph, engage the focus question directly. Do not restate the question.
5
Total length: 200–250 words. Submit as Canvas text entry, or attach a .docx or .pdf.
✅ SUCCESS CRITERIA
☐
Distinguishes proto-oncogenes from tumor-suppressor genes accurately.
☐
Traces the mutation-to-tumor chain, and correctly names the epidermal cell type behind basal/squamous cell carcinoma (keratinocytes) and melanoma (melanocytes).
☐
Names at least two body defenses against cancer and describes what each does.
☐
Final paragraph engages the melanin/UV focus question with original reasoning.
