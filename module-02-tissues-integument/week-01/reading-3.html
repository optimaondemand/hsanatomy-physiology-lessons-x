Skin as your first organ system
The skin is the largest organ in your body, by area and by mass. An average adult’s skin covers roughly two square meters and weighs about four kilograms (nine pounds). It is also the most accessible organ — you can touch it, look at it, and run experiments on it without any specialized equipment. We start the course’s eleven systems here, with the integumentary system, because it is a clean and complete example of how form follows function in the body. Two of the four tissue types you read about this week (epithelial and connective) do almost all of the work in skin, and the structure of each layer is matched precisely to a job.
What skin does
Skin performs several critical functions simultaneously.
Barrier protection. Skin separates your internal environment from the external world. It prevents water loss (without skin you would dehydrate within hours), blocks most pathogens from entering, and resists physical and chemical damage.
Thermoregulation. Skin is central to maintaining a stable body temperature (from thermo-, “temperature”). Sweat glands cool you when you are hot. Blood vessels in the dermis dilate (releasing heat) or constrict (conserving heat) depending on need. Subcutaneous fat insulates against cold. When these systems are overwhelmed, the result is hyperthermia (from hyper-, “above”) — a dangerous rise in core body temperature, as in heat stroke, when sweating can no longer keep pace with heat gain — or hypothermia (from hypo-, “below”), a dangerous drop in core temperature when heat loss outpaces the body’s ability to generate or conserve it. You will see thermoregulation as the canonical example of homeostasis throughout this course, and you will look more closely at what happens when it fails in Lesson 2.04.
The thermoregulation feedback loop: skin blood vessels and sweat glands respond to deviations from the homeostatic temperature set point in either direction.
Sensation. Skin contains a dense field of receptors, each tuned to a different job: free nerve endings for pain and temperature, Meissner’s corpuscles for light touch, Pacinian corpuscles (deeper in the dermis and hypodermis) for pressure and vibration, and Merkel cells for sustained touch and texture. The signals these receptors generate are carried by sensory neurons to the spinal cord and brain. (Module 5.)
Mechanoreceptors in the skin: Meissner's corpuscle, Merkel's disks, Krause end bulbs, Ruffini's endings, Pacinian corpuscle, and free nerve endings (nociceptors).
A Merkel cell in the stratum basale, in direct contact with a sensory neuron — the structural basis of sustained touch and texture sensation.
Vitamin D synthesis. When ultraviolet light from sunlight strikes the skin, it converts a precursor molecule into vitamin D₃, which the liver and kidney further modify into active vitamin D. This vitamin is essential for absorbing calcium from food — without it, bones cannot form properly.
Immune defense. Skin contains its own population of immune cells (Langerhans cells, dermal dendritic cells, resident T cells) that detect and respond to invading pathogens before they can reach deeper tissues. (Module 8.)
The skin's resident immune cells: Langerhans cells (epidermis), and macrophages, dermal dendritic cells, T cells, and innate lymphoid cells (dermis).
The three layers of skin
Skin (from derm-, “skin”) is built from three layers stacked on top of one another. Each layer is dominated by one tissue type, and each does a different job.
A full cross-section of skin, showing the epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis along with accessory structures and receptors embedded in them.
Epidermis (top layer)
The epidermis is the outermost layer — the part you see when you look at your skin. It is built of stratified squamous epithelium, and it is organized into five named strata (from deepest to most superficial): stratum basale (also called the germinativum, where division happens), stratum spinosum, stratum granulosum, stratum lucidum (present only in the thick, hairless skin of palms and soles), and stratum corneum, the tough, dead surface layer.
The five strata of the epidermis under the microscope, with insets showing corneocytes, granular keratinocytes, Langerhans cells, spinous keratinocytes, epidermal basal cells, and melanocytes. (Image credit: kenhub)
The epidermis is built from a small number of specialized cell types. Keratinocytes make up the great majority of epidermal cells; they are born by division in the stratum basale, then are pushed upward through each stratum in turn, filling with the tough protein keratin as they rise, flattening, and finally dying by the time they reach the stratum corneum, where they form a layer of dead protective shingles. The whole process takes about a month, and it is the rapid renewal you read about in the epithelial-tissue reading, made specific. Melanocytes, seated in the stratum basale alongside the dividing keratinocytes, produce the brown pigment melanin, which protects deeper cells from UV damage and is the primary determinant of skin color — all humans have roughly the same number of melanocytes; people with darker skin have melanocytes that produce more melanin per cell. Dendritic cells (also called Langerhans cells) are immune sentinels scattered through the epidermis, on constant patrol for pathogens that breach the surface. The epidermis itself has no blood vessels — epithelium is avascular — so it depends entirely on the layer beneath it for nutrients.
The four epidermal cell types: (a) keratinocyte, (b) melanocyte, (c) Langerhans cell, and (d) Merkel cell. (Image credit: John Wiley & Sons, Inc.)
Skin pigmentation: the amount of melanin produced by melanocytes in the stratum basale, and taken up by surrounding keratinocytes, determines skin coloration.
Dermis (middle layer)
Beneath the epidermis lies the dermis, made primarily of dense irregular connective tissue. The collagen and elastin fibers in the dermis run in many directions, giving the skin both strength and stretch. The dermis is where most of the active machinery of skin lives: blood vessels (which feed the avascular epidermis above), sensory receptors, hair follicles, sweat glands, and sebaceous glands. The dermis is divided into a thinner upper papillary layer (looser, more cellular, with fingerlike projections that interlock with the epidermis above) and a thicker lower reticular layer (more fibrous, providing most of the strength). Together, the epidermis and dermis make up what anatomists call the cutaneous membrane — the true skin.
Layers of the skin: epidermis, dermis (papillary and reticular), and hypodermis. (Image credit: Cleveland Clinic)
The papillary layer (looser, more cellular) and reticular layer (denser, more fibrous) of the dermis, under the microscope.
Hypodermis (subcutaneous layer)
The hypodermis (also called the subcutaneous layer or superficial fascia, from sub-, “below”) lies beneath the dermis and connects skin to the underlying tissues. Strictly, it is not one of the two layers of the cutaneous membrane — anatomists classify it as a supporting region rather than part of the skin proper — but it is covered here alongside the epidermis and dermis because it works so closely with them. It is mostly loose connective tissue and adipose tissue (fat). Its functions are to insulate the body against heat loss, to cushion deeper structures from impact, and to store energy. Hypodermal fat is unevenly distributed across the body and varies by sex, age, and individual.
Adipose tissue of the hypodermis, showing fat-storing adipocytes. (Image credit: Science Photo Library)
