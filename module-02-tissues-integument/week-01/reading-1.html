The four tissue types
Welcome to Module 2. Last week you read that the body is built from cells, and that cells of similar type and function group into tissues. Despite the body’s variety, there are exactly four tissue types: epithelial, connective, muscle, and nervous. Every organ in your body is built from some combination of these four. This lesson covers the first two; tomorrow’s reading covers the other two.
The four tissue types, shown in representative locations throughout the body.
Epithelial tissue
Epithelial tissue (from the Greek epithe-, “laid on, covering”) covers the body’s surfaces, lines its cavities, and forms its glands. The outer layer of your skin is epithelium. So is the lining of your stomach, the lining of your blood vessels, the lining of your lungs, and the cells that produce sweat, saliva, hormones, and enzymes. Wherever the body needs a boundary — outside the body or between compartments inside it — epithelium does the work.
Three structural features define epithelium.
Cellular packing. Cells are tightly bound to each other with little extracellular matrix between them. The cells are essentially shoulder to shoulder.
Polarity. Epithelial cells have a top (apical surface, facing the outside world or a hollow space) and a bottom (basal surface, facing the body’s interior). The basal surface attaches to a thin sheet of extracellular matrix called the basement membrane.
Avascularity and regeneration. Epithelium has no blood vessels of its own — it gets its nutrients by diffusion from the connective tissue beneath. To compensate, epithelial cells divide rapidly and replace themselves frequently. The cells lining your small intestine, for example, are completely replaced every 3–5 days.
Classifying epithelium
Epithelium is named by two features: how many layers of cells, and the shape of the cells in the top layer.
Number of layers. Simple epithelium is one cell layer thick (from the Greek strata-/stratum-, “layer”). Stratified epithelium is multiple cell layers thick.
Cell shape. Squamous cells are flat and scale-like (from squam-, “scale”). Cuboidal cells are roughly cube-shaped. Columnar cells are tall, like columns.
Combine the two and you get the names. Simple squamous epithelium — one flat layer — lines blood vessels and forms the air-exchange surface of the lungs (where flatness allows fast diffusion). Stratified squamous epithelium — many flat layers — covers the skin and the inside of the mouth (where many layers protect against abrasion). Simple columnar epithelium lines the small intestine (where the tall cells absorb nutrients and contain glands). The naming is mechanical: figure out the layers and the shape, name the tissue.
Two further types are worth knowing because they look deceptively simple but aren’t. Pseudostratified columnar epithelium (from pseud-, “false”) is actually a single layer of cells, but the cells vary in height and their nuclei sit at different levels, making the tissue look layered under a microscope when it isn’t. It lines much of the respiratory tract, where it is often ciliated to sweep mucus and trapped particles up and out of the airway. Transitional epithelium is a stratified tissue whose cells can change shape — flattening when stretched, rounding up when relaxed — which lets it stretch dramatically without tearing. It lines the bladder and ureters, organs whose volume changes constantly as they fill and empty.
The eight types of epithelium, classified by number of layers and cell shape, including pseudostratified columnar and transitional epithelium.
Glands are also made of epithelium. Exocrine glands release their products through ducts to a body surface or cavity (sweat glands, salivary glands, mammary glands). Endocrine glands release their products — hormones — directly into the bloodstream (thyroid, pituitary, adrenal). You will meet endocrine glands in detail in Module 6.
Connective tissue
Connective tissue is the most diverse and most abundant tissue type in the body. It includes the loose padding under your skin, the dense fibrous bands of your tendons and ligaments, the cartilage in your joints, the bone of your skeleton, and the blood circulating in your vessels — all of them connective tissues. The shared trait is structural: connective tissue cells are sparse, and they sit in a large amount of extracellular matrix that they themselves produce.
The matrix has two components. Ground substance is a gel-like fluid (sometimes thin, sometimes very stiff) that fills the spaces between cells. Fibers — collagen, elastic, and reticular — are protein strands woven through the ground substance that give the tissue its mechanical properties. Different proportions of cells, ground substance, and fibers produce different connective tissues. A connective tissue that transports rather than supports needs a matrix built entirely differently — which is exactly what you’ll see when blood, below, turns the matrix into a liquid (from vas-, “vessel”).
Loose (areolar, adipose, reticular) connective tissue
Loose connective tissue has cells suspended in a soft, watery matrix with relatively few fibers. It is the body’s general-purpose packing material. Three subtypes:
Areolar tissue is found beneath every epithelium, around every blood vessel, between muscle bundles — everywhere general support and cushioning are needed.
Areolar tissue. LM × 400. (Micrograph provided by the Regents of University of Michigan Medical School © 2012)
Adipose tissue is fat — cells full of stored lipids that act as energy reserves, thermal insulation, and physical cushioning around organs.
Adipose tissue, showing adipocytes and their stored lipid droplets.
Reticular tissue forms the structural mesh of organs like the spleen and lymph nodes, where it cradles populations of immune cells.
Reticular tissue. LM × 1600. (Micrograph provided by the Regents of University of Michigan Medical School © 2012)
Dense (regular, irregular) connective tissue
Dense connective tissue is mostly collagen fibers. The matrix is reduced; the fibers dominate. Two subtypes:
Dense regular tissue has its collagen fibers all running in the same direction — tendons (muscle to bone) and ligaments (bone to bone). The parallel arrangement makes them very strong along the line of pull, but with little stretch sideways.
Dense regular connective tissue: collagen fibers and fibroblast nuclei run in parallel, as in a tendon. LM × 1000.
Dense irregular tissue has its collagen fibers running in many directions, like a tangled mat. It is found in the deep dermis of skin and in the protective capsules around many organs, where forces can come from any direction.
Dense irregular connective tissue: collagen fiber bundles and fibroblast nuclei run in a tangled mat, as in the deep dermis. LM × 200.
Specialized connective tissues
Three connective tissues sit apart because their matrices are highly specialized.
Cartilage has a dense, gel-like matrix loaded with collagen and other fibers, with cells (chondrocytes) housed in small cavities called lacunae. It is firm but flexible, and it has no blood supply. You will return to cartilage in Module 3 (skeletal joints).
Types of Cartilage: (a) hyaline cartilage, (b) fibrocartilage, and (c) elastic cartilage, each with chondrocytes housed in lacunae.
Bone has a matrix hardened with calcium phosphate, giving it the rigidity of stone with the responsiveness of living tissue. Module 3 covers bone in depth.
Blood is a connective tissue in which the matrix is liquid (plasma) and the “cells” (erythrocytes, leukocytes, platelets) are suspended in it. Module 7 covers blood in depth.
Blood: a fluid connective tissue containing erythrocytes and leukocytes (here, a lymphocyte) circulating in a liquid extracellular matrix. LM × 1600.
Notice the pattern: in connective tissue, the matrix can be liquid, gel, fibrous, or stone-hard. The cells are similar enough to share a name; the matrix is what gives each connective tissue its character.
💡 MARCELLO MALPIGHI AND THE LAYERS NO ONE COULD SEE
Marcello Malpighi (1628–1694) was an Italian physician and anatomist, working in Bologna a generation before the modern compound microscope reached its mature form. He used the early microscopes that were available to look more carefully at tissues than anyone before him. In 1661 he became the first person to observe capillaries — the tiny vessels that connect arteries to veins. William Harvey had argued in 1628 that blood must circulate from arteries to veins through some unseen connection, but he had never been able to see one. Malpighi saw them, in a frog’s lungs, and the model of circulation was complete.

Malpighi also made the first detailed observations of the layered structure of skin and the internal architecture of the spleen, kidney, and lungs. The deep cellular layer of the epidermis still bears his name — the stratum basale is sometimes called the “Malpighian layer.” He pushed anatomy from the gross-organ scale, where Vesalius had worked, to the tissue and microscopic scale — the level at which most disease begins, and at which most modern medicine still operates. You will meet the stratum basale again this week, when it becomes the engine of the epidermis in Reading 2.03.
When you look at any organ in this course, your first habit should be to ask: what tissues is it made of, and in what proportions? A liver is mostly epithelial cells in a connective-tissue scaffold. A tendon is almost pure dense regular connective tissue. A heart is mostly muscle (next reading), wrapped in connective tissue, lined with epithelium. The four tissue types are the alphabet of every organ.
