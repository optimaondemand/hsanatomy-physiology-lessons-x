The other two of the four
The previous reading covered epithelial and connective tissues — the body’s boundaries and its scaffolding. The remaining two tissue types share a different defining property: excitability. Cells in muscle and nervous tissue can change their electrical state and respond to that change in ways no other cells can. They are the body’s movement and signaling tissues.
Muscle tissue
All muscle cells are contractile: they have proteins arranged so that, when triggered, they slide past each other and shorten the cell. The body has three muscle types, each tuned to a different job.
Muscle tissue types compared: cell structure, major function, and location for skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle.
Skeletal muscle
The muscle that moves your bones. Skeletal muscle cells are unusually large — long, thin fibers that can run the entire length of a muscle. They appear striated under a microscope (the contractile proteins are arranged in repeating bands), and each fiber contains many nuclei (because, during development, many smaller cells fused). Skeletal muscle is voluntary: under conscious control by motor neurons. You will study its ultrastructure, the sliding-filament theory, and the neuromuscular junction in detail in Module 4.
Cardiac muscle
The muscle of the heart, found nowhere else in the body. Cardiac cells are also striated, but each one has a single nucleus and is much shorter than a skeletal-muscle fiber. They connect to each other through specialized junctions called intercalated discs, which let electrical signals pass directly from cell to cell — allowing the entire heart to contract as a coordinated unit. Cardiac muscle is involuntary: it generates its own rhythm and does not require conscious thought. You will study cardiac muscle in Module 7.
Smooth muscle
The muscle of the body’s tubes and hollow organs — blood vessels, the digestive tract, the bladder, the uterus, the airways, and many others. Smooth muscle cells are spindle-shaped and lack visible striations under a microscope (hence “smooth”). Each cell has one nucleus. Smooth muscle is involuntary: it contracts slowly and can stay contracted for long periods without fatigue. It is the muscle that moves food through your gut, that constricts a blood vessel to raise blood pressure, that pushes a baby through the birth canal. You will meet smooth muscle in nearly every system module from Module 7 onward.
Muscle tissue micrographs: (a) skeletal, (b) smooth, and (c) cardiac muscle. All images LM × 1600.
Nervous tissue
Nervous tissue (from neuro-, “nerve”) is found in the brain, the spinal cord, and the nerves that branch from them. Like muscle tissue, its cells are excitable — but instead of contracting, they generate electrical signals and pass them to other cells. Nervous tissue is what allows you to sense, think, decide, and move. It is built from two cell types.
Neurons
Neurons are the signaling cells. Each neuron has three structural parts. The cell body (soma) contains the nucleus and most organelles. The dendrites are short branched extensions that receive signals from other neurons. The axon is a single long extension that carries the signal away from the cell body and toward the next cell — another neuron, or a muscle, or a gland. A motor neuron in the spinal cord can have an axon a meter long, running all the way down to the muscles of the foot. Neurons are extremely specialized for signal transmission and largely do not divide once mature, which is why nervous-tissue injuries heal so poorly. You will study neurons in detail in Module 5.
A neuron: the cell body (containing the nucleus), the dendrites, and the axon, with microfibrils and microtubules visible inside.
Glia
Glia (sometimes called “neuroglia,” from glia-, “glue”) are the support cells of nervous tissue. They are not signaling cells, but neurons cannot work without them. There are several types — you will meet four in Module 5, but two are worth introducing now. Astrocytes wrap around neurons and blood vessels in the brain, regulating the chemical environment and helping form what is called the blood-brain barrier. Oligodendrocytes wrap themselves around the axons of neurons in the brain and spinal cord, forming a fatty insulating layer called myelin that dramatically speeds up signal transmission. (In peripheral nerves, a different cell type called the Schwann cell does the same job.) Glia outnumber neurons in the brain by a substantial margin.
Neuroglia: astrocytes, microglial cells, and oligodendrocytes (which form the myelin sheath around axons), alongside neurons.
Excitability: the trait muscle and nervous tissue share
Both muscle and nervous tissue are excitable — their cells maintain an electrical voltage across the membrane (the resting membrane potential), and they can rapidly change that voltage in response to a stimulus. The change is called an action potential: a brief, traveling pulse of electrical activity. Underneath both a muscle contraction and a nerve signal is the same basic event: charged particles (ions) move across the cell membrane through specific ion channels, doorways that open and close in a precisely timed sequence. In a neuron, the moving signal is heading to the next cell. In a muscle cell, it is the trigger that causes contraction. You do not need the electrical details yet — those arrive in Module 5 (nervous) and Module 4 (skeletal muscle), and again in Module 7 (cardiac). For now, hold onto the picture: ions moving down their concentration gradients through membrane channels is the basis of electrical signaling everywhere in the body.
This is the architecture of the body, in tissue terms: epithelium covers and lines, connective tissue scaffolds and connects, muscle contracts, and nervous tissue signals. Every organ in the eleven systems you study this year is some combination of these four. As you walk through the modules, ask of every organ: which tissues, and in what proportions?
