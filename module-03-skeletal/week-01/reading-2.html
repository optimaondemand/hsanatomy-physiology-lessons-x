Axial and appendicular — the two halves of the skeleton
Before we look at any single bone, we need a way to organize all 206 of them. Anatomists divide the skeleton into two regions:
Figure — The axial skeleton (tan) and appendicular skeleton (green).
The axial skeleton is the central column of the body — 80 bones arranged along the long axis from the top of the skull to the tip of the tailbone. It includes the skull, the vertebral column (spine), the rib cage, and the sternum. The axial skeleton is the body’s protective core: it surrounds the brain, the spinal cord, the heart, and the lungs. Almost everything the axial skeleton does is a variation on the verb protect.
The appendicular skeleton is everything else — 126 bones that hang off the axial skeleton in your arms, legs, and the bony girdles (shoulder and pelvis) that attach them. The appendicular skeleton is the body’s moving frame: it is what lets you reach, walk, lift, and run. Almost everything the appendicular skeleton does is a variation on the verb move.
80 + 126 = 206. We will spend Week 1 and the first half of Week 2 in the axial skeleton, then move to the appendicular skeleton through the rest of the module. We begin where most anatomy textbooks begin: at the top.
The skull — cranial and facial
The skull is the most architecturally complex region of the human skeleton. Twenty-two bones fit together with the precision of a stone arch, locked into place by jagged seams called sutures — the zig-zag lines you can see running across any skull you look at closely. Once you reach adulthood, every suture in your skull is fused, with one exception: the joints around the lower jaw, which stay mobile for chewing and speaking.
Figure — The skull’s major sutures: sagittal, coronal, squamous, and lambdoid.
The 22 skull bones split into two groups by function. Cranial bones (crani/o- = skull; the 8 you will trace this week) form the rounded vault that surrounds and protects the brain. Facial bones (the 14 you will meet in Reading 3.03) build the face, hold the teeth, and frame the eyes, nose, and mouth. This reading covers the eight cranial bones. The facial bones come next.
The eight cranial bones
The cranial bones are flat bones (remember — thin sandwich of compact bone around spongy bone in the middle). All eight together form the cranial cavity, the dome that houses the brain. Six of them are visible from the outside of the skull. Two are buried deep inside.
Figure — The skull, right lateral view, with the cranial bones, sutures, and major landmarks labeled.
Frontal bone (1)
The frontal bone forms your forehead and the upper part of each eye socket. Run your fingers across your forehead — that whole curved plate is one bone. The frontal bone also extends backward to form the roof of the orbits (the bony sockets that hold your eyes) and the front portion of the floor of the cranial cavity, where the front of the brain rests.
Parietal bones (2)
The two parietal bones — one on each side, left and right — form the upper sides and roof of the cranium. If you rest your hand flat on top of your head, you are touching both parietal bones. They meet each other along a suture that runs front-to-back along the midline of the skull (the sagittal suture), and they meet the frontal bone along the suture across your hairline (the coronal suture).
Temporal bones (2)
The two temporal bones — one on each side — form the lower sides of the cranium, the area around and behind your ears. The temporal bones are denser and more complex than the other cranial bones, because they house the inner ear (the hearing and balance organs you will meet in Module 5). The bony bump you can feel behind each ear — the mastoid process — is a projection of the temporal bone that anchors several muscles of the neck. Two more temporal bone landmarks matter for what comes next. The zygomatic process is a slender bridge of bone that projects forward from the temporal bone toward the cheek, where it forms a joint with the zygomatic bone (the cheekbone) — the two together form the zygomatic arch you can feel along the side of your face. The styloid process is a thin, pointed spike of bone projecting downward from the underside of the temporal bone, just in front of the mastoid process; several muscles and ligaments of the tongue and neck anchor to it.
Figure — The temporal bone, showing the mastoid process, styloid process, zygomatic process, and mandibular fossa.
Occipital bone (1)
The occipital bone forms the back of the skull and most of its base. The largest opening in the entire skeleton runs through the occipital bone — the foramen magnum, the “great opening,” through which the spinal cord descends from the brain. On either side of the foramen magnum, two rounded knobs (the occipital condyles) form the joint between the skull and the first vertebra of the neck. Without this joint you could not nod “yes.”
Figure — The skull, posterior view, showing the occipital bone, foramen magnum, and occipital condyles.
Sphenoid bone (1, deep)
The sphenoid bone sits deep inside the skull, behind the eyes, spanning across the cranial floor from one side to the other. From the side, it is shaped like a butterfly with wings spread — or, in older anatomy texts, like a bat. The sphenoid bone touches every other cranial bone, which has earned it the nickname “keystone of the cranial floor.” Sitting on top of the sphenoid is a small bony pocket called the sella turcica (“Turkish saddle”) that cradles the pituitary gland — the master gland you will meet in Module 6.
Figure — The sphenoid bone, superior and posterior views, showing its wing-shaped structure.
Ethmoid bone (1, deep)
The ethmoid bone is the most fragile bone in the skull. It sits in front of the sphenoid, between the two orbits (eye sockets), and forms much of the roof of the nasal cavity. The ethmoid is honeycombed with tiny air-filled spaces (the ethmoidal sinuses), and its upper surface is perforated with dozens of small holes through which the nerve fibers responsible for smell pass from the nose into the brain. Because the ethmoid is so thin and so close to the brain, head injuries that fracture this bone can be serious.
Figure — The ethmoid bone, showing the crista galli, cribriform plate, and superior/middle nasal conchae.
Sinuses, sutures, and openings — the marks of an efficient design
Several of the cranial bones (and several facial bones) contain hollow, air-filled spaces called paranasal sinuses (para- = beside, near — the sinuses sit beside the nasal cavity). The named sinuses follow the bone they sit in — frontal sinuses (above the eyes), ethmoidal sinuses (between the eyes), sphenoidal sinuses (behind the eyes), and maxillary sinuses (in the cheekbones, which are facial bones). Air-filled sinuses make the skull lighter while still preserving its strength. They also warm and humidify the air you breathe and resonate when you speak — which is why your voice sounds different when you have a head cold.
Sutures hold the cranial bones together. In a newborn, the sutures are still wide-open membranous gaps called fontanelles (“little fountains”), which is why a baby’s skull can compress slightly during birth and why it grows rapidly during the first two years of life. By around age two, the fontanelles have closed and the bones are fitted edge-to-edge. By adulthood the sutures themselves are fused. Adult human skulls do not flex.
Finally, every cranial bone is perforated with foramina — small holes through which blood vessels and cranial nerves pass into and out of the brain. The foramen magnum, mentioned above, is the largest, but there are dozens of smaller ones. Each foramen exists because something has to get through.
Figure — The cranial base, viewed from below and above, showing how the eight cranial bones and their foramina fit together.
