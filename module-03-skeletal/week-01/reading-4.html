📖 READING
The spine — a stack with a job
The vertebral column (vertebr/o- = vertebra, backbone) — what most people call the spine — is a vertical stack of bones that runs from the base of the skull to the tip of the tailbone. It does three things simultaneously, and the design of every vertebra is a compromise among them: it supports the weight of the head and trunk; it protects the spinal cord, which threads through a tunnel in the middle of the column; and it allows movement, especially flexion and rotation, in many directions.
Figure — The vertebral column: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, and 5 lumbar vertebrae, plus the sacrum and coccyx.
An adult human vertebral column has 26 bones: 7 cervical vertebrae in the neck, 12 thoracic vertebrae in the upper and middle back, 5 lumbar vertebrae in the lower back, 1 sacrum (5 fused bones), and 1 coccyx (3–5 small fused bones). Mnemonic that anatomy students use: breakfast at 7, lunch at 12, dinner at 5.
Between most pairs of vertebrae sits an intervertebral disc (inter- = between) — a tough fibrous ring with a softer gel-like center. Discs act as shock absorbers, distributing compression as you walk, run, and lift. About a quarter of the height of your spine is disc material, not bone. You are noticeably taller in the morning than at the end of the day, because discs compress slightly under gravity through the day and rebound overnight.
Figure — The intervertebral disc: annulus fibrosus, nucleus pulposus, and cartilaginous endplate.
The four spinal curves
Viewed from the side, the human spine is not straight — it has four gentle curves that alternate front and back. The cervical curve (in the neck) bends slightly forward. The thoracic curve (in the upper back) bends slightly back. The lumbar curve (in the lower back) bends slightly forward again. The sacral curve (at the tailbone region) bends back. These curves give the spine a shape like a stretched-out letter S, which is far stronger and more shock-absorbing than a straight column would be. Lose the curves, and the spine cannot do its job.
Figure — The four natural curves of the vertebral column, alternating front and back.
When these curves become exaggerated or twisted, the result is a spinal disorder. Scoliosis is an abnormal sideways curve. Kyphosis is an exaggerated thoracic curve (sometimes called a hunchback). Lordosis is an exaggerated lumbar curve. We will return to these in Lesson 3.12, when we cover skeletal pathology.
Figure — Scoliosis, kyphosis, and lordosis compared to a normal spinal curve.
The general design of a vertebra
Every vertebra in the column shares the same basic design, modified for the load and movement it has to handle. Three features matter most:
Figure — A typical vertebra, superior view (left), and articulated vertebrae, posterolateral view (right).
The body is the thick, weight-bearing block of bone on the anterior (front) side. The deeper into the spine you go, the larger the bodies get, because they carry more weight from above. The body is what makes a vertebra an irregular bone in the long, narrow column.
The vertebral arch is the bony ring on the posterior (back) side. Together, the body and the arch surround a hole called the vertebral foramen. Stack 26 of them, and the foramina form a continuous tunnel — the vertebral canal — through which the spinal cord runs from the brain to the lower back.
The processes are bony projections sticking out from each vertebra. There are typically seven of them per vertebra: one spinous process (the bump you can feel along the midline of your back), two transverse processes (one on each side), and four articular processes (two pointing up, two pointing down, where one vertebra meets its neighbors). These processes are where ligaments and muscles attach — every back muscle you will meet in Module 4 has at least one origin or insertion on a vertebral process.
Cervical vertebrae (C1–C7)
The seven cervical vertebrae (cervic- = neck) form the neck. They are the smallest and most mobile vertebrae in the column — they carry the least weight (just the head, which weighs about 5 kg in an adult) but allow the greatest range of motion. Cervical vertebrae have an additional feature that no other vertebrae have: a small hole in each transverse process called the transverse foramen. The vertebral arteries thread up through these foramina to supply blood to the back of the brain.
Figure — A typical cervical vertebra, and the atlas (C1) and axis (C2), which support the skull and allow it to turn.
The first two cervical vertebrae are also unique. C1, the atlas, is named after the Greek Titan who held up the sky — it holds up the skull. The atlas has no body of its own; instead, it has a ring shape that articulates with the occipital condyles of the skull and allows you to nod “yes.” C2, the axis, has a tooth-like projection (the dens) that points upward into the atlas. The atlas pivots around the dens, which is how you turn your head left and right to say “no.” This is the single most mobile joint in the spine, and one of the most vulnerable — severe whiplash injuries can damage the dens or its supporting ligaments.
Thoracic vertebrae (T1–T12)
The twelve thoracic vertebrae form the upper and middle back. Each one is paired with a rib — one rib on each side, anchoring posteriorly to the vertebra and curving forward toward the sternum. The thoracic vertebrae are larger than cervical vertebrae (more weight to carry from above) and have small flat faces (costal facets) where the ribs articulate. The spinous processes of thoracic vertebrae angle sharply downward, which limits backward extension but reinforces forward flexion — the thoracic spine is built more for stability than for mobility.
Figure — A thoracic vertebra, with the costal facets where each rib’s head and tubercle articulate.
The 24 ribs (12 pairs, one pair per thoracic vertebra) make up the rib cage, the bony shield that protects the heart and lungs. We will study the ribs themselves and the sternum in Week 2.
Figure — A rib articulating with its thoracic vertebra (top), and the assembled rib cage (bottom).
🔮 CURRENT RESEARCH — SEEING THE SPINE IN 3D
Scoliosis — a sideways curvature of the spine — develops in roughly 2–3% of adolescents. For most of the past century, scoliosis screening used a forward-bend test plus 2D X-rays, with imaging repeated every several months to track progression. The trouble is that 2D X-rays show the spine from a single angle; they miss the rotational component that often accompanies the lateral curve, and frequent X-rays expose growing patients to cumulative radiation.

Scientists and clinicians are currently exploring low-dose 3D imaging systems (such as biplanar imaging that reconstructs a 3D model from two simultaneous low-dose images) and surface-topography systems that map the back without radiation at all. Early results suggest these approaches can match or improve on the accuracy of traditional X-rays while reducing radiation exposure. Whether they will fully replace conventional imaging is still an open question — cost, availability, and clinical workflow all matter — but the underlying shift is clear: the future of spine screening is three-dimensional.
