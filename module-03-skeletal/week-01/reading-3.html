Facial Bones, the Hyoid, and Dentition

📖 READING
Fourteen facial bones
If the eight cranial bones are the vault that protects the brain, the fourteen facial bones are the architecture of who you look like and how you eat, speak, and breathe. The face is where the skull does most of its non-protective work: holding the teeth, framing the eyes, channeling air through the nose, hinging the jaw, and giving the skin a structure to drape over. The set of fourteen includes some single bones on the midline and several mirrored pairs.
Figure — The skull, anterior view, with the fourteen facial bones and their surrounding cranial bones labeled.
Figure — The paranasal sinuses: frontal, ethmoid, sphenoid, and maxillary.
Mandible (1)
The mandible is the lower jaw — the only bone of the skull that can move freely on its neighbors. It holds the lower teeth, anchors the muscles of chewing, and hinges on the temporal bone at a joint called the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), one on each side. That joint has a specific anatomical address: the rounded knob at the top of each side of the mandible, called the mandibular condyle (or condylar process), fits into a matching hollow on the temporal bone called the mandibular fossa. This is the exact location where your lower jaw meets the rest of your skull — every time you chew, speak, or yawn, the mandibular condyle is sliding and rotating inside the mandibular fossa. The mandible is the largest and strongest bone in the face. It is also the only one most people regularly fracture, because it sticks out.
Figure — The mandible, right lateral view, with the condylar process, coronoid process, ramus, and mandibular foramen labeled.
Maxilla (2)
The two maxillae (singular: maxilla, but the left and right halves fuse so completely that the result is often described as one bone) form the upper jaw. They hold the upper teeth, contain the maxillary sinuses (the largest of the paranasal sinuses), and form most of the hard palate that separates your mouth from your nasal cavity. The maxillae also contribute to the floor of each orbit and the side of each nasal opening. They are doing five jobs at once, which is why this is the most complex pair of facial bones.
Figure — The maxilla, right lateral view, showing the floor of the orbit, infraorbital foramen, and alveolar process.
Figure — Inferior view of the skull, showing the maxillae’s palatine processes forming the front of the hard palate.
Zygomatic bones (2)
The two zygomatic bones are your cheekbones — one on each side, prominent under each eye. They form the lateral wall and floor of the orbit and the bony arch you can feel running back to your ear (the zygomatic arch) — formed where the zygomatic bone meets the zygomatic process of the temporal bone. It is a small but useful pattern in skeletal anatomy: when two bones share a joint, they often share a name. Beyond shaping the face visually, the zygomatic bones are structural — the bony bridge they form helps transfer force from a blow to the side of the face away from the eye.
Figure — The zygomatic bone, anterior and posterior views.
Nasal bones (2)
The two small nasal bones meet at the midline of the face to form the bridge of the nose — the hard part you can feel near the top. Below the nasal bones, the rest of your nose is cartilage. This is why nasal-bone fractures are common in sports and why the lower nose can bend without breaking.
Figure — The nasal bones, anterior and lateral views.
The remaining facial bones (7)
There are seven additional facial bones you should know by name even if you will not be drawing them all. The two lacrimal bones sit at the medial corner of each eye socket and contain a small channel for tears to drain into the nasal cavity (which is why crying makes your nose run). The two palatine bones sit behind the maxillae and form the back of the hard palate. The vomer is a single bone forming the lower part of the nasal septum (the dividing wall between your nostrils). And the two inferior nasal conchae are scroll-shaped bones inside the nasal cavity that increase its surface area for warming and humidifying the air you breathe in. Together, that is 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 2 = 14 facial bones.
Figure — The lacrimal bone, at the medial corner of the eye socket.
Figure — The palatine bone, forming the back of the hard palate.
Figure — The vomer, forming the lower part of the nasal septum.
Figure — The inferior nasal concha, a scroll-shaped bone inside the nasal cavity.
The hyoid — the one bone that touches no other
The hyoid bone is unique in the human skeleton. It is the only bone that does not form a joint with another bone — it floats. Shaped like a slim horseshoe, the hyoid sits high in the front of the neck, between the lower jaw and the larynx, suspended in place by ligaments and the muscles that anchor to it. You can find your own hyoid: tilt your head back, then gently feel just below the angle of your jaw on the side of your neck. The small mobile bone that shifts when you swallow is the hyoid.
Figure — The hyoid bone, shown in place in the neck and in isolation, with its greater and lesser horns.
Because it touches no other bone, the hyoid is sometimes counted as part of the axial skeleton and sometimes counted on its own. Its role is to give the muscles of the tongue, the throat, and the floor of the mouth an anchor to pull against. Without the hyoid, you could not swallow effectively. You could not speak with the precision your language demands. The hyoid is small, it is hard to see, and it is essential.
Dentition — teeth are not technically bone
Your teeth look like bones and sit in bones, but anatomists do not classify them as bones. Teeth are a different kind of mineralized tissue, with different cells, a different developmental origin, and a different repair capacity (very little, compared to bone’s extensive remodeling). Formally, teeth are accessory structures of the digestive system, which you will meet in detail in Module 10. We cover them here because they are seated in the maxilla and mandible and travel with the skeleton in nearly every diagram and illustration.
An adult human has 32 permanent teeth: 16 in the upper jaw, 16 in the lower jaw. Each quadrant of the mouth (upper left, upper right, lower left, lower right) holds 8 teeth: 2 incisors, 1 canine, 2 premolars, and 3 molars (the last molar is the “wisdom tooth,” which some adults never grow or have surgically removed). Children begin with 20 deciduous (“baby”) teeth, which are shed and replaced over roughly the first twelve years of life.
Figure — The 32 permanent teeth, upper (maxillary) and lower (mandibular), numbered by quadrant.
The shape of each tooth follows its job. Incisors are blade-shaped — they cut. Canines are pointed — they pierce and tear. Premolars and molars are broad and flat-topped with raised cusps — they crush and grind. The change in tooth shape from the front to the back of your mouth is a textbook example of form following function: every bite of food is processed by the right tool for the job, in sequence.
