📖 READING
Bone is living tissue
If you have ever seen a skeleton in a biology classroom, you may have come away thinking of bones as inert — dry, rigid, finished objects that simply hold the body up. They are not. The bones in your body right now are active connective tissue: full of living cells, fed by blood vessels, threaded with nerves, and constantly remodeling themselves in response to the loads you place on them. The skeleton you have today is not the skeleton you had ten years ago. The atoms have been replaced, the architecture has shifted, and the bones you walk on tomorrow morning will be slightly different from the bones you walked on this morning.
Figure — The axial skeleton (tan) and appendicular skeleton (green), anterior and posterior views.
An adult human skeleton has 206 bones. Each one is a separate organ — remember that an organ is two or more tissue types working together for a specific function. Bones contain bone tissue, of course, but also connective tissue (the periosteum that wraps each bone), cartilage (at the joint surfaces and inside developing bone), nervous tissue (sensory and pain fibers), and vascular tissue (the blood vessels feeding the cells). The femur in your thigh is an organ. So is the smallest bone of your inner ear. Every one of them is alive.
Figure — Macroscopic anatomy of a long bone: diaphysis, epiphyses, medullary cavity, and periosteum.
The skeletal system as a whole has five major jobs. Support: it gives the body its frame, the way the studs and beams of a house give the house its frame. Protection: the skull surrounds the brain, the vertebrae surround the spinal cord, the rib cage shields the heart and lungs, and the pelvis cradles the bladder and reproductive organs. Movement: muscles pull on bones, and bones lever the body through space. Mineral storage: more than 99% of the body’s calcium is stored in bone, along with most of its phosphorus — both released into the bloodstream as needed. Hematopoiesis (hemo- = blood, -poiesis = formation, making): red bone marrow inside certain bones produces nearly all of the body’s red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets. This last function is the cross-connection to the cardiovascular system that you will meet again in Module 7.
Figure — Hematopoiesis: red bone marrow’s hematopoietic stem cells give rise to red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets.
Two kinds of bone tissue
At the tissue level, every bone is built from two kinds of bone tissue, arranged differently in different locations.
Compact (cortical) bone
Compact bone (ost-, osteo- = bone) is dense and solid. It forms the outer shell of every bone in the body, and the long shaft of the bones in your arms and legs. Under a microscope, compact bone is organized into osteons — concentric rings of mineralized matrix, called lamellae, wrapped around a central canal that carries a blood vessel and a nerve. Trapped between the lamellae are tiny spaces called lacunae, each holding one bone cell, connected to its neighbors by a microscopic web of channels called canaliculi — the routes nutrients and waste travel through solid bone. Each osteon is a tiny structural unit. Many osteons packed side by side give compact bone its remarkable resistance to compression. Compact bone is what an architect would call load-bearing material.
Figure — (a) Compact bone tissue, with osteons, concentric lamellae, and a central canal; (b) a single osteon under the microscope.
Zoom back out to the whole bone, and a typical long bone has a matching large-scale architecture. The shaft — the diaphysis (-physis = to grow) — is almost entirely compact bone wrapped around a hollow medullary cavity. At each end, the shaft widens through a transition zone called the metaphysis into the epiphysis, the rounded end built mostly of spongy bone that articulates with the neighboring bone at a joint. Two connective-tissue membranes complete the picture: the periosteum (peri- = around, surrounding) is a tough sheath wrapping the entire outer surface of the bone, anchoring muscle tendons and carrying the blood vessels and nerves that keep the bone alive; the endosteum is a thinner membrane lining the inside of the medullary cavity and the surfaces of spongy bone, where most of the cellular activity of bone remodeling actually happens.
Figure — Compact and spongy bone within a long bone: dense compact bone forms the shaft, porous spongy bone fills the ends.
Spongy (trabecular) bone
Spongy bone is found inside the ends of long bones, inside flat bones (like the bones of the skull), and inside vertebral bodies. It looks porous — like a frozen sea sponge — but the porosity is deceptive. The thin struts of bone tissue inside spongy bone, called trabeculae, are arranged precisely along the lines of force that the bone has to handle. Spongy bone is light, strong, and uses far less material than compact bone would. It also leaves spaces — spaces that are filled with bone marrow.
Figure — Spongy bone in cross-section: trabeculae, canaliculi, lacunae, and the osteocytes and osteoblasts lining them.
The cells of bone
Three cell types make and maintain bone. Osteoblasts (-blast = embryonic, formative cell, stem cell) build new bone, laying down the collagen-and-mineral matrix. Osteoclasts (-clast = break, destroy) tear it down, dissolving the matrix and releasing the calcium and phosphorus back into the bloodstream. Osteocytes (-cyt = cell) are mature, settled cells — former osteoblasts that have been trapped inside the matrix they built. Osteocytes act as mechanical sensors: they detect strain in the bone around them and signal when the architecture needs to be reinforced or pruned. Together, these three cells run a continuous demolition-and-construction project inside every bone of your body. About 10% of your skeleton is replaced each year.
Figure — The four cell types of bone tissue: osteocyte (maintains), osteoblast (forms matrix), osteogenic cell (stem cell), and osteoclast (resorbs).
💡 THE MAN WHO LOOKED INSIDE
For about thirteen hundred years, European medical schools taught anatomy from the writings of a Greek physician named Galen, who lived in the second century. Galen was a careful observer for his time, but Roman law forbade him from dissecting human bodies, so most of what he wrote about the human skeleton was inferred from dissecting Barbary macaques. His errors were repeated by every textbook that followed him.

In 1543, a 28-year-old Flemish anatomist named Andreas Vesalius published a book called De humani corporis fabrica — “On the Fabric of the Human Body.” Vesalius did something simple and radical: he dissected real human cadavers and drew what he actually saw. He hired the finest engravers in Italy to render his findings in detail, and the resulting illustrations corrected hundreds of Galenic errors — including specific errors about the structure of the human sternum, jaw, and femur. The Fabrica is sometimes called the first modern science book. It established the simple principle every working anatomist still follows: look at the body itself, not at what was written about it long ago. Without Vesalius, the rest of this course — and the VR figure you are about to study — would not be possible.
Four bone shapes
Anatomists classify bones into four major shapes. The shape tells you almost nothing about the bone’s size — long bones can be enormous (the femur) or tiny (the phalanges of your little finger) — but it tells you a great deal about what the bone is for.
Figure — Bone classified by shape: long (femur), flat (sternum), irregular (vertebra), short (cuneiforms), and sesamoid (patella).
Table — Bone classifications by shape, with distinguishing features, functions, and examples of each.
Long bones
Long bones are dog-bone shaped: narrow in the middle, expanded at each end. The femur in your thigh, the humerus in your upper arm, the tibia in your lower leg, and all the small finger and toe bones are long bones. The narrow middle (the diaphysis) is built for leverage and compression. The expanded ends (the epiphyses) are built to articulate with neighboring bones at joints. Long bones contain a hollow medullary cavity inside their shafts — a cavity that holds bone marrow. The fact that you can lift things at all is mostly because long bones exist.
Short bones
Short bones are roughly cube-shaped — about as long as they are wide. The eight carpal bones in each of your wrists and the seven tarsal bones in each of your ankles are short bones. They allow many small movements in many directions at once: think of how a wrist can twist, tilt, and rotate, in part because it is built out of eight independently-moving blocks rather than one solid bone.
Flat bones
Flat bones are thin and broad, often curved. The bones of the cranium that surround your brain are flat. So are your sternum (breastbone), your ribs, and the scapulae (shoulder blades) on your back. Flat bones are built to protect what is underneath them and to provide broad surfaces for muscle attachment. They are typically a sandwich: a layer of spongy bone (rich with red marrow) between two thin layers of compact bone.
Irregular bones
Irregular bones are everything else — bones whose shape does not fit any of the categories above because their job is too specific. Your vertebrae are irregular bones, each with a thick weight-bearing body, a hole through the middle for the spinal cord, and multiple bony projections sticking out. The bones of the face are mostly irregular. The shape is dictated entirely by the function: a vertebra is shaped exactly the way it has to be shaped to do what a vertebra has to do.
Bone markings — the surface features
If you run a finger along the surface of any bone — or examine one closely in VR — you will notice it is not smooth. Every bone is covered in markings: bumps, ridges, hollows, and holes. These are not decorations. Each one exists because something uses it.
Bony markings come in three broad categories. Projections stick out from the surface — bumps, ridges, spines, and tubercles. Most projections are muscle attachment sites, where a tendon anchors muscle to bone. A few projections are designed to form joints: the head of the humerus, for example, is a smooth, rounded projection that fits into a matching socket in the shoulder blade. Depressions are dents and grooves on the bone’s surface. They allow tendons, nerves, or blood vessels to pass along the bone without being damaged. The intertubercular groove of the humerus, near the shoulder, is a slot that holds a tendon of the biceps muscle in place. Openings are holes that pass straight through bone, like the foramen magnum at the base of the skull through which the spinal cord exits the brain. Every opening exists because some vessel or nerve needs to get through.
When you study a new bone this year, your first habit should be to find at least one marking and ask: what uses that? The answer will almost always be a muscle, a tendon, a ligament, a nerve, or a blood vessel. The marking is the bone’s record of a working relationship with another structure.
Figure — Example bone markings and features: processes (tubercle, tuberosity, crest), depressions (fossa), and openings (foramen, fissure, canal, sinus).
🔮 CURRENT RESEARCH — CAN WE PRINT BONE?
When a person loses a large piece of bone — to cancer surgery, trauma, or birth defect — the standard treatment has been to take bone from elsewhere in the body (an autograft) or from a donor (an allograft). Both work, but both have limits: harvesting bone from a patient hurts, and donor bone can fail to integrate or carry infection.

Scientists are currently exploring a different approach: bioprinting bone scaffolds. The idea is to use a 3D printer to lay down a porous scaffold in the exact shape of the missing bone — usually from biodegradable polymers, sometimes seeded with the patient’s own stem cells. The scaffold provides a temporary architecture, the cells colonize it and lay down real bone matrix, and over time the scaffold dissolves while the new bone grows in. Recent research has shown the basic principle works in animal models and in a small number of early human cases, particularly for jaw and skull reconstruction. Whether bioprinted scaffolds will become routine is still an open question — healing rates vary, and recreating the precise architecture of spongy bone’s load-aligned trabeculae remains hard. But the underlying biology — that bone responds to a scaffold and rebuilds itself around it — is exactly the same principle that has kept your skeleton alive and remodeling for as long as you have had one.
