The Lower Spine — Lumbar, Sacrum, Coccyx, and Intervertebral Discs
Reading
📖 READING
Lumbar vertebrae (L1–L5)
Below the rib cage, the spine enters its lumbar region — five vertebrae (L1 through L5) that carry more weight than any other vertebrae in the column. By the time you reach the lumbar spine, the body of each vertebra is supporting essentially everything above it: head, neck, arms, chest. Lumbar vertebral bodies are the largest in the spine. They are roughly kidney-shaped from above, and their thickness is what gives the lower back its visible curve.
Figure — A lumbar vertebra, with its large body and articular processes, shown in its position in the lower spine.
Lumbar vertebrae have several other distinctive features. Their spinous processes are short, blunt, and project almost straight backward — you can feel them as the bumpy ridge along the middle of your lower back. Their transverse processes are long and slender, providing leverage for the deep back muscles you will meet in Module 4. They do not have transverse foramina (a cervical feature) and they do not have costal facets (a thoracic feature). Lumbar vertebrae are built for one thing: bearing load.
Because they carry the most weight, lumbar vertebrae are also where many spine problems originate. Disc herniations and lower-back pain most commonly involve the L4–L5 and L5–S1 levels. A herniated disc at one of these levels can compress a spinal nerve root and cause pain that radiates down a leg — the condition popularly known as sciatica.
Figure — A herniated (slipped) disk compressing a spinal nerve, compared to a healthy disk.
The sacrum — five bones that became one
Below L5 sits the sacrum, a single triangular bone that forms the back wall of the pelvis. The sacrum began life as five separate vertebrae (S1–S5). Sometime between roughly age 18 and age 30, these five vertebrae fuse together into one bone. You can still see their outlines — horizontal ridges on the front of the sacrum (the transverse lines) mark where the original intervertebral discs sat. By adulthood, however, the sacrum behaves as one solid mass.
The sacrum’s shape is built for transferring load. At the top, the broad triangular surface meets L5 through a relatively flat joint. On each side, two surfaces called auricular surfaces meet the ilium bones of the pelvis (which you will meet in Lesson 3.09) at the sacroiliac joints. Weight from the upper body flows down the spine, into the sacrum, and out through the sacroiliac joints into the pelvis and the legs. This makes the sacrum a key “hub” bone — it does very little moving (the sacroiliac joints allow only millimeters of motion) but a tremendous amount of load transfer.
Figure — The sacrum and coccyx, anterior and posterior views, showing the sacral foramina and the fused vertebral remnants.
The coccyx — the tailbone
The coccyx, popularly called the tailbone, is the small triangular bone at the very bottom of the spine. Like the sacrum, the coccyx is a fusion product — typically three to five small vertebral remnants that fuse together over years. The coccyx is the evolutionary remnant of the tail our distant ancestors used; humans no longer use it for balance or signaling, but it remains useful: several pelvic-floor muscles and ligaments attach to it, supporting the pelvic organs.
A bruised or fractured coccyx (most often from falling backwards onto a hard surface) is one of the most painful and most stubborn of bone injuries to heal — partly because the coccyx is hard to immobilize, and partly because every time you sit down you put weight back on it.
Intervertebral discs — the shock absorbers
Between every adjacent pair of vertebrae from C2–C3 all the way down to L5–S1, there is an intervertebral disc. (The atlas–axis joint between C1 and C2 is the only spinal joint without a disc — its pivot architecture cannot accommodate one.) The discs are not bone. They are fibrocartilage — a tough, fibrous, weight-bearing connective tissue.
Figure — The intervertebral disc: the annulus fibrosus surrounding the nucleus pulposus, beneath the vertebra’s spinal cord and spinal nerves.
Each disc has two parts. The annulus fibrosus is the outer ring — tough, fibrous, made of layered collagen oriented in alternating directions. It is built to resist tearing and twisting. The nucleus pulposus is the inner gel-like core — about 80% water, surrounded by the annulus. When you load the spine, the nucleus pulposus deforms inside the annulus and distributes the pressure outward in all directions, evening out the load and absorbing some of the shock.
A herniated disc occurs when the annulus tears and some of the nucleus pulposus squeezes outward — sometimes far enough to press on a spinal nerve root passing through a nearby intervertebral foramen. This is what causes the leg pain in classical sciatica. Lumbar discs herniate more often than cervical discs not because they are weaker but because they have to bear more load.
Putting the spine together
You now have all the pieces. From top to bottom: 7 cervical vertebrae (with C1 atlas and C2 axis as specialists), 12 thoracic vertebrae (each paired with a rib), 5 lumbar vertebrae (large, weight-bearing), 1 sacrum (five fused), 1 coccyx (three to five fused). Between every pair of adjacent vertebrae except C1–C2 sits an intervertebral disc. Four gentle curves — cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral — give the column a long S-shape that distributes load and absorbs shock. Stacked vertically, all 26 bones add up to roughly 70 cm of spinal column carrying everything above it, all day, every day. The skeleton you will assemble in your Module 3 snapshot starts with this column.
▶  Self-check: name the two parts of an intervertebral disc before clicking
Annulus fibrosus (outer fibrous ring) and nucleus pulposus (inner gel core). A herniated disc is a tear in the annulus that allows the nucleus to bulge outward and potentially press on a nerve.
