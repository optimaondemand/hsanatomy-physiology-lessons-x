Lesson 3.05
The Lower Spine — Lumbar, Sacrum, Coccyx, and Intervertebral Discs
Lesson
Module thread — Perseverance. The lumbar spine bears more weight than any other part of the column. Every step you take loads it; every disc you have absorbs the shock so the bone above does not have to.
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Reading 3.05 — The Lower Spine: Lumbar, Sacrum, Coccyx, and Intervertebral Discs
The substantive content for this lesson lives on the paired Reading page in Canvas.

1
I will be able to describe how the lumbar vertebrae differ from cervical and thoracic vertebrae, and explain why.
2
I will be able to explain how the sacrum forms from five fused vertebrae and what role the sacroiliac joints play in load transfer.

✏️ YOUR DRAWING SESSION THIS WEEK
This week you complete the spinal column — lumbar vertebrae, sacrum, coccyx, and all intervertebral discs.
▶ Take a fresh page (paper highly recommended; a digital drawing file is also fine), title it with this lesson’s region as the next page of your Skeleton Atlas, and use the figures in this week’s reading as your reference. Draw each bone large enough to label.
▶ Draw the 5 lumbar vertebrae (yellow). Note how much larger the bodies are compared to the cervical and thoracic vertebrae you drew last week.
▶ Draw the sacrum (pink/magenta). Look for the transverse lines that mark the original five fused vertebrae.
▶ Draw the coccyx (blue), at the very tip of the spine.
▶ Draw each intervertebral disc (lighter color). Add one label on the L4–L5 disc reading “most herniated disc level.”

Tall-by-morning experiment
5 min today + ongoing
1
Measure your height first thing tomorrow morning (immediately after getting out of bed) and again just before bedtime. Record both. Most adults lose 1–2 cm of height during the day as the lumbar and lower thoracic discs compress, then regain it overnight. If you don’t notice a change in your height, don’t assume the experiment failed — propose a hypothesis for why: Did you measure at inconsistent times or with inconsistent posture? Is your day unusually low-load (mostly lying or sitting) or unusually high-load (mostly standing or carrying weight)? What would you measure differently tomorrow to test your hypothesis?
