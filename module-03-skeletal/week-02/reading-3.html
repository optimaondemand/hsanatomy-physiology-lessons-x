The Shoulder Girdle and the Upper Arm
Reading
📖 READING
Into the appendicular skeleton
For the first six lessons of this module you have been working through the axial skeleton — the central column built for protection. From this point on, you move into the appendicular skeleton: 126 bones built for movement. You will spend the rest of Week 2 and the first part of Week 3 on the upper limb, then finish the module with the lower limb and the joints that connect everything together.
Figure — The axial skeleton (tan) and appendicular skeleton (green) — you are now moving into the green.
The upper limb is anchored to the axial skeleton by a structure called the shoulder girdle (also called the pectoral girdle). Unlike the pelvic girdle, which is firmly fused to the spine through the sacrum, the shoulder girdle is loosely attached to the axial skeleton — mostly held in place by muscles, with only a single small bony connection per side. This is a deliberate trade-off: a loosely held shoulder is dramatically more mobile than a tightly bound one, which is why your arms can move in nearly every direction.
The clavicle — the only bony bridge
Figure — The pectoral (shoulder) girdle, anterior and posterior views, and the clavicle in detail.
The clavicle, commonly called the collarbone, is a long, S-shaped bone that runs across the front of the upper chest. You can feel both of yours easily — trace a finger from the top of your sternum out to your shoulder, and the hard ridge you encounter is the clavicle. There is one on each side, left and right.
The clavicle is the only bony connection between the shoulder girdle and the axial skeleton. It articulates with the manubrium of the sternum at the sternoclavicular joint (the medial end) and with the scapula at the acromioclavicular joint, the AC joint (the lateral end). Together, the two clavicles function as a pair of struts that hold the shoulders out and away from the chest. Without them, your shoulders would collapse forward.
The clavicle is also the most frequently broken bone in the human body. Its shape and position make it almost too good at its job — when you fall forward onto an outstretched arm, the force travels up the arm, into the shoulder, and along the clavicle to the sternum. The clavicle is the structural fuse in this chain, and it gives way before the more catastrophic injuries upstream (a dislocated shoulder, a fractured sternum, or worse) can occur. Most clavicle fractures heal cleanly with rest.
The scapula — the shoulder blade
The scapula — the shoulder blade — is a flat, triangular bone that sits on the upper back. There is one on each side, but neither of them touches the spine directly. The scapula is held in place by an enormous network of muscles (the rhomboids, the trapezius, the serratus anterior, the rotator cuff group, and several more you will meet in Module 4). It can move in nearly every direction along the back, which is why the shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body.
Figure — The scapula, anterior and posterior aspects, with the glenoid cavity, acromion, and coracoid process labeled.
Three features of the scapula matter most for our purposes. The glenoid cavity is the shallow socket on the lateral edge of the scapula where the head of the humerus fits — this is the bony part of the shoulder joint. The shallow shape of the glenoid cavity is essential to the joint’s mobility: a deeper socket would lock the humerus in place, like the hip joint locks the femur. The price is stability — the shoulder is also the most commonly dislocated major joint in the body. The acromion is a flat process at the top of the scapula that overhangs the glenoid cavity and articulates with the clavicle; it is the hard bony ridge you can feel on top of your shoulder. The coracoid process is a smaller, beak-like projection on the front of the scapula that anchors several upper-limb muscles, including a head of the biceps.
The humerus — bone of the upper arm
The humerus is the single bone of the upper arm — what most people loosely call “the arm bone” even though there are two more bones in the forearm. It is a long bone, narrow in the shaft and expanded at each end. Like all long bones, its narrow shaft is built for leverage, and its expanded ends are built to form joints with neighboring bones.
Figure — The humerus, anterior and posterior views, with the head, greater/lesser tubercles, epicondyles, and trochlea labeled.
At its proximal (upper) end, the humerus has a smooth, rounded head that fits into the glenoid cavity of the scapula to form the shoulder joint. Just below the head, the humerus narrows into the anatomical neck; a little further down sits the surgical neck, the most common fracture site in the upper humerus, especially in older adults with osteoporosis. Two bumps near the proximal end — the greater tubercle and lesser tubercle — serve as muscle attachment points, with the long groove between them (the intertubercular groove) acting as a track for one of the biceps tendons.
At its distal (lower) end, the humerus widens again to form the elbow joint. Two prominent bony bumps on either side of the lower humerus — the medial epicondyle and the lateral epicondyle — serve as muscle attachment sites. (Now you know what uses the medial epicondyle you found in your very first drawing session in Lesson 3.01: the muscles of the forearm.) The smooth articulating surfaces of the humerus’s distal end — the trochlea and the capitulum — meet the two forearm bones at the elbow joint. We will look at the elbow joint and the forearm bones in Lesson 3.08.
▶  Self-check: name the two shoulder girdle bones and the single upper-arm bone before clicking
Shoulder girdle: clavicle (collarbone) + scapula (shoulder blade), per side. Upper arm: humerus (one bone). The humerus head + scapula’s glenoid cavity = shoulder joint. The humerus distal end + radius + ulna = elbow joint (covered next lesson).
