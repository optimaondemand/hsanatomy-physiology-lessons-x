The Forearm, Wrist, and Hand
Reading
📖 READING
The forearm — two bones, two motions
Anatomically, the “arm” is just the upper portion — from shoulder to elbow — and the segment from elbow to wrist is called the forearm. The forearm has two parallel long bones running side by side: the radius on the lateral (thumb) side, and the ulna on the medial (little-finger) side. Each bone is shaped slightly differently and serves a slightly different purpose.
Figure — The forearm: radius and ulna, anterior and posterior views, with the olecranon and both styloid processes labeled.
The ulna is the larger of the two at the elbow end and the smaller at the wrist end. It is the main bone of the elbow joint: a deep, hook-shaped notch at its proximal end (the trochlear notch) wraps around the trochlea of the humerus, forming a hinge. The bony bump you can feel at the back of your elbow — the olecranon — is the proximal tip of the ulna. The ulna is what makes the elbow stable. At its distal (wrist) end, the ulna narrows to a small bony point called the styloid process — you can feel it as the bump on the little-finger side of your wrist.
The radius is the smaller of the two at the elbow and the larger at the wrist. Its disc-shaped head articulates with the capitulum of the humerus — and, crucially, the head of the radius can rotate in place as the forearm pronates and supinates (turns palm-down or palm-up). At the wrist end, the radius is the bone that actually meets most of the carpal bones to form the wrist joint, and it too ends in its own styloid process — the bump you can feel on the thumb side of your wrist. So the ulna anchors the elbow; the radius anchors the wrist. They turn around each other in between, and each one signs off at the wrist with a styloid process of its own.
When you turn a doorknob, you are pronating and supinating your forearm. The radius is sweeping around the ulna while the ulna stays roughly still. This is one of the most distinctive movements in the human body and one reason humans are so versatile at tool use.
The wrist — eight short bones
The wrist contains eight carpal bones arranged in two rows of four. The proximal row (closer to the forearm), from lateral to medial, is scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum, and pisiform. The distal row (closer to the fingers), from lateral to medial, is trapezium, trapezoid, capitate, and hamate. The easiest way to hold these in memory is by row, not as one long list: learn the four proximal bones as a set (scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum, pisiform), then the four distal bones as a second set (trapezium, trapezoid, capitate, hamate). Naming them lateral-to-medial, row by row, mirrors how they actually sit in the wrist — the copywork-and-recitation activity in this lesson uses exactly this grouping.
Figure — The hand, anterior and posterior views, with all eight carpal bones named in their proximal and distal rows.
These eight short bones do not move much individually, but the small movements between them add up: together they let the wrist tilt, twist, and roll in directions that no single joint could. The scaphoid is the most commonly fractured carpal bone — it sits at the base of the thumb and takes the impact when you fall forward onto an outstretched hand.
Through the front of the wrist runs a narrow tunnel called the carpal tunnel, formed by an arch of carpal bones on three sides and a tough ligament (the flexor retinaculum) closing the fourth. Tendons that flex the fingers pass through this tunnel, along with the median nerve. When the soft tissue around these tendons swells — from repetitive motion, pregnancy, arthritis, or other causes — the median nerve gets squeezed. The result is the familiar tingling, weakness, and pain of carpal tunnel syndrome.
Figure — Carpal tunnel syndrome: the median nerve compressed within the carpal tunnel, and the resulting area of numbness.
The hand — 5 metacarpals + 14 phalanges
The palm of the hand contains five metacarpal bones, numbered 1 through 5 from thumb to little finger. They are long bones, each running from a wrist (carpal) bone at one end to a finger joint at the other. The lumps you can feel as your knuckles are the heads of the metacarpals.
Figure — The metacarpals (numbered 1–5) and the proximal, middle, and distal phalanges of each finger.
Each finger contains phalanges. Fingers 2 through 5 (index through little) each have three phalanges: proximal, middle, and distal. The thumb has only two: proximal and distal. That gives a total of 14 phalanges per hand (3+3+3+3+2).
Counting all the way through: humerus (1) + radius (1) + ulna (1) + carpals (8) + metacarpals (5) + phalanges (14) = 30 bones per upper limb. Each hand and wrist contains 27 of them. Two hands and two wrists = 54 bones — just over a quarter of the entire human skeleton. The level of detail in our hands is the bony foundation of every tool, every instrument, and every art form humans have ever invented.
A note on the thumb — the opposable digit
The first metacarpal (the thumb’s metacarpal) meets its carpal (the trapezium) at a special joint called a saddle joint. The saddle shape lets the thumb move in two perpendicular planes — not just back and forth, but also across the palm to touch the tips of the other fingers. This is what makes the human thumb opposable. Most other primates have some thumb opposition, but the human range is unusually wide. The pen you write with, the cup you hold, and the tools you operate all depend on a saddle joint at the base of the thumb.
🔮 CURRENT RESEARCH — HIGH-RESOLUTION ULTRASOUND AND 3D-PRINTED SPLINTS
Carpal tunnel syndrome was historically diagnosed by symptoms plus nerve-conduction studies (which can be painful and not always conclusive). Treatment relied on off-the-shelf wrist splints that fit poorly and were often abandoned by the patient before they could do much good.

Two recent shifts in technology are changing both ends of this. On the diagnostic side, scientists and clinicians are exploring high-resolution ultrasound imaging of the median nerve, which can directly visualize nerve compression and swelling inside the carpal tunnel — sometimes faster, cheaper, and more comfortably than nerve-conduction testing. On the treatment side, several research groups are exploring 3D-printed custom splints made from scans of the patient’s own wrist. Early reports suggest these splints fit better and are worn more consistently, but whether they outperform standard splints in the long term is still being studied. Both technologies are examples of a broader trend: making anatomical care more specific to the individual rather than averaged across a population.
