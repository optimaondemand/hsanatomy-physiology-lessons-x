The Thorax — Sternum, Ribs, and Costal Cartilages
Reading
📖 READING
The rib cage — a bony chamber that has to breathe
The thoracic cage, or rib cage, is the bony chamber that surrounds the heart and the lungs. It is an architectural puzzle: it has to be protective enough to shield delicate organs from blows, yet flexible enough to expand and contract twelve to twenty times per minute, every minute, for the entire life of the person inside it. A rigid box would suffocate you. A purely flexible cage would offer no protection. The human thoracic cage solves this by mixing bone with cartilage.
All in, the thoracic cage contains 25 bones: 12 pairs of ribs (24 total) plus the sternum. Cartilage between ribs and sternum supplies the elasticity. We will take each piece in turn.
The sternum — the breastbone
Figure — The sternum (manubrium, body, xiphoid process), and its place in the thoracic cage.
The sternum is a single flat bone in the center of the front of the chest. Run your finger straight down the midline of your chest from the base of your throat — the hard bony plate you feel is the sternum. It has three parts: the manubrium at the top (which articulates with the clavicles and the first pair of ribs), the long body in the middle (which articulates with ribs 2 through 7), and the small xiphoid process at the bottom (a downward-pointing piece of cartilage early in life that ossifies to bone by middle age).
There is a useful surface landmark here. The sternal angle is the slight bony ridge where the manubrium meets the body of the sternum — you can feel it about an inch and a half below the top of the breastbone. The sternal angle aligns with the second pair of ribs, which makes it the standard landmark medical professionals use to count ribs. (You cannot easily feel the first rib; it is hidden under the clavicle.)
The sternum also has a clinical role you may have heard of: CPR compressions are delivered on the body of the sternum, pressing down on the heart that sits behind it. The xiphoid process is deliberately avoided in CPR because it is small and can break off.
The twelve pairs of ribs
A human has twelve pairs of ribs — one rib on each side per thoracic vertebra. The ribs are long, flat, curved bones. Every rib attaches posteriorly to a thoracic vertebra and curves forward around the body, but how each rib attaches anteriorly varies, and the variation is the key to how the cage breathes. Ribs are grouped into three categories.
Figure — True ribs (1–7), false ribs (8–10), and floating ribs (11–12), and how each attaches at the sternum.
True ribs (1–7)
The first seven pairs of ribs are called true ribs. Each one has its own piece of costal cartilage that attaches it directly to the sternum. (“Costal” just means “rib” in Latin.) The first rib’s costal cartilage attaches to the manubrium; the second through seventh attach to the body of the sternum. Because of these direct connections, the true ribs are the most stable.
False ribs (8–10)
The next three pairs, ribs 8 through 10, are called false ribs. Their costal cartilages do not reach the sternum directly. Instead, they join the costal cartilage of rib 7 above, forming a curved arch along each side of the lower thorax (the costal margin, which you can feel along the bottom edge of your rib cage).
Floating ribs (11–12)
The last two pairs, ribs 11 and 12, are called floating ribs. They have only short tips of cartilage and do not attach to the sternum or to other costal cartilages at all. They float free anteriorly, anchored only at the back (posteriorly) to their thoracic vertebrae. The floating ribs are short and tucked beneath the back muscles. Their main function is to protect the upper part of each kidney.
Why cartilage matters here
If the ribs attached directly to the sternum with bone, the thoracic cage could not expand. Cartilage is what makes breathing possible. When you inhale, your diaphragm contracts and pulls downward, your external intercostal muscles (between the ribs) contract and pull the rib cage upward and outward, and the entire bony cage moves like the handle of a bucket lifted at one end. The costal cartilages flex slightly to accommodate the motion. Then everything relaxes, the cartilages spring back, and you exhale. Every breath you have ever taken has depended on these strips of cartilage allowing the rib cage to flex without breaking. Cartilage hardens slightly with age, which is one reason older adults have shallower breathing capacity than children.
We will return to the mechanics of breathing in Module 9. For now, hold this thought: the architecture of the thorax is a compromise between protection and motion, and cartilage is the compromise.
