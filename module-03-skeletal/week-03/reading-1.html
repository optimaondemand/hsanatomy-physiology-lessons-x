The Pelvic Girdle and the Femur
Reading
📖 READING
A girdle built for weight, not reach
In the upper limb, the shoulder girdle was built for mobility. The lower limb solves a different problem. Walking, standing, and running all require that the upper body’s weight be transferred efficiently and stably into the ground — through the pelvis, the legs, and the feet. The lower limb is built first for stability, second for movement. Every architectural choice in the pelvis and femur reflects that priority.
The pelvic girdle is the bony ring that connects the lower limbs to the axial skeleton. Unlike the shoulder girdle — held to the trunk by a single small clavicular joint per side — the pelvic girdle is firmly fused to the sacrum at two sacroiliac joints, and the two halves of the pelvis meet each other in front at a strong cartilage joint (the pubic symphysis, sym-, syn- = together, joined). The result is a bony bowl that does not flex meaningfully under load. The pelvis is the spine’s stable platform.
Figure — The pelvic girdle: the two hip bones, joined to the sacrum, forming a stable bony ring.
The os coxa — three bones in one
Each half of the pelvic girdle is a single bone called the os coxa (also called the hip bone or coxal bone). One os coxa on each side, left and right. Together with the sacrum and coccyx, they form the bony pelvis.
Figure — The right hip bone, lateral and medial views, with the ilium, ischium, and pubis—the three bones that fuse to form it—labeled.
Each os coxa is itself a fusion of three smaller bones that begin life separately and fuse together in adolescence: the ilium, the ischium, and the pubis. The fused product looks like a single bone in an adult, but you can still see the boundaries where they came together — particularly inside a bony socket on the lateral surface called the acetabulum, which is the hip joint’s socket. Each of the three contributing bones contributes a portion of the acetabulum.
The ilium is the largest of the three — the broad, fan-shaped upper part. The iliac crest — the top edge of the ilium — is the bony ridge you can rest your hands on when you put them “on your hips.” The ischium is the lower-back portion. The pair of bony bumps you sit on (your sit bones, technically the ischial tuberosities) are part of the ischium — literally the bones that bear your weight when you sit. The pubis is the small front portion. The left and right pubic bones meet in the midline at the pubic symphysis.
A note on pelvic shape
Pelvic anatomy differs in shape between males and females, with female pelves typically broader and shallower in the central opening (pelvic inlet and pelvic outlet). This is the principal architectural accommodation for childbirth in the human skeleton. Beyond that adaptation, the bones are functionally the same: the same three contributing bones, the same acetabulum, the same surfaces for muscle attachment. This module’s figure uses the male skeleton (per course convention from Module 3 onward), but the bones you are studying are present in both.
The acetabulum — a deep socket
The acetabulum is the hip joint’s socket. Unlike the shallow glenoid cavity of the scapula, the acetabulum is deep: more than a third of the head of the femur fits inside it, surrounded by a tough fibrous lip (the labrum, labrum = lip, rim, edge) and held in place by powerful ligaments. The depth of the acetabulum is precisely why the hip is so much more stable than the shoulder. The shoulder gives up stability for mobility; the hip gives up some mobility for stability. The trade-off is the same one we mentioned in Lesson 3.07, viewed from the other side.
Hip dislocations do happen, but they require considerable force — a car accident, a serious sports injury, or a fall from height. Shoulder dislocations, by contrast, can occur from a hard throw or an awkward arm position. The architectural difference is the depth of the socket.
The femur — the strongest bone
The femur is the long bone of the thigh and the single largest bone in the human body. By weight, by length, and by compressive strength it is the most substantial bone you have. An average adult femur is approximately 48 cm long (about 19 inches) and can support roughly 30 times an adult’s body weight in vertical compression — far more than is needed for normal activity, and a strong margin for sudden loads like running and jumping.
Figure — The femur, anterior and posterior views, with the head, neck, greater/lesser trochanters, and condyles labeled.
At its proximal (upper) end, the femur has a smooth, rounded head that fits into the acetabulum to form the hip joint. The head sits on a short, angled neck — an anatomical detail that matters because the neck of the femur is the bone’s narrowest, most fragile section. Hip fractures in older adults with osteoporosis are usually fractures of the femoral neck, not the shaft. Below the neck the femur widens at the greater trochanter (the lateral bump you can feel on the outside of your hip) and the lesser trochanter (a smaller bump on the medial side, not externally palpable).
The long shaft (diaphysis) of the femur runs slightly medially as it descends — the femur is angled inward from hip to knee, which is why the knees of a standing person are closer together than the hips. At the distal (lower) end, the femur widens into the medial condyle and lateral condyle, two large bony surfaces that articulate with the tibia at the knee joint. Between the two condyles, on the front, is a smooth groove that holds the patella — the kneecap — which is a small bone that sits inside the tendon of the quadriceps muscle. (The patella is technically a sesamoid bone — a bone that develops inside a tendon — and it is the largest one in the body.)
▶  Self-check: name the three bones that fuse to form an os coxa before clicking
Ilium (largest, broad upper portion), ischium (sit-bone portion), and pubis (small front portion, meets at the pubic symphysis). All three contribute to the acetabulum — the deep socket where the femoral head fits to form the hip joint.
