The tibia and fibula — the bones of the lower leg
Below the knee, the lower leg has two long bones that run side by side, much like the radius and ulna of the forearm. But the parallel ends there. The lower leg’s two bones share weight-bearing very unequally, because the lower leg does not need to rotate the way the forearm does.
The tibia is the large, weight-bearing bone of the lower leg — the “shin bone.” It is the second-largest bone in the body after the femur and the strongest below the femur. The tibia’s broad proximal end has two large flat surfaces (the medial and lateral condyles of the tibia) that meet the corresponding condyles of the femur to form the knee joint. At the distal end, the tibia ends in a bony bump on the inner side of your ankle called the medial malleolus — the lump you can feel on the inside of your ankle when you take off a sock. The medial malleolus is what makes your ankle bumpy on the inner side.
Figure — The tibia and fibula, anterior and posterior views, with the condyles, tibial tuberosity, and both malleoli labeled.
The fibula is the smaller, lateral bone of the lower leg. It runs parallel to the tibia but is much thinner and carries very little body weight — roughly 10% of the load through the lower leg goes through the fibula in normal standing. Its main job is to provide muscle attachment surfaces and to form the lateral wall of the ankle joint. The bony bump on the outside of your ankle — the lateral malleolus — is the distal end of the fibula. The fibula does not participate in the knee joint.
A note on the patella
The patella — the kneecap — sits in front of the knee joint, riding in a smooth groove on the front of the femur. The patella is technically a sesamoid bone, a bone that develops inside a tendon: in this case, the tendon of the quadriceps muscle (which you will meet in Module 4). The patella protects the front of the knee, improves the angle at which the quadriceps pulls on the tibia, and is the only bone in the body that develops in this unusual way and grows to a clinically significant size.
The ankle — seven tarsals
Just as the wrist has eight short bones, the ankle has seven tarsal bones. Unlike the wrist’s carpals, however, the tarsals are not arranged in two neat rows of four — they form a more complex grouping that supports body weight when standing.
The seven tarsals are: calcaneus, talus, cuboid, navicular, and three cuneiforms (medial, intermediate, lateral). Two of these matter most for our purposes. The talus is the tarsal that sits at the top of the foot, between the medial and lateral malleoli, and forms the joint with the tibia at the ankle (the talocrural joint). The calcaneus is the largest tarsal — the heel bone — that bears the impact of every footstep and serves as the attachment point for the Achilles tendon at the back of the foot.
Figure — The bones of the foot, medial, lateral, and superior views, with the talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, and three cuneiforms labeled.
The foot — metatarsals and phalanges
The architecture of the foot mirrors the hand — with five metatarsals running through the arch of the foot and 14 phalanges in the toes — but with crucial structural differences. The five metatarsals are numbered 1 through 5 from the big toe to the little toe. The first metatarsal (big toe) is thicker than the others because it carries more weight during walking.
The toes are made of 14 phalanges: the big toe (first toe) has 2 (proximal and distal), and toes 2 through 5 each have 3 (proximal, middle, distal). This is the same numbering pattern as the fingers, but the toes are shorter and the joints between them are less flexible. The toes are not built for grasping — they are built for the final push-off of each step.
Counting through: tibia (1) + fibula (1) + patella (1) + tarsals (7) + metatarsals (5) + phalanges (14) = 29 bones per lower limb below the knee. With the femur (1) added, the entire lower limb has 30 bones, just like the upper limb — though the architecture and proportions are entirely different.
The arches of the foot
The bones of the foot are not arranged flat. They form three arches — the medial longitudinal arch (running along the inside of the foot, from heel to big toe), the lateral longitudinal arch (along the outer side, lower than the medial), and the transverse arch (running across the foot near the ball). The arches act like a spring: they compress slightly when you put weight on the foot, then rebound as you push off. This is the foot’s primary shock-absorbing mechanism, and it is one reason most adults can walk comfortably for hours. People with flat feet (collapsed arches) often experience foot fatigue and pain because the spring mechanism is reduced.
▶  Self-check: name the two malleoli and the bones they belong to before clicking
Medial malleolus: distal end of the tibia (inside of the ankle). Lateral malleolus: distal end of the fibula (outside of the ankle). Together they form the bony “mortise” that holds the talus.
