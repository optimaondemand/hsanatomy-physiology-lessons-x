Joints — Where Bones Meet
Reading
📖 READING
What is a joint?
A joint — technically an articulation — is any place where two bones meet. The skeleton is, in a sense, just a collection of bones organized by their joints: nothing is welded. Even the bones that look fused (sutures of the skull, the sacrum) are technically joints, just immobile ones.
Figure — Joints classified by the shape and motion of their articulating surfaces.
Joints are classified two ways (arth- = joint — you will see this root in every joint name from here on). Functionally, they are grouped by how much they move: synarthroses (immovable), amphiarthroses (slightly movable), and diarthroses (freely movable). Structurally, they are grouped by what holds them together: fibrous joints (connected by dense fibrous connective tissue), cartilaginous joints (connected by cartilage), and synovial joints (connected by a fluid-filled capsule). The two classifications often line up: most fibrous joints are synarthroses; most cartilaginous joints are amphiarthroses; all synovial joints are diarthroses.
Fibrous joints — the immobile kind
A fibrous joint is held together by dense fibrous connective tissue, with no joint cavity. The three types you should know are sutures (between cranial bones — almost completely immobile in adults), gomphoses (the joints holding each tooth in its socket — technically a joint, even though the “bone” is really a tooth), and syndesmoses (where two bones are held side by side by a sheet of fibrous tissue, like the distal end of the radius-ulna pair or the distal tibia-fibula). Syndesmoses allow some movement, but the others do not.
Figure — Fibrous joints: (a) a suture, (b) a syndesmosis, (c) a gomphosis.
Cartilaginous joints — the slightly mobile kind
A cartilaginous joint is held together by cartilage, with no fluid cavity. Synchondroses (chondro- = cartilage) use hyaline cartilage and are usually temporary — for example, the growth plates in long bones during childhood. Symphyses use fibrocartilage and are permanent. The pubic symphysis (between the two halves of the pelvis) and the intervertebral discs (between vertebrae) are symphyses. Both allow slight motion, but no joint cavity, and limited range.
Figure — Cartilaginous joints: (a) a synchondrosis (the epiphyseal plate), (b) a symphysis (the pubic symphysis).
Synovial joints — freely movable
A synovial joint is the kind most people picture when they think of a joint: the knee, the shoulder, the hip, the elbow, the wrist, the knuckles. All synovial joints share the same basic architecture, with six features:
Figure — A synovial joint: articular cartilage, joint cavity, articular capsule, and synovial membrane.
1. Articular cartilage — a smooth layer of hyaline cartilage covering the ends of the bones where they meet. Articular cartilage is glassy-smooth and slippery, with a very low coefficient of friction.
2. Joint cavity — a small space between the bones, sealed off from surrounding tissues.
3. Articular capsule — a fibrous sleeve surrounding the joint, with an inner synovial membrane that lines the cavity.
4. Synovial fluid — a viscous, egg-white-like fluid produced by the synovial membrane. It lubricates the cartilage surfaces, delivers nutrients to the cartilage (which has no blood supply of its own), and absorbs shock.
5. Ligaments — tough cords of dense connective tissue running from one bone to the other, holding the joint together while allowing motion. Some ligaments are inside the capsule (like the cruciate ligaments inside the knee), and some are outside (like the medial and lateral collateral ligaments of the knee).
6. Sensory nerves — that report on joint position and pain. (You will meet these in detail in Module 5.)
Some synovial joints have additional features: menisci (fibrocartilage pads inside the joint, like the C-shaped menisci of the knee that distribute load), bursae (small fluid-filled cushions that reduce friction between tendons and bones), and fat pads (cushioning at the joint margins).
Figure — The knee joint’s bursae (suprapatellar, prepatellar, infrapatellar) and its cruciate ligaments.

📑 VOCABULARY — THE MOVEMENTS JOINTS ALLOW
Before you read the six joint types below, learn the names of the motions they produce. You will use these terms constantly this year, and again when you name specific bone-to-bone joints in this week’s activities. Gliding (flat surfaces sliding past each other), flexion / extension (decreasing / increasing the angle of a joint), abduction / adduction (moving a limb away from / toward the midline), circumduction (tracing a cone shape, combining several motions), rotation (turning around a long axis), elevation / depression (moving up / down, as at the shoulder girdle), pronation / supination (rotating the forearm palm-down / palm-up), dorsiflexion / plantarflexion (pulling the foot up / pointing it down), and opposition / reposition (touching the thumb to a fingertip, and returning it).
Figure — The six types of synovial joint, located on the skeleton: pivot, hinge, saddle, plane, condyloid, and ball-and-socket.
Plane (gliding) joints — flat or slightly curved surfaces that slide past each other. Limited motion. Example: between two carpal bones.
Hinge joints — one bone’s convex surface fits a concave one. Motion in one plane only. Example: the elbow, where the trochlea of the humerus fits the trochlear notch of the ulna.
Pivot joints — one bone rotates inside a ring of bone and ligament. Example: the joint between C1 (atlas) and C2 (axis) that lets you turn your head “no.”
Condyloid (ellipsoidal) joints — an oval-shaped convex surface fits an oval-shaped concave surface. Motion in two planes. Example: the wrist (radiocarpal joint).
Saddle joints — both surfaces are curved like a saddle, allowing motion in two perpendicular planes with greater range than a condyloid joint. Example: the joint at the base of the thumb (between the trapezium and the first metacarpal).
Ball-and-socket joints — a spherical bone head fits inside a cup-shaped socket. Most mobile of all: motion in all three planes plus rotation. Examples: the shoulder (humerus + glenoid cavity) and the hip (femur + acetabulum). You already know the trade-off: shallow socket = high mobility, low stability (shoulder); deep socket = lower mobility, high stability (hip).
🔮 WHEN A HINGE WEARS OUT — JOINT REPLACEMENT
The knee — a hinge joint — is one of the most common sites of osteoarthritis (osteo- = bone, arth- = joint, -itis = inflammation), a condition in which the articular cartilage covering the ends of the femur and tibia gradually wears down until bone can grind directly against bone. Almost everyone has heard the phrase “knee replacement,” but few people know what actually happens in one. In a total knee replacement, a surgeon does not remove the whole bone — only the damaged cartilage and a thin layer of the underlying bone at the end of the femur and the top of the tibia (and often the back of the patella) are resurfaced with smooth metal and durable plastic components, which recreate the hinge joint’s articulating surfaces without the worn cartilage that caused the pain. The ligaments, in most designs, are left in place to preserve normal joint stability. It is a resurfacing, not a replacement of the whole limb — a distinction that matters as you think about hinge joints as an engineered system with replaceable wear parts.
🔮 CURRENT RESEARCH — CAN CARTILAGE BE REGROWN?
Articular cartilage has a serious problem: it does not have its own blood supply. Cartilage cells (chondrocytes) get their nutrients by diffusion from synovial fluid — which works well for maintenance but very poorly for repair. When articular cartilage is damaged by injury or worn down by osteoarthritis, the body cannot meaningfully regrow it. The only long-term clinical option for severe wear has been to replace the joint surgically with metal and plastic.

Scientists are currently exploring several approaches to regrowing articular cartilage: harvesting healthy cartilage cells from elsewhere in the patient’s body and culturing them on a scaffold; using stem cells to coax growth of new cartilage in place; and engineering hybrid scaffolds that mimic the layered structure of native cartilage. Some of these approaches are in early clinical use for focal cartilage defects in younger patients, but treating widespread osteoarthritis in older adults remains hard. The big challenge is reproducing cartilage’s unique combination of strength, slipperiness, and load distribution — properties that emerge from how the tissue is structured at a microscopic level.
