Where the adrenal glands sit
The adrenal glands (also called the suprarenal glands) are two small triangular caps that sit on top of the kidneys. They take their name straight from anatomy: ad- means “upon,” renal means kidney. Each adrenal weighs only about 5 grams in a healthy adult, and the two glands together are smaller than a single thumb. Despite their size, they are responsible for one of the most powerful endocrine outputs in the body.
Each adrenal gland is really two different organs squeezed inside a shared capsule. They have completely different embryological origins, completely different products, and completely different control systems. The only thing they share is their address.
The outer layer is the adrenal cortex. It makes up about 80% of the gland by volume. It is built from epithelial-like cells that produce steroid hormones from cholesterol. The cortex is under hypothalamic-pituitary control, just like the thyroid you met last week.
The inner core is the adrenal medulla. It is the modified sympathetic ganglion you compared in your drawing last week — cells called chromaffin cells that act like postganglionic sympathetic neurons that have given up their axons. When a sympathetic preganglionic fiber fires onto them, they dump catecholamines (epinephrine and norepinephrine) directly into the bloodstream. The medulla is under direct nervous-system control. There is no pituitary in this loop — the brain signals it through nerves, not hormones.
The adrenal cortex: three zones, three families of hormones
Under a microscope, the adrenal cortex falls into three concentric layers, each of which makes its own family of steroid hormones. The classic mnemonic is “Salt, Sugar, Sex — the deeper you go, the sweeter it gets.”
Zona glomerulosa — salt (mineralocorticoids)
The outermost zone makes aldosterone, the body’s master mineralocorticoid. Aldosterone acts on the kidney to retain sodium and excrete potassium. Where sodium goes, water follows, so aldosterone effectively expands blood volume and raises blood pressure. You will see this hormone again in Module 11 when you study the kidney; for now, hold onto the basic principle — aldosterone is the body’s salt-keeper.
Aldosterone is regulated mostly through the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), a feedback loop that begins in the kidney when blood pressure drops. The hypothalamus and pituitary are only marginally involved — the adrenal cortex’s zona glomerulosa is one of the few endocrine tissues that is not primarily driven by ACTH.
Zona fasciculata — sugar (glucocorticoids)
The thick middle zone makes cortisol, the body’s master glucocorticoid. Cortisol is the slow side of the stress response — the hormone the body releases when stress is going to last more than a few minutes. Its job is to keep fuel available for sustained effort. Specifically, cortisol:
Raises blood glucose by stimulating gluconeogenesis (the liver builds new glucose from amino acids) and by reducing how much glucose other tissues take up. Mobilizes amino acids by breaking down muscle protein, providing raw material for gluconeogenesis. Mobilizes fat by stimulating lipolysis. Dampens inflammation by inhibiting immune cell activity — which is why synthetic cortisol (prednisone, hydrocortisone) is given for autoimmune disease, asthma, and allergic reactions.
Cortisol is regulated by the HPA axis you met in Reading 6.03: hypothalamus releases CRH, pituitary releases ACTH, adrenal cortex releases cortisol, and cortisol feeds back to suppress both CRH and ACTH. The system has a distinct daily rhythm: cortisol is highest in the early morning (helping you wake up and mobilize fuel) and lowest around midnight.
Zona reticularis — sex (androgens)
The innermost cortical zone makes weak adrenal androgens — primarily DHEA (dehydroepiandrosterone). In adults these have modest effects: in men they are dwarfed by testicular testosterone; in women they are one of the body’s main sources of androgen and contribute to muscle mass, libido, and the development of pubic and axillary hair at puberty. You will return to this zone briefly in Module 12 when you study reproductive hormones.
💡 HANS SELYE AND THE GENERAL ADAPTATION SYNDROME
In the 1930s an Austrian-Canadian endocrinologist named Hans Selye (1907–1982) noticed something strange in his lab. He had been injecting rats with ovarian extracts to study the effect on reproductive organs, but the rats in his control group — injected with saline, no hormones — were developing some of the same problems: enlarged adrenal glands, ulcers in the gut, shrunken lymphatic tissue. Selye realized that the rats were responding not to the chemistry of the injection but to the stress of being repeatedly handled, injected, and disturbed.

He named this body-wide response the general adaptation syndrome and described it in three stages: alarm (the fight-or-flight surge from the adrenal medulla); resistance (sustained cortisol from the adrenal cortex, keeping fuel available); and exhaustion (if stress is prolonged, the adrenal cortex and other body systems eventually fail to keep up). The term “stress” as we use it today — a generalized biological state — was Selye’s invention. He borrowed the word from engineering, where it referred to forces acting on materials, and applied it to the body.

Selye’s framework is now considered too simple in its details, but his core insight was right: very different kinds of threats — cold, infection, surgery, emotional upset — produce a recognizable, stereotyped endocrine response, with the adrenal glands at its center. Every time you hear someone describe a person as “stressed,” you are using language Selye gave us.

The adrenal medulla: fast hormones for fast emergencies
The adrenal medulla’s chromaffin cells release two hormones in response to sympathetic stimulation: epinephrine (about 80% of the output) and norepinephrine (about 20%). These are the same molecules used as neurotransmitters at sympathetic synapses elsewhere in the body — the difference is that when they come from the medulla they enter the bloodstream and reach every cell in the body, not just the cells of one organ.
Their effects are the textbook fight-or-flight changes: heart rate up, contractility up, vessels in the gut constrict, vessels in skeletal muscle dilate, pupils dilate, airway smooth muscle relaxes, the liver dumps glucose into the blood, energy stores mobilize. Where sympathetic nerves take milliseconds to reach their direct targets, the medulla’s broadcast takes seconds to spread but reaches every tissue.
The two systems are layered. In the first few seconds of a sudden threat, sympathetic nerves dominate. In the next minute or two, the adrenal medulla’s hormones extend and broaden the response. Then, if the stress lasts longer than a few minutes, the slow side of the HPA axis — CRH, ACTH, cortisol — takes over to sustain it. Three time scales, two adrenal organs, one threat.

When the adrenals break down
Three clinical conditions make the adrenal cortex’s role unmistakable.
Cushing’s syndrome is the picture of chronic excess cortisol — whether from a pituitary tumor secreting ACTH (Cushing’s disease, named by Harvey Cushing himself, whom you met in Reading 6.03), an adrenal tumor, or prolonged treatment with synthetic steroids. The classic features — weight gain centered on the trunk and face, weakened muscle and skin, easy bruising, high blood sugar, high blood pressure — are exactly what chronic cortisol does. Cortisol is essential in normal amounts; in excess it dismantles the body it is supposed to support.
Addison’s disease is the opposite: chronic insufficiency of the adrenal cortex, usually from autoimmune destruction. Cortisol falls, aldosterone falls, blood sugar drops, blood pressure drops, sodium falls, potassium rises. Without treatment, patients live in a state of near-constant exhaustion, and a severe stressor (a major infection, surgery, an accident) can produce an “adrenal crisis” that is medically life-threatening. The treatment is straightforward in principle: replace cortisol with daily synthetic glucocorticoid and replace aldosterone when needed. President John F. Kennedy lived with Addison’s disease and managed it with daily hormone replacement throughout his presidency.
Pheochromocytoma is a rare tumor of the adrenal medulla’s chromaffin cells. It causes the body to be flooded with epinephrine and norepinephrine at unpredictable moments: pounding headaches, severe hypertension, sweating, palpitations, anxiety. It is one of the few endocrine conditions whose name is famously hard to spell, and one of the few causes of high blood pressure that can be cured completely by surgery.

Outermost: zona glomerulosa — aldosterone (mineralocorticoid). Keeps salt; raises blood pressure.
Middle: zona fasciculata — cortisol (glucocorticoid). Raises blood glucose; dampens inflammation; supports sustained stress.
Innermost: zona reticularis — adrenal androgens (DHEA). Modest in men, important in women.
Core: adrenal medulla — epinephrine + norepinephrine. Fast hormones; controlled by the sympathetic nervous system, not by ACTH.
