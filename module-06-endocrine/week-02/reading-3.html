Secondary Endocrine Organs — The Body's Other Hormone Producers
Reading
📖 READING
Hormones from organs that have other jobs
The classical endocrine glands — the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreatic islets — account for most of the hormones a textbook lists by name. But the body has many more chemical messengers than that, and most of them come from organs whose main job is something else entirely. The heart pumps blood and releases a hormone. The kidneys filter urine and release several hormones. The stomach digests food and releases hormones to coordinate the meal. The ovaries and testes make gametes and release powerful sex hormones.
Anatomists call these secondary endocrine organs: organs that are not categorized as part of the endocrine system but that nonetheless release at least one hormone as part of their daily function. The category is a useful reminder that endocrine signaling is not confined to the “glands of the endocrine system” the way nerve fibers are confined to the nervous system. The whole body talks to itself chemically. The classical endocrine glands are simply the parts of that conversation that have no other job to do.
This lesson walks you through the most important secondary endocrine organs. You will not need to memorize every hormone. The goal is to recognize that organs you have already studied (or will study) are also part of the endocrine conversation, and to see a few specific hormones that matter for understanding the body as a whole.
The heart
When the atria of the heart are stretched by too much blood volume, specialized cardiac muscle cells in their walls release atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) into the blood. ANP travels to the kidney and tells it to dump more sodium (and therefore water) into the urine. The result: lower blood volume, lower blood pressure. ANP is one of the body’s natural antagonists of aldosterone — aldosterone says “keep salt,” ANP says “dump salt.” The two work in tension to defend the right blood volume.
You will return to this hormone in Module 7 (cardiovascular) and Module 11 (urinary). For now, hold on to the fact that the heart is also an endocrine organ.
The kidneys
The kidneys release three hormones (or hormone-precursors) that matter for this course.
Erythropoietin (EPO) — when the kidney senses that blood oxygen is low (high altitude, anemia, chronic lung disease), it releases EPO. EPO travels to the red bone marrow and stimulates the production of more red blood cells. Over weeks, hemoglobin rises and oxygen carrying capacity increases. EPO is one of the more famous hormones of modern sports doping — injected EPO raises hematocrit and improves endurance, which is why it is banned in nearly every endurance sport.
Renin — technically an enzyme, not a hormone, but treated as part of an endocrine cascade. When blood pressure or sodium falls, the kidney releases renin into the blood. Renin starts a chain reaction that ultimately produces angiotensin II, a powerful vasoconstrictor that also tells the adrenal cortex to release aldosterone (which then tells the kidney to keep salt). This loop — the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, or RAAS — is the system many blood-pressure medications target. ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and direct renin inhibitors all interrupt it at different points.
Figure — The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS): kidney-released renin sets off a cascade ending in vasoconstriction and aldosterone-driven salt retention. (Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc.)
Calcitriol (active vitamin D) — you met this in Reading 6.04. Vitamin D from food or sunlight is processed in the liver and then activated by the kidney into calcitriol, the form that actually helps the gut absorb dietary calcium. The kidney is, in this sense, an essential partner in the body’s calcium regulation, even though we usually think of it as part of the urinary system.
The gastrointestinal tract
The lining of the stomach and small intestine contains specialized endocrine cells — the so-called enteroendocrine cells — that detect what is in the gut and release hormones into the bloodstream to coordinate digestion. Four of these GI hormones are worth meeting by name now; you will see all of them again in Module 10.
Gastrin is released from the stomach in response to a meal. It tells the stomach’s parietal cells to produce hydrochloric acid for digestion.
Secretin is released from the small intestine when acidic stomach contents arrive there. It tells the pancreas to release bicarbonate (to neutralize the acid) and tells the stomach to slow down acid production. Secretin, discovered in 1902, was the first chemical messenger ever identified as a “hormone” — that word was coined to describe exactly what secretin does.
Cholecystokinin (CCK) is released from the small intestine in response to fats and proteins. It tells the gallbladder to contract (releasing stored bile to emulsify fats) and tells the pancreas to release digestive enzymes. It also signals fullness to the brain.
GLP-1 — the one you met in the last reading’s contemporary-research callout — comes from L cells in the small intestine. It boosts insulin secretion when blood glucose rises after a meal, slows gastric emptying, and reduces appetite. GLP-1 has become one of the most clinically important hormones in medicine over the past decade.
Figure — GLP-1's reach beyond the gut: it acts on the brain, heart, pancreas, muscle, fat, and liver, which is part of why GLP-1-based drugs affect so much more than blood glucose alone.
The liver
The liver is principally a metabolic organ, but it also releases a handful of hormones. The most important for this course is insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1). When growth hormone from the pituitary reaches the liver, it stimulates IGF-1 production. IGF-1 is what actually does most of GH’s growth-promoting work on bone and other tissues. So when you read “growth hormone drives growth,” the chain is really GH → liver → IGF-1 → tissue growth.
The gonads
The ovaries and testes are both gamete-producing organs (their non-endocrine role) and major hormone producers (their endocrine role). The testes’ Leydig cells produce testosterone under the control of LH from the anterior pituitary. The ovaries’ follicular cells produce estrogen (especially estradiol) under the control of FSH, and after ovulation the corpus luteum produces progesterone under the control of LH.
All three are steroid hormones, derived from cholesterol, and they act on intracellular receptors to change gene expression in target tissues. Their effects shape the body across the lifespan: sexual development at puberty, the monthly cycle, fertility, pregnancy, the menopause transition, and the gradual decline of testosterone in older men. You will study all of this in detail in Module 12.
A bigger picture of hormone signaling
Adding it up: the heart, kidneys, GI tract, liver, gonads, plus a handful of less prominent secondary endocrine sites (skin makes vitamin D precursors, adipose tissue releases leptin to signal energy stores, the placenta during pregnancy is a major endocrine organ) — the body has dozens of cells and tissues that fire chemical messages into the bloodstream. The classical “endocrine system” is the part of the network whose only job is signaling. The rest of the body is doing other things and signaling on the side.
As you finish this module, hold this layered picture in your head. The endocrine system is not a discrete network like the nervous system. It is a property of the bloodstream itself — the way the body talks to itself wherever cells happen to be making and releasing messages. The classical glands are simply the parts that show up most reliably in textbook diagrams.
▶  Self-check: match the secondary endocrine organ to its hormone before clicking
Heart → ANP — tells the kidney to dump salt.
Kidney → EPO (makes red blood cells), renin (starts RAAS), and calcitriol (active vitamin D).
Stomach → gastrin — tells parietal cells to make acid.
Small intestine → secretin, CCK, GLP-1.
Liver → IGF-1 (mediates most of growth hormone’s effects).
Testes → testosterone.
Ovaries → estrogen and progesterone.

Notice the integration. The heart talks to the kidney. The kidney talks to the bone marrow, the adrenal cortex, the gut, the bone. The gut talks to the pancreas and the brain. The body’s “eleven organ systems” are a teaching convenience — the body itself does not respect the boundaries.
