Where the pancreas sits
The pancreas is a long, soft organ tucked into the upper abdomen behind the stomach. It looks like a flattened tadpole: a wide head nestled in the C-shaped curve of the duodenum, a long body crossing horizontally to the left, and a tapering tail that nearly reaches the spleen. In an adult it is about 15 cm (6 inches) long and weighs roughly 75 grams. You will see it again in Module 10, when you study its exocrine role in digestion. This week we are interested in its endocrine role — the tiny clusters of cells scattered through it that regulate blood sugar.
Figure — The pancreas in the upper abdomen (left), behind the stomach and against the duodenum, and a close-up of its head/body/tail and pancreatic duct, with an islet of Langerhans visible among the exocrine acinar cells (right).
The islets of Langerhans
Roughly 1 to 2 percent of the pancreas’s mass — about a million tiny islands of tissue — is endocrine. These islands are called the islets of Langerhans, after the German medical student who discovered them in 1869 (see callout below). The rest of the pancreas is exocrine: acinar cells that secrete digestive enzymes through a duct system into the duodenum. The endocrine cells release their hormones directly into capillaries that wind through each islet. The two systems live in the same organ but never share their products.
Each islet contains four cell types, but two of them carry the bulk of the work:
Alpha cells (α cells) — about 20% of an islet’s cells — secrete glucagon, the hormone that raises blood glucose.
Beta cells (β cells) — about 70% of an islet’s cells — secrete insulin, the hormone that lowers blood glucose.
A smaller fraction of cells (delta cells, PP cells) produce somatostatin and pancreatic polypeptide, which fine-tune the system. For this lesson, hold on to alpha-glucagon and beta-insulin — this is the see-saw at the heart of the body’s most-monitored hormone loop.
Figure — A single islet of Langerhans, enlarged, showing its cell types: alpha cells (glucagon), beta cells (insulin), delta cells (somatostatin), and the surrounding exocrine acinar cells.
💡 LANGERHANS’S ISLANDS, AND THE DISCOVERY OF INSULIN
In 1869, a 22-year-old German medical student named Paul Langerhans (1847–1888) was working on his doctoral thesis at the Pathological Institute in Berlin. Using a new staining technique on slices of pancreas, he noticed scattered through the tissue a series of small clumps of cells that looked different from the rest. He could not figure out what they did, but he described them carefully in his thesis. He called them simply “cell heaps.” Other anatomists started calling them the islands of Langerhans, and the name stuck. Langerhans himself died young, of tuberculosis, at age 41 — before anyone understood what his islands were for.

The function of those islands was solved in 1921 in a small lab at the University of Toronto. A young Canadian surgeon named Frederick Banting (1891–1941) and a medical student named Charles Best (1899–1978) tied off the ducts of dog pancreases to destroy the exocrine tissue, then extracted what remained from the surviving islets. The extract, injected into other dogs whose pancreases had been removed (making them severely diabetic), restored their blood sugar to normal. They named the substance “isletin,” later renamed insulin (Latin insula, “island”). Within a year, a 14-year-old boy named Leonard Thompson, dying of type 1 diabetes in a Toronto hospital, received the first human insulin injection. He lived another 13 years. Banting and his lab director J.J.R. Macleod received the Nobel Prize in 1923. Banting split his half of the prize money with Charles Best.
How insulin and glucagon keep blood sugar steady
Normal fasting blood glucose is about 70–100 mg/dL. After a meal it may rise to 140 mg/dL or so; between meals or overnight it stays in the lower part of the range. The body works hard to defend these numbers because the brain depends on a steady glucose supply — brain cells cannot store glucose, and they cannot easily switch to fat for fuel.
Figure — The insulin-glucagon see-saw: whichever direction blood glucose tips away from the normal range, the opposing hormone tips it back. (Body Scientific International, LLC.)
When blood sugar rises (after a meal)
Beta cells sense the rise in glucose and release insulin. Insulin then acts on three main target tissues:
Skeletal muscle and fat tissue insert more glucose transporters (GLUT4) into their cell membranes, pulling glucose out of the blood and into the cells. Liver takes up glucose and converts it to glycogen (the body’s short-term carbohydrate storage). Brain, red blood cells, and some other tissues use glucose continuously and do not require insulin to do so — insulin’s job is to redirect extra glucose away from the brain’s supply and into storage.
Within an hour or two, blood glucose has fallen back into range, and insulin secretion drops back to baseline.
When blood sugar falls (between meals, overnight, exercise)
Alpha cells sense the drop and release glucagon. Glucagon acts mainly on the liver, where it does two things: glycogenolysis (breaking glycogen back down into glucose, which the liver releases into the blood) and, if fasting continues, gluconeogenesis (building new glucose from amino acids and other precursors). Cortisol from the adrenal cortex helps with the slower gluconeogenesis side. Together these processes keep blood glucose from falling dangerously low during an overnight fast or a long run.
When the loop breaks: diabetes
Diabetes mellitus is the condition of chronically elevated blood glucose, and it comes in two distinct forms with the same end result.
Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune disease. The body’s own immune system attacks and destroys the beta cells of the pancreas. The result: no insulin, regardless of blood sugar. Type 1 typically appears in childhood or adolescence, comes on quickly, and is fatal without insulin replacement. Daily insulin injections (or continuous insulin pump delivery) keep type 1 diabetics alive. Before Banting and Best, type 1 was uniformly fatal.
Type 2 diabetes is more common (about 90% of all diabetes cases) and follows a different pathway. The beta cells still make insulin, but the body’s tissues become resistant to it — they do not respond well to the insulin signal. The beta cells try to compensate by making more, sometimes for years, until they eventually wear out. Type 2 is strongly linked to obesity, inactivity, and genetic predisposition. It usually appears in adulthood, but is now increasingly seen in adolescents, and it can sometimes be partly reversed with weight loss and exercise.
Either way, persistent high blood sugar damages the body over years: small blood vessels in the retina (diabetic retinopathy — a leading cause of blindness in adults), kidneys (diabetic nephropathy — a leading cause of kidney failure), peripheral nerves (diabetic neuropathy), and large arteries (accelerated atherosclerosis). The classic short-term symptoms — frequent urination, thirst, weight loss, fatigue — are downstream of the body trying to dump excess glucose into the urine.
✨ CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH: GLP-1 AGONISTS
When you eat, the cells of your small intestine release a hormone called GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) into the blood. GLP-1 reaches the pancreas and tells the beta cells to release more insulin in response to the rising glucose. It also reaches the brain and signals fullness, slowing how fast the stomach empties and reducing appetite. GLP-1 has a very short half-life in the body — just a few minutes — so its effect is brief.

In the early 2000s, researchers developed synthetic versions of GLP-1 that resist breakdown and last hours to days in the body. These drugs — GLP-1 receptor agonists, including liraglutide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide (which targets a related receptor as well) — were originally approved for type 2 diabetes. In clinical trials they lowered blood glucose effectively, but they also produced striking weight loss, often 15 percent or more of body weight in patients who took them long-term. Newer formulations approved specifically for obesity (and brand-name medications like Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, and Zepbound) have become some of the most-prescribed drugs in the United States. Researchers are now studying GLP-1 agonists for cardiovascular disease, addiction, fatty liver disease, and sleep apnea — not because the original hypothesis predicted those effects, but because the drugs keep producing benefits that the developers did not foresee. This is one of the most active areas in clinical endocrinology today.
A preview of what comes next
The pancreas is a perfect transition into the next lesson’s topic. It is a dual endocrine organ — an organ with a major non-endocrine function (digestion) that also makes important hormones. You will spend Lesson 6.07 walking through several other organs that fit this pattern: the heart, kidneys, gut, liver, and gonads. None of them is “part of the endocrine system” in the textbook sense, but each releases at least one hormone that contributes to the body’s coordination — and several (like GLP-1 from the small intestine) are now among the most clinically important hormones in medicine.
▶  Self-check: trace the loop in both directions before clicking
You just ate a sandwich. Blood glucose rises → beta cells release insulin → muscle and fat take up glucose, liver stores it as glycogen → blood glucose returns to normal → insulin drops back to baseline.

You skipped breakfast and have been at school for 4 hours. Blood glucose falls → alpha cells release glucagon → liver breaks down glycogen and releases glucose; if fasting continues, gluconeogenesis begins → blood glucose returns to normal → glucagon drops back to baseline.

Notice the structural difference: insulin acts on many tissues (muscle, fat, liver), glucagon acts mainly on the liver. The body has many places to store glucose; it has one main place to make it.
