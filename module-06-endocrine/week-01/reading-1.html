Endocrine vs. Exocrine: What Is a Hormone?

📖 READING
Two ways the body talks to itself
In Module 5 you traced the body’s fast communication system — nerves carrying electrical signals at meters per second, delivered point-to-point to a single muscle fiber or a single glandular cell. The endocrine system is the body’s slow communication system. Instead of wires, it uses the bloodstream. Instead of an electrical pulse, it uses a chemical messenger. A signal that leaves an endocrine gland may travel for seconds or minutes before it arrives at its target, but when it arrives, it can change the behavior of millions of cells across the body at the same time.
Both systems are necessary. A signal that has to act in 200 milliseconds — the withdrawal of a hand from a hot pan — cannot wait for the bloodstream. A signal that has to coordinate hundreds of organs in the same direction for hours — the growth of a thirteen-year-old, the daily rhythm of waking and sleeping, the female monthly cycle — cannot be carried on a single nerve. The body uses nerves where speed matters and hormones where breadth and duration matter, and at a few important places it uses both at once.
Endocrine vs. exocrine glands
A gland is any group of cells specialized to make and release a chemical product. The body has two organizational schemes for glands, distinguished by where the product goes.
Exocrine glands release their product through a duct — a hollow tube — onto a surface. Your salivary glands deposit saliva into the mouth through three pairs of ducts. Sweat glands open onto the skin. The pancreas’s digestive enzymes empty into the duodenum through the pancreatic duct. The product reaches its destination by flowing through a tube to a surface.
Endocrine glands have no ducts. They release their product — a hormone — directly into the surrounding tissue fluid, where it diffuses into the bloodstream. The blood then carries the hormone to every part of the body. The Greek prefixes capture the distinction perfectly: endo- means “within,” and exo- means “outside.” Endocrine glands secrete inward to the blood; exocrine glands secrete outward through ducts.
Figure — An exocrine gland (left) releases its product through a duct onto a surface. An endocrine gland (right) has no duct; its hormones diffuse directly into the surrounding capillaries.
A few organs are both. The pancreas is the most important example: its acinar cells are exocrine (digestive enzymes through the pancreatic duct), but scattered through it are clusters of cells called the islets of Langerhans that release insulin and glucagon directly into the blood. You will meet these cells in detail next week.
What is a hormone?
A hormone is a chemical messenger released by a cell into the bloodstream that travels to a distant cell and changes that cell’s behavior. The word comes from the Greek horman, meaning “to set in motion” or “to urge on.” A hormone does not do the work itself. It tells a target cell to do work the target was already capable of doing.
Three properties make the bloodstream a powerful medium for messaging. First, every tissue is bathed in blood, so a hormone released from one gland reaches every cell in the body within about a minute. Second, only cells that carry the right receptor respond — the hormone passes by every other cell without effect. Third, the rate of secretion can be tuned exquisitely, so the body can adjust the strength of the signal moment to moment.
Hormones fall into three chemical families. Knowing which family a hormone belongs to tells you almost everything about how it will work at its target cell.
Peptide and protein hormones
Most hormones in the body are peptides or proteins — chains of amino acids ranging from three residues (TRH) to nearly two hundred (growth hormone). They are water-soluble (hydrophilic), so they dissolve readily in plasma and circulate freely. Insulin, glucagon, growth hormone, all the anterior pituitary hormones, and most hypothalamic releasing factors are peptides. Because they cannot cross the lipid bilayer of a cell membrane, they must bind to a receptor on the outside of the target cell.
Steroid hormones
Steroid hormones are built from a four-ring cholesterol skeleton. They are lipid-soluble (lipophilic), so they cross cell membranes effortlessly — but they do not dissolve well in plasma and must be carried on transport proteins in the blood. The major steroid hormones are produced by the adrenal cortex (cortisol, aldosterone, adrenal androgens) and the gonads (testosterone, estrogen, progesterone). Because they cross the membrane, their receptors sit inside the target cell — in the cytoplasm or directly in the nucleus.
Amine hormones
Amine hormones are small molecules derived from a single amino acid. Two families matter for this course. The catecholamines (epinephrine, norepinephrine) are made from tyrosine in the adrenal medulla. They behave like peptide hormones — water-soluble, fast-acting, bind surface receptors. The thyroid hormones (T3, T4) are also made from tyrosine but with iodine atoms attached, and they behave like steroids — lipid-soluble, slow-acting, cross the membrane to bind intracellular receptors.
💡 CLAUDE BERNARD AND THE MILIEU INTÉRIEUR
Claude Bernard (1813–1878) was a French physiologist who proposed an idea so simple that it now seems obvious: a living body maintains its own internal environment — he called it the milieu intérieur, the “interior environment” — at conditions different from the outside world, and that this internal stability is what makes complex life possible.

Bernard noticed that warm-blooded animals keep their body temperature steady even when the air around them changes. Their blood sugar stays in a narrow range whether they have just eaten or fasted overnight. Their salt and water balance changes only slightly across a wide range of activity. From this, Bernard drew his now-famous principle: “La fixité du milieu intérieur est la condition de la vie libre” — the stability of the internal environment is the condition of free, independent life.

Bernard did not yet know about hormones; the word would not be coined until 1905, almost three decades after his death. But the system he described — many organs working together to defend a steady internal state — is exactly the role the endocrine system plays. Every gland you will study this module exists to defend some part of the milieu intérieur: blood sugar, blood calcium, blood pressure, body temperature, salt and water balance. Bernard gave the endocrine system its job description before anyone knew the system existed.
How a hormone changes a cell
A hormone’s effect depends entirely on its receptor. Two hormones can travel through the same drop of blood; the cell that has the receptor for one ignores the other completely. This is what makes hormone signaling specific in a non-specific medium.
Surface receptors and second messengers. Hydrophilic hormones (peptides, catecholamines) cannot cross the membrane, so they bind a receptor that sticks out from the cell surface. The receptor passes the message inward by activating an intracellular signaling cascade — often starting with a molecule called cyclic AMP, which then activates enzymes that change what the cell is doing within seconds. This is fast hormone action, and it is largely reversible: when the hormone leaves, the cascade stops.
Intracellular receptors and gene expression. Lipophilic hormones (steroids, thyroid hormone) pass through the membrane and bind a receptor in the cytoplasm or nucleus. The hormone’s job is to turn genes on or off. The cell’s response is to make new proteins. This takes hours to days, and once made, the effect persists long after the hormone is gone. This is why the actions of testosterone, estrogen, cortisol, and thyroid hormone are described as slow and sustained: they are literally rebuilding the cell’s protein inventory.
Figure — Two mechanisms of action, side by side. Steroid hormones (left) cross the membrane and act on DNA in the nucleus. Peptide hormones (right) bind a surface receptor and trigger a cascade of intracellular messengers. (Jack Westin.)
How the body controls its own hormones
If the body simply released hormones, they would never stop. Almost every endocrine pathway is therefore controlled by negative feedback: the hormone’s downstream effect — or the level of the hormone itself in the blood — signals back to the gland to slow down. When blood calcium rises, the parathyroid glands release less PTH. When blood thyroid hormone is high, the hypothalamus and pituitary release less TRH and TSH. When the bloodstream has enough cortisol, the brain reduces ACTH.
A small number of pathways use positive feedback, where the effect accelerates the cause. The release of oxytocin during childbirth is the clearest example: stretching of the cervix triggers oxytocin release, which causes uterine contractions, which stretch the cervix more, which triggers more oxytocin. Positive feedback is rare because it is, by definition, runaway — useful only where you want a process to escalate to a clear endpoint and then stop.
A first map of the endocrine system
The endocrine system is unlike every other organ system you have studied this year. It has no continuous tube (like the digestive or respiratory tract), no central pump (like the heart), no protective frame (like the skeleton). It is a scattered collection of small glands tucked into various corners of the body, connected by the bloodstream that carries their products.
The primary endocrine glands — organs whose only job is to make hormones — are the pituitary, the thyroid, the parathyroids, and the adrenal glands. The dual endocrine organs have major endocrine functions and major non-endocrine functions; the most important are the hypothalamus (which is also a brain region) and the pancreas (which is also a digestive organ). Many other organs — the heart, kidneys, stomach, small intestine, gonads — release at least one hormone as part of their daily work; these are the secondary endocrine organs, and we will return to them in Week 2.
By the end of this two-week module you will know where every one of these structures sits in the body, what hormones it makes, and what those hormones do. The system whose job is to keep the milieu intérieur steady is about to come into view.
▶  Self-check: name two clear differences between endocrine and exocrine glands before clicking
1. Endocrine glands have no ducts; they release hormones directly into the bloodstream. Exocrine glands release products through ducts onto a surface (skin, mouth, gut).
2. Endocrine products (hormones) act on distant cells via the blood. Exocrine products act locally at the surface where the duct opens.

Bonus: name one organ that is both, and what each of its halves does. Answer: the pancreas. Its exocrine acinar cells release digestive enzymes through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum. Its endocrine islet cells release insulin and glucagon directly into the blood.
