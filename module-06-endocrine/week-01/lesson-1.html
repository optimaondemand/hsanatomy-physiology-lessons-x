Lesson 6.01
Endocrine vs. Exocrine: What Is a Hormone?
Lesson
Module thread — Courtesy. Hormones are the body’s quiet politeness: a steady whisper of chemical messages that ask cells to do their work without ever raising the voice of a nerve. Notice this week how little it takes — nanograms per liter — to coordinate the whole organism.

✏️ YOUR DRAWING SESSION THIS WEEK
What to do. On a large page (paper highly recommended; a digital drawing file works too), draw a front-view outline of a person in anatomical position and title it “Endocrine Map.” Keep the outline large — the endocrine glands are so small and so internal that you need the body outline to keep your bearings, and it stays in front of you for this entire module. Using Reading 6.01 and an anatomy reference, locate and mark every primary endocrine gland for the first time: pituitary (under the brain), thyroid (front of neck), parathyroids (back of thyroid — you will not see them from the front, so just mark where they live with a dotted outline), adrenal glands (atop the kidneys), and pancreas (deep in the upper abdomen). You will not draw them in detail yet — that begins in Lesson 6.03.
Goal. Get a sense of how small and how scattered the endocrine system is. The largest pure endocrine gland (thyroid) weighs ~25 grams. The pituitary weighs half a gram. Compare those to a kidney (~150 g) or the heart (~300 g). The endocrine system runs the body using a total of about 100 grams of tissue.
Time: ~15 minutes.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to distinguish endocrine glands from exocrine glands and give one example of an organ that is both.
2
I will be able to classify a hormone as peptide, steroid, or amine, and predict from that classification whether its receptor will be at the cell surface or inside the cell.
3
I will be able to describe a negative feedback loop in plain language, using one specific hormone as my example.
