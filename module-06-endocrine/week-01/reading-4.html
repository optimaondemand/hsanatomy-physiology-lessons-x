The Thyroid and Parathyroid Glands

📖 READING
Where the thyroid sits
The thyroid gland is the largest pure endocrine gland in the body. It sits in the front of the neck, wrapped around the upper trachea, just inferior to the larynx. Two lateral lobes — a right and a left — hang on either side of the trachea, joined across the front by a thin bridge of tissue called the isthmus. The shape is striking: a butterfly, or the letter H turned on its side. The whole gland weighs about 25 to 30 grams in a healthy adult, and you can sometimes see it move when a person swallows — it rides up and down with the larynx because it is firmly attached to the trachea below it.
Microscopically, the thyroid is made of millions of tiny spherical sacs called follicles. Each follicle is a single-cell-thick wall of follicular cells surrounding a central pool of a sticky protein called colloid. The follicular cells use the colloid as a storage tank for thyroid hormone in its precursor form. This is unusual: most endocrine cells make hormone on demand and release it immediately. The thyroid is the only gland that stores months’ worth of hormone outside its cells, in a private reservoir each follicle keeps for itself.
Thyroid hormone: T3, T4, and iodine
The thyroid releases two related hormones built around the amino acid tyrosine plus iodine atoms. T4 (thyroxine) has four iodine atoms attached. T3 (triiodothyronine) has three. T4 is by far the more abundant in the blood, but T3 is roughly four times more potent at the receptor. Target tissues convert T4 to T3 as needed by removing one iodine atom. T4, in effect, is the prohormone — the body’s slow-release form. T3 is what actually does the work at the receptor.
Thyroid hormone is one of the most consequential signals in the body. It binds intracellular receptors and changes gene expression in nearly every tissue. Its master effect is to set the basal metabolic rate — the rate at which resting tissues consume oxygen and produce energy. When thyroid hormone is high, every cell runs warmer and faster: heart rate up, body temperature up, weight loss, restlessness, sometimes tremor. When thyroid hormone is low, every cell runs cooler and slower: cold intolerance, slowed heart rate, weight gain despite low appetite, fatigue, slowed thinking. There are few hormones in the body whose dose-effect relationship is felt as systemically as thyroid hormone.
A key fact about thyroid hormone is that the body cannot make it without iodine. Iodine is a trace element that we obtain almost entirely from food. The thyroid is the only tissue in the body that actively concentrates iodine from the blood; in the United States and many other countries, common table salt is iodized specifically to ensure adequate intake. Without iodine, the thyroid cannot manufacture T3 or T4, and in compensation it can swell to enormous size — a visibly enlarged thyroid is called a goiter.
✨ CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH: IODINE DEFICIENCY GLOBALLY
Iodine deficiency is, by some measures, the leading preventable cause of brain damage in the world. The World Health Organization estimates that nearly 2 billion people live with inadequate iodine intake. In adults, severe deficiency produces a goiter and the cold, slowed picture of hypothyroidism; in pregnant women, it impairs the fetus’s neurological development; in infants, it produces a severe and irreversible cognitive disability historically called cretinism (now called congenital iodine deficiency syndrome).

Public-health researchers are still studying the effect of universal salt iodization — the policy that started in Switzerland in 1922, spread to the United States in 1924, and has been adopted by most countries since. The intervention is one of the simplest and cheapest in modern medicine: a few dollars per ton of salt produces measurable IQ gains across whole populations. Researchers continue to track regions where iodized salt is not consistently used — parts of Central Africa, the Andes, the Himalayas, and rural pockets of South Asia — and to study how marginally low intake (not full deficiency) affects child development. This is anatomy and physiology meeting public health: a single trace element, a single small gland, and the cognitive trajectory of millions of children.
How the thyroid is regulated
The thyroid sits on the bottom floor of the HPT axis you met last reading. When blood T4/T3 falls, the hypothalamus releases TRH, which drives the anterior pituitary to release TSH, which travels in the blood to the thyroid and stimulates the follicular cells to release stored hormone and to make more. When blood thyroid hormone rises, both TRH and TSH are suppressed. The result is a slow, smooth oscillation that keeps thyroid hormone within a narrow range across the day and across the seasons.
Figure — The HPT axis regulating the thyroid: TRH → TSH → T3/T4, with negative feedback (–) rising back to both the hypothalamus and the pituitary.
Two clinical pictures result from breakdowns of this loop. Hypothyroidism — most often caused by autoimmune destruction of the thyroid (Hashimoto’s thyroiditis) — produces low T4 and, in compensation, very high TSH. Hyperthyroidism — most often caused by an autoimmune antibody that mimics TSH (Graves’ disease) — produces high T4 and, in compensation, very low TSH. Measuring TSH is the standard first test of thyroid status precisely because it changes in the opposite direction from the thyroid hormone itself.
Calcitonin: the thyroid’s second hormone
Scattered between the follicles is a second cell type called parafollicular cells, or C cells. They make a different hormone — calcitonin — that has nothing to do with metabolic rate. Calcitonin lowers blood calcium by inhibiting bone-dissolving cells (osteoclasts) and by promoting calcium loss in the urine. Calcitonin is much less important than its counterpart from the parathyroid glands; the thyroid’s role in calcium balance is modest in adult humans. But its presence here, in a gland mostly devoted to metabolism, is a useful reminder that anatomical neighbors are not always functional partners. Calcitonin is a tenant in the thyroid, not part of its main business.
The parathyroid glands and blood calcium
Tucked into the posterior face of each thyroid lobe are usually four tiny glands — the parathyroid glands. They are each about the size of a grain of rice, no more than 30 milligrams apiece. Anatomically they are independent endocrine organs that just happen to be embedded in the thyroid’s back surface, and they have nothing to do with thyroid hormone production. They make one product: parathyroid hormone (PTH).
PTH is the master regulator of blood calcium. The body keeps blood calcium in an unusually tight range — roughly 8.5 to 10.5 mg/dL — because calcium ions are necessary for nerve firing, muscle contraction, and blood clotting. Calcium too low: nerves fire spontaneously, muscles cramp, tetany sets in. Calcium too high: nerves and muscles become less excitable, fatigue and cognitive slowing set in, and over time calcium can deposit in soft tissues. The body cannot afford to lose this regulation.
Figure — The calcium homeostasis loop in both directions: PTH from the parathyroid glands raises blood calcium via bone, kidney, and gut; calcitonin from the thyroid nudges it back down.
When blood calcium falls, the parathyroid glands release PTH, which raises blood calcium through three coordinated actions:
Bone. PTH stimulates osteoclasts to dissolve a small amount of bone matrix, releasing calcium and phosphate into the blood. Bone is the body’s calcium reservoir; the skeleton is doing more work than just holding you up.
Kidney. PTH causes the kidney to reabsorb more calcium from the urine back into the blood, and to dump more phosphate into the urine (we want calcium back, not phosphate, because together they would precipitate out as bone mineral).
Gut, indirectly. PTH activates an enzyme in the kidney that converts vitamin D to its active form (calcitriol). Active vitamin D then increases calcium absorption from food in the small intestine. This is why vitamin D matters for bone health: without it, the gut cannot absorb dietary calcium efficiently.
When blood calcium rises too high, PTH falls, and these three pathways slow down. The opposing hormone — calcitonin from the thyroid — nudges calcium downward, though as noted it plays only a small role in adults.
Figure — The thyroid gland from the front (butterfly shape, right and left lobes joined by the isthmus) and from the back, where the four parathyroid glands sit on its posterior surface.
A surgical note
Because the parathyroid glands are so small, so closely attached to the thyroid’s back, and so easily mistaken for a lump of fat, they were occasionally removed by accident during 19th- and early 20th-century thyroid surgery. Patients would survive the thyroid operation only to develop tetany — spontaneous muscle spasms — within days. The lesson, learned the hard way, is that the parathyroids are not part of the thyroid; they are anatomical neighbors with a completely different and life-critical job. Modern thyroid surgery preserves them carefully.
▶  Self-check: state the regulation pattern before clicking
If blood calcium drops, what does the body do?
Parathyroid glands release more PTH. PTH dissolves a little bone, recovers more calcium from urine, and activates vitamin D to absorb more calcium from food.

If blood T4 drops, what does the body do?
Hypothalamus releases more TRH, anterior pituitary releases more TSH, thyroid follicular cells release more T4 from stored colloid (and make more).

Notice the difference: the thyroid is controlled top-down through a 3-floor axis. The parathyroid is controlled directly by the bloodstream — it monitors blood calcium itself and responds. Not every endocrine gland has a pituitary boss.
