The master gland and its conductor
The pituitary gland is small — about the size of a pea, weighing roughly half a gram — and it sits in a bony cup in the floor of the skull called the sella turcica, the “Turkish saddle.” You can locate this cup on the sphenoid bone you traced in Module 3. The pituitary hangs from the underside of the brain on a short stalk called the infundibulum, which connects it to the hypothalamus directly above. The hypothalamus is a region of the diencephalon — one of the four brain divisions you sculpted in Module 5 — and it is the brain’s direct line into the endocrine system.
The pituitary is sometimes called the “master gland” because its hormones control other endocrine glands. But the term is slightly misleading: the pituitary itself takes orders from the hypothalamus. A more accurate description treats the hypothalamus as the conductor and the pituitary as the first-chair section of the orchestra — the pituitary directs the rest of the endocrine ensemble, but only when the conductor tells it to.
Anatomically, the pituitary has two halves with completely different origins. The anterior pituitary (also called the adenohypophysis) is glandular tissue derived from the embryonic roof of the mouth. It is, in tissue type and behavior, a typical endocrine gland. The posterior pituitary (the neurohypophysis) is nervous tissue derived from the floor of the brain. It is not really a gland at all — it is a downward extension of the hypothalamus that stores and releases hormones made by neurons up in the brain. The two halves do not share hormones; they do not even share their basic biology. They are two organs that happen to occupy the same anatomical position.
Figure — The hypothalamus and pituitary in anatomical relationship: the infundibular stalk connects the hypothalamus above to the pituitary’s anterior (adenohypophysis) and posterior (neurohypophysis) halves, seated in the sella turcica. (Kenhub.)
The anterior pituitary
The anterior pituitary makes and releases six classical hormones. The hypothalamus controls each one by sending it small releasing hormones (or, in two cases, inhibiting hormones) through a specialized blood supply called the hypophyseal portal system. This portal system is a short loop of capillaries that picks up hypothalamic hormones from the median eminence and carries them straight to the anterior pituitary, so they arrive concentrated rather than diluted across the entire body.

Growth hormone (GH)Liver, bone, muscle, most tissuesPromotes growth (directly and via IGF-1); raises blood glucose; mobilizes fat
Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)Thyroid glandDrives production and release of thyroid hormones T3 and T4
Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)Adrenal cortexDrives production and release of cortisol (and modest adrenal androgens)
Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)Ovaries (women); testes (men)Drives follicle development and sperm production
Luteinizing hormone (LH)Ovaries; testesTriggers ovulation; drives testosterone production (Module 12)
Prolactin (PRL)Mammary glandsStimulates milk production after birth
Four of these — TSH, ACTH, FSH, and LH — are called tropic hormones because their job is to drive a downstream endocrine gland rather than to act on body tissues directly. The other two — growth hormone and prolactin — act on non-endocrine tissues to produce their effects.
The hypothalamus regulates each of these with a specific releasing or inhibiting hormone: TRH (thyrotropin-releasing hormone) for TSH, CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone) for ACTH, GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) for FSH and LH, GHRH and somatostatin for growth hormone, and dopamine for prolactin (dopamine inhibits prolactin — one of the small number of inhibition-only relationships in the system).
The posterior pituitary
The posterior pituitary makes no hormones of its own. It is a storage and release site for two hormones manufactured in the hypothalamus. Specialized neurons in the supraoptic nucleus and paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus produce these hormones in their cell bodies, package them into vesicles, and ship them down the axons that run through the infundibulum and end in the posterior pituitary. When the hypothalamic neuron fires, the vesicles dump their contents directly into the bloodstream there.
The two hormones are:
Antidiuretic hormone (ADH), also called vasopressin. ADH acts on the kidneys to retain water. Production rises when the blood becomes too concentrated (high osmolarity) or when blood pressure falls. The kidneys respond by reabsorbing more water back into the blood, producing smaller volumes of more concentrated urine. You will study this mechanism in detail in Module 11.
Oxytocin. Oxytocin causes contraction of smooth muscle in the uterus during childbirth (the classic positive feedback example) and contraction of cells around the milk ducts to eject milk during breastfeeding. It has also been studied for its role in social bonding, but its endocrine job is the reproductive one.
Figure — The functional difference between the two halves: the posterior pituitary (left) only stores and releases hormones made by hypothalamic neurons; the anterior pituitary (right) is a true gland, manufacturing its own six hormones under hypothalamic control. (Addison Wesley Longman, Inc.)
💡 HARVEY CUSHING AND THE PITUITARY
Harvey Cushing (1869–1939) was an American neurosurgeon at Johns Hopkins and Harvard who first established that the small pea-sized gland tucked into the sella turcica governed an extraordinary share of the body’s endocrine activity. In the early 1900s, surgical access to the pituitary was almost unthinkable — it sits beneath the brain, behind the eyes, and lateral to the optic nerves. Cushing pioneered a surgical route through the nose and sphenoid sinus that made the pituitary reachable without lifting the brain, and he developed the operating technique with such care that he reduced neurosurgical mortality from above 50 percent to below 10 percent in his lifetime.

Through careful clinical observation of pituitary tumor patients, Cushing established two conditions that still bear his name. Cushing’s disease is the syndrome caused by a pituitary tumor that secretes too much ACTH, driving the adrenal cortex to make too much cortisol — producing the distinctive features of weight gain centered on the trunk and face, weakened skin, muscle wasting, and high blood pressure. Cushing’s syndrome is the same downstream picture when caused by any source of excess cortisol, including treatment with synthetic steroids. Distinguishing the two became one of the foundational diagnostic problems in endocrinology — and Cushing’s careful documentation of his patients, including photographs that mapped the progression of the disease, is still consulted by clinicians today.
The hypothalamic-pituitary-target axes
Three regulatory loops are worth memorizing as patterns rather than as lists of letters. In each, the hypothalamus releases a small hormone that drives the anterior pituitary, which releases a tropic hormone that drives a target gland, which releases its own hormone — and the downstream hormone feeds back to suppress further activity in the hypothalamus and pituitary. This is classic negative feedback.
Figure — The same three-floor pattern, three times: HPA (stress), HPG (reproduction), and HPT (metabolism) axes, each with its own negative-feedback loop rising from the target-gland hormone back to the hypothalamus and pituitary.
Hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis (HPT). TRH → TSH → T3/T4. Thyroid hormones travel everywhere and set metabolic rate. When their level rises, they suppress TRH and TSH; when it falls, the brakes come off. You will see this axis again next lesson, in the thyroid reading.
Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (HPA). CRH → ACTH → cortisol. Cortisol prepares the body for sustained stress (Week 2). Cortisol itself shuts down further CRH and ACTH.
Hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis (HPG). GnRH → FSH & LH → testosterone or estrogen and progesterone. These hormones drive sexual development and reproduction (Module 12), and they suppress their own driving hormones in feedback loops that are more complex in the female cycle than in the male system.
Each axis is a three-floor building. The hypothalamus is on top. The pituitary is the middle floor. The target gland is the ground floor. Signals descend; feedback ascends. Almost every major hormone you will study sits somewhere in one of these three axes.

Growth hormone (GH); thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH); adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH); follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH); luteinizing hormone (LH); prolactin (PRL).

A common mnemonic is FLAT PiG: FSH, LH, ACTH, TSH, Prolactin, Growth hormone. The lowercase i is just a placeholder to make the word pronounceable.
