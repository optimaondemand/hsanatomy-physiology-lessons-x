Muscles of the Upper Limb

📖 READING
From shoulder to fingertip
This reading covers the major muscles that move the shoulder, the elbow, the wrist, and the fingers. Five named muscles, plus two groups of forearm muscles. Most of them attach to the bones of the upper limb that you traced in Module 3 — the humerus, radius, ulna, and the carpals. The hand has 30+ additional small muscles for fine motor control, which you will not be required to memorize this year.
As you read, hold an arm out in front of you. Every action described here you can perform on your own arm.
Figure — Muscles of the upper limb, from the deltoid at the shoulder to the flexor and extensor compartments of the forearm.
Deltoid — the shoulder cap
The rounded muscle that gives the shoulder its shape is the deltoid. Its name comes from the Greek letter delta — it is roughly triangular. The deltoid has three fiber groups (anterior, middle, posterior), each producing a different motion of the arm at the shoulder. The middle fibers do most of what people mean when they say “raise your arm out to the side” (abduction).
Origin: lateral third of the clavicle, the acromion, and the spine of the scapula.  Insertion: the deltoid tuberosity of the humerus (a roughened bump on the lateral side of the bone).  Action: primarily abducts the arm at the shoulder; anterior fibers flex and medially rotate; posterior fibers extend and laterally rotate.
Biceps brachii — the elbow flexor with two heads
The biceps brachii is the front-of-upper-arm muscle that bulges when you flex your elbow. It is called biceps because it has two heads at its origin — the long head from the supraglenoid tubercle of the scapula (running through the intertubercular groove of the humerus), and the short head from the coracoid process of the scapula. The two heads merge into a single muscle belly and a single tendon that inserts on the radial tuberosity.
The biceps does two interesting things at once: it flexes the elbow, and it supinates the forearm (turns the palm to face upward). This is why the wrong way to turn a screwdriver feels weak — with a right hand, a clockwise (tightening) screwdriver motion turns the palm upward, which is the supination motion the biceps is built for, and the muscle generates maximum torque there.
Origin: long head — supraglenoid tubercle of scapula; short head — coracoid process of scapula.  Insertion: radial tuberosity of the radius.  Action: flexes the elbow; supinates the forearm; weakly flexes the shoulder.
Triceps brachii — the elbow extensor with three heads
On the back of the upper arm sits the triceps brachii — three heads converging on a single insertion. The triceps and the biceps are antagonist muscles at the elbow: the biceps flexes the elbow, the triceps extends it. Every motion at a joint has at least one muscle pulling each way; the body never relies on relaxation alone to reverse a motion.
Origin: long head — infraglenoid tubercle of scapula; lateral and medial heads — posterior shaft of humerus.  Insertion: olecranon process of the ulna.  Action: extends the elbow; the long head also extends the shoulder.
Forearm flexors — the anterior compartment
The front of your forearm is filled with the forearm flexor muscles — a group of long, parallel muscles whose tendons cross the wrist and reach into the hand. They flex the wrist and the fingers. Together they originate on or near the medial epicondyle of the humerus — the same bone marking you traced and labeled on your VR figure in Module 3. (Recall the M3 promise that the medial epicondyle would matter when muscles came in. Here it is.)
The named members of the group include the flexor carpi radialis, the flexor carpi ulnaris, the palmaris longus (absent in some people — press your thumb and pinky together and look at the inside of your wrist; the tendon that pops up in the middle is palmaris longus, if you have it), and the flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus, which curl the fingers.
Common origin (most): medial epicondyle of the humerus.  Insertion: bones of the wrist (carpals + base of metacarpals) and the fingers (phalanges).  Action: flex the wrist and the fingers.
Forearm extensors — the posterior compartment
The back of the forearm contains the forearm extensor muscles. They are the antagonists of the flexors: they extend (straighten) the wrist and the fingers. Most of them originate on or near the lateral epicondyle of the humerus, on the outside of the elbow. When this common origin is overworked — commonly in tennis players — it can develop chronic inflammation called lateral epicondylitis, also known as “tennis elbow.”
Named members of the group include the extensor carpi radialis longus and brevis, the extensor carpi ulnaris, and the extensor digitorum (the muscle whose tendons you can see riding up the back of your hand when you straighten your fingers).
Common origin (most): lateral epicondyle of the humerus.  Insertion: bones of the wrist and the fingers (posterior side).  Action: extend the wrist and the fingers.
The antagonist pattern, generalized
Notice the architecture: biceps in front of the elbow flexes; triceps behind the elbow extends. Flexor compartment in the front of the forearm flexes; extensor compartment in the back of the forearm extends. The pattern continues into the lower limb next week: quadriceps in the front extends the knee; hamstrings in the back flex the knee. Antagonist pairs are the rule, not the exception. Whenever you find a muscle that does something, look across the joint — there will be a muscle that undoes it.
Figure — The antagonist pattern at the elbow: biceps contracted and triceps relaxed produces flexion; triceps contracted and biceps relaxed produces extension.
