📖 READING
Two regions, one trunk
This reading covers two neighboring regions of the trunk in one sitting: the muscles of the back (which move the shoulder and hold the spine upright) and the muscles of the thorax (which drive breathing). They share a reading because they share a lot of anatomy — the back muscles you learn first sit directly over the rib cage the thorax muscles occupy, and by the end of this reading you will have traced the trunk from the skin down to the diaphragm.
Two layers of back muscle
The back is built in layers. Superficial back muscles — the ones near the surface — mainly move the shoulder and arm. Their origins are on the spine and skull, but their insertions are on the bones of the shoulder girdle or the humerus. They are the visible “back” muscles when you look at someone’s back. Deep back muscles — the ones underneath — mostly extend and stabilize the vertebral column itself. They are what holds you upright, and they fire continuously while you stand.
This section covers two superficial back muscles and one deep back group. You drew the underlying vertebral column in Module 3; now you put the muscles that move it (and that pull on the shoulder) directly onto that frame.
Trapezius — the diamond on your back
The trapezius is a large, flat, diamond-shaped muscle spanning the upper back and neck. The left and right trapezius together form one big four-sided figure: top point at the base of the skull, side points at the shoulders, bottom point at about T12. It has three fiber bundles (upper, middle, lower) that produce different motions of the scapula. When you shrug your shoulders, your upper trapezius is firing. When you pull your shoulder blades back to stand at attention, your middle trapezius is firing.
Origin: external occipital protuberance and ligamentum nuchae, plus spinous processes of C7–T12.  Insertion: lateral third of the clavicle, the acromion, and the spine of the scapula.  Action: elevates, retracts, depresses, and rotates the scapula (depending on which fibers fire); extends the neck.
Latissimus dorsi — the swimmer’s muscle
Below the trapezius, on the lower and middle back, sits the latissimus dorsi — Latin for “broadest of the back.” It is wide and flat, sweeping from the lower spine all the way up to a small attachment on the front of the humerus. Because its insertion is on the front of the upper arm bone, when latissimus contracts it pulls the arm down and back. This is the swimmer’s pull-through stroke. It is also the climber’s pull-up. The latissimus is what allows you to pull your body upward when your hand is fixed above your head.
Origin: spinous processes of T7–L5, thoracolumbar fascia, the iliac crest, and the lower 3–4 ribs.  Insertion: floor of the intertubercular (bicipital) groove of the humerus, on the front of the upper arm.  Action: extends, adducts, and medially rotates the arm at the shoulder; pulls the body upward when the hand is fixed (pull-up, climb, swim stroke).
Figure — The trapezius and latissimus dorsi, the two large superficial muscles of the back.
Erector spinae — the three columns that keep you upright
Beneath the trapezius and latissimus is a deep set of muscles called the erector spinae group — literally “the raisers of the spine.” They are not one muscle but three parallel columns that run vertically alongside the spine, from the sacrum and iliac crest all the way up to the skull. Each column inserts on multiple ribs and vertebrae along the way. Together, when both sides contract, they extend the vertebral column — they pull you upright. When one side contracts alone, the column bends sideways toward that side (lateral flexion).
Working from lateral (out at the sides) to medial (close to the spine), the three columns are:
▶ Iliocostalis — the most lateral; attaches the iliac crest below to the ribs above.
▶ Longissimus — the middle column; the longest of the three; runs the full length of the back into the skull.
▶ Spinalis — the most medial, sitting right against the spinous processes of the vertebrae.
Origin: sacrum, iliac crest, lumbar / thoracic spinous and transverse processes.  Insertion: ribs, transverse and spinous processes of higher vertebrae, mastoid process of the temporal bone (longissimus capitis).  Action: bilaterally, extends the vertebral column and head; unilaterally, laterally flexes the column to the same side; tonically active to maintain upright posture.
Figure — The three columns of the erector spinae, from medial to lateral: spinalis (thoracic only), longissimus, and iliocostalis.
Stand up straight and notice: there is no “decide to stand straight” muscle. Standing upright is the constant background activity of the erector spinae group, which fires just enough to keep gravity from folding you forward. The trapezius and latissimus, by contrast, are mostly silent while you stand and become loud when you move — especially when you move your shoulders or your arms. Together, the three layers (deep stabilizers + superficial movers) are an architecture: deep muscles keep the spine vertical so that the superficial muscles have a stable frame to pull against.
This constant background firing is called muscle tone, and it is not one set of fibers working nonstop — that would fatigue almost immediately. Instead, only a few motor units are active in the erector spinae at any given instant, and the nervous system cycles which units are firing, most likely in a rotating pattern. While some motor units are contracted, others rest and recover, so the muscle as a whole never fatigues completely even though it never fully relaxes. This is why you can stand for hours without your back giving out, and why damage to the parts of the nervous system that manage this cycling — the cerebellum, or the nerve supply to the muscle itself — produces hypotonia, an abnormal loss of that background firing.
The muscles you cannot stop using
Skeletal muscle is voluntary. You decide when to clench your fist, you decide when to walk. But two sets of skeletal muscles work continuously, day and night, asleep and awake, without your having to think about them: the muscles that drive breathing. They are still under voluntary control — you can hold your breath, you can pant on purpose — but most of the time, the brainstem fires them for you on a rhythm you do not have to maintain.
The rest of this reading covers the two main groups of breathing muscles: the intercostals between your ribs, and the diaphragm at the bottom of your rib cage. These are the muscles you will revisit in Module 9 when you learn the mechanics of ventilation. For now, the goal is to know where they are and what they do.
The intercostals — muscles between the ribs
There are 11 pairs of ribs that connect to the sternum directly or indirectly (you drew them all in Module 3), and there is a muscle in each space between adjacent ribs. These are the intercostal muscles. There are actually three layers per space — external, internal, and innermost — but for high school A&P we focus on the two layers that produce the largest motions: the externals and the internals.
Figure — Intercostal Muscles. The external intercostals are located laterally on the sides of the body. The internal intercostals are located medially near the sternum. The innermost intercostals are deep to both.
The fibers of the external intercostals run downward and forward (think of pushing your hands into your pockets — the fibers run the way your fingers point). When they contract, they pull the rib below upward toward the rib above. The whole rib cage lifts and swings outward. The thoracic cavity gets larger. This is the major action that drives inspiration (breathing in).
The fibers of the internal intercostals run perpendicular to the externals — downward and backward. When they contract, they pull the ribs downward and inward, making the thoracic cavity smaller. Most of the time this is not necessary — ordinary exhalation is passive (the rib cage falls back down on its own, helped by the elastic recoil of the lungs). The internal intercostals are recruited when you exhale forcefully: blowing out a candle, shouting, sneezing, coughing.
Figure — Inhalation: the rib cage expands as rib muscles contract and the diaphragm moves down. Exhalation: the rib cage gets smaller as rib muscles relax and the diaphragm moves up.
External intercostals — Origin: inferior border of the rib above. Insertion: superior border of the rib below. Action: elevates the ribs (inspiration).
Internal intercostals — Origin: superior border of the rib below. Insertion: inferior border of the rib above. Action: depresses the ribs (forced expiration).
The diaphragm — the dome that does most of the work
Stretched across the bottom of your rib cage like a parachute is the diaphragm. It is a wide, dome-shaped sheet of skeletal muscle that completely separates the thoracic cavity (above) from the abdominal cavity (below). The diaphragm is the single most important muscle for breathing. About 75% of the volume change in a normal inhalation is the diaphragm’s doing; the intercostals handle the rest. When the diaphragm contracts, the dome flattens, pulling the floor of the thoracic cavity downward, like a piston dropping. The thoracic cavity gets taller, the pressure inside the lungs drops, air rushes in. When the diaphragm relaxes, the dome returns to its resting upward curve and air flows out passively.
Origin: the inner surface of the lower 6 ribs and costal cartilages, the xiphoid process of the sternum, and the bodies of L1–L3.  Insertion: the central tendon — a flat tendinous sheet at the top of the dome.  Action: flattens the dome and enlarges the thoracic cavity (inspiration). Innervated by the phrenic nerve (C3, C4, C5 — “C3, 4, 5 keep the diaphragm alive”).
Three openings through the diaphragm
The diaphragm separates the thorax from the abdomen, but several things still need to pass between the two cavities. There are three named openings through the muscle, each at a specific vertebral level. You can use the mnemonic I (1) ate (8) ten (10) eggs (12) at twelve to remember the levels:
▶ Caval opening at T8 — the inferior vena cava passes through. (8 letters in “vena cava.”)
▶ Esophageal hiatus at T10 — the esophagus and the vagus nerves pass through. (10 letters in “esophagus.”)
▶ Aortic hiatus at T12 — the descending aorta passes through. (12 letters in “aortic hiatus.” Also note: the aorta passes behind the diaphragm, not through it, which is why its hiatus is the lowest and least mobile.)
Figure — Muscles of the Diaphragm (inferior view). The diaphragm separates the thoracic and abdominal cavities; the vena cava, esophagus, and aorta each pass through their own named opening.
A preview of Module 9
The intercostals and diaphragm are introduced here because they are skeletal muscle — the kind we are studying this module. But their full physiological story is told in Module 9 (the respiratory system), where you will learn how the volume changes caused by the diaphragm and intercostals produce pressure changes in the lungs, and how those pressure changes draw air in and push it out. For now: a flat diaphragm and lifted ribs let air in; a relaxed diaphragm and dropped ribs let air out. The chemistry of what happens to that air will come later.
