Muscles of the Abdominal Wall
Reading
📖 READING
A wall built of perpendicular fibers
The thoracic cavity has the rib cage to protect it. The pelvic cavity has the bones of the pelvis. In between — from the lower border of the rib cage to the iliac crest — there is no bone in front. The abdominal wall is held together by four flat muscles, arranged in layers like plywood: each layer’s fibers run in a different direction, and the cross-grained design makes the wall stronger than any single layer would be alone.
Three of the four muscles (external oblique, internal oblique, transversus abdominis) are flat sheets on each side. Their fibers end in broad tendinous sheets called aponeuroses, which meet in the midline of the abdomen at a tough seam called the linea alba. The fourth muscle (rectus abdominis) is a strap that runs straight up the front of the abdomen, sheathed inside the meeting aponeuroses. Together the four muscles do three jobs: they protect the abdominal contents, they compress the abdomen (which matters for coughing, defecating, giving birth), and they flex and twist the trunk.
Figure — Superficial and deep abdominal muscles (anterior lateral view), showing the rectus sheath, rectus abdominis, and the layered oblique and transversus abdominis muscles.
Rectus abdominis — the “six-pack”
The rectus abdominis is the long strap muscle that runs straight up and down the front of the abdomen on each side of the midline, from the pubic bone to the lower rib cage. It is crossed by 3 or 4 horizontal bands of tendon called tendinous intersections, which is why a well-developed rectus abdominis looks like a six-pack (or eight-pack, depending on the number of intersections). When the muscle contracts, it pulls the rib cage and the pelvis toward each other — the classic sit-up motion.
Origin: pubic crest and pubic symphysis.  Insertion: xiphoid process of the sternum and costal cartilages of ribs 5–7.  Action: flexes the vertebral column (sit-up); compresses the abdomen.
External oblique — outermost, hands-in-pockets fibers
The external oblique is the most superficial of the three flat lateral muscles. Its fibers run downward and forward — the same direction as the external intercostals just above it, and the same direction as your fingers when you slide your hands into your front pockets. The right and left external obliques together rotate the trunk to the opposite side — the right external oblique rotates the trunk to the left, and vice versa.
Origin: the outer surfaces of the lower 8 ribs (5–12).  Insertion: the iliac crest, pubic crest, and linea alba (via its aponeurosis).  Action: compresses the abdomen; bilaterally, flexes the trunk; unilaterally, rotates the trunk to the opposite side and laterally flexes to the same side.
Internal oblique — the perpendicular layer
Directly beneath the external oblique is the internal oblique. Its fibers run nearly perpendicular to the external oblique — upward and forward. (One layer’s fingers in the front pockets, the next layer’s fingers up and inward toward the navel.) Acting alone, the right internal oblique rotates the trunk to the right (same-side rotation), opposite the external oblique.
Origin: iliac crest, inguinal ligament, and thoracolumbar fascia.  Insertion: lower ribs (10–12) and linea alba (via its aponeurosis).  Action: compresses the abdomen; bilaterally, flexes the trunk; unilaterally, rotates the trunk to the same side and laterally flexes to the same side.
Transversus abdominis — the corset
The deepest of the three flat muscles is the transversus abdominis. Its fibers run horizontally across the abdomen, like a belt or corset. Because the fibers are horizontal, the transversus abdominis cannot flex or rotate the trunk — it has only one action: it compresses the abdomen. It is the most important muscle for the "bracing" you feel when you cough or push, and the muscle that obstetricians cue patients to engage during labor.
Origin: inguinal ligament, iliac crest, thoracolumbar fascia, and lower 6 costal cartilages.  Insertion: linea alba and pubic crest (via its aponeurosis).  Action: compresses the abdomen.
Figure — The four abdominal wall muscles dissected in layers: external oblique (most superficial), transversus abdominis and internal oblique beneath it, and the rectus abdominis at the midline.
The cross-grained design and why it matters
Three lateral muscles, three fiber directions: external (down and forward), internal (up and forward), transversus (horizontal). The result is a wall that resists pressure from any direction. If the abdomen needs to push downward — during a cough, a sneeze, defecation, or childbirth — all three flat muscles plus the rectus abdominis contract together. The abdomen becomes a closed container, the pressure inside rises, and that pressure does the pushing.
There is also a cross-system connection to remember from last lesson: a forceful exhale — a cough, a shout — recruits the internal intercostals from the chest and the abdominal wall muscles from the abdomen at the same time. The abdomen pushes the diaphragm upward, the internal intercostals pull the ribs downward, and air is forced out under pressure. The abdomen is part of breathing.
▶  Self-check: name the fiber direction of each of the three flat lateral muscles, outermost to innermost
External oblique: down and forward (hands in front pockets). Internal oblique: up and forward (perpendicular to external). Transversus abdominis: horizontal (belt direction).
