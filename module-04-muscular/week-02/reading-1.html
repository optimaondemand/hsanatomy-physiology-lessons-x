📖 READING
From microscope to body region
Week 1 of this module zoomed in to the molecular machinery: the sarcomere, the cross-bridge cycle, and the neuromuscular junction. Week 2 zooms back out. Now you will learn the named muscles of the body region by region, beginning with the head and neck. For every muscle in this reading and the next, you will get four pieces of information:
▶ The name and where it is.
▶ Its origin — the bone or structure it is anchored to that does not move.
▶ Its insertion — the bone or structure it pulls on when it contracts (the movable end).
▶ Its action — what motion it produces when it contracts.
When a muscle contracts, it pulls its insertion toward its origin. That is the entire mechanical principle of skeletal muscle. Naming every muscle this way builds a habit: where it starts, where it ends, what it does. Memorize the four pieces for the five muscles below.
Five muscles you will know by sight
The head and neck contain dozens of muscles. This lesson focuses on eight, chosen because they are large enough to identify on a diagram, do work you can feel, and matter for the physiology of chewing, expression, and head movement.
Figure — Muscles of the head and neck.
Masseter — the chewing muscle you can feel
Place a fingertip on your cheek just in front of and below your ear and clench your jaw. The bulge that pushes against your finger is the masseter. It is one of the four muscles of mastication (chewing), and the most superficial.
Origin: the zygomatic arch (cheekbone).  Insertion: the lateral surface of the mandible (angle and ramus).  Action: elevates the mandible — closes the jaw and produces the force of chewing.
Temporalis — the second chewing muscle
Place a fingertip on your temple just behind and above your eye and clench again. The fan-shaped muscle that flexes is the temporalis. It works with the masseter to close the jaw, but its angled fibers also pull the mandible backward, which matters when you bite down hard on something behind your incisors.
Origin: the temporal fossa (the shallow depression on the side of the skull, formed mostly by the temporal and parietal bones).  Insertion: the coronoid process of the mandible.  Action: elevates and retracts the mandible.
Figure — Muscles That Move the Lower Jaw. The temporalis and masseter are located within the cheek and originate from processes of the skull, giving the jaw muscles the leverage needed for chewing.
Orbicularis oculi — the eyelid closer
A ring-shaped muscle wrapping around each eye. The name means “little ring around the eye” in Latin. It is the muscle that closes your eyelid when you blink, when you squint into bright light, and when you screw your face up in concentration.
Origin: the medial rim of the orbit (eye socket).  Insertion: circular — the fibers wrap around and return to the medial rim.  Action: closes the eyelid (blinking) and tightens the skin of the eye region (squinting).
Orbicularis oris — the lip closer
The matching ring-shaped muscle around the mouth. It is the muscle that closes the lips, presses them together, and rounds them — making the sounds of P, B, M, and W in English depend on it.
Origin: the maxilla and mandible at the midline.  Insertion: the skin and tissue of the lips.  Action: closes, compresses, and protrudes the lips.
Frontalis — the eyebrow raiser
Raise your eyebrows and wrinkle your forehead. The muscle doing that is the frontalis, the front belly of a larger sheet called the occipitofrontalis. Unlike the chewing muscles, frontalis has no bone insertion at all — like most muscles of facial expression, it inserts into skin and the connective tissue under it, which is exactly why it can wrinkle skin instead of just moving a joint.
Origin: the epicranial aponeurosis (a tendinous sheet over the top of the skull).  Insertion: the skin of the eyebrows and forehead.  Action: raises the eyebrows; wrinkles the forehead skin horizontally.
Zygomaticus major — the smiling muscle
Smile. The muscle that pulls the corner of your mouth up and back, toward your cheekbone, is the zygomaticus major. Its name tells you its origin (zygoma, the cheekbone) and its shape (a long, narrow strap running diagonally down to the mouth).
Origin: the zygomatic bone.  Insertion: the skin and muscle at the corner of the mouth.  Action: elevates the corner of the mouth (smiling).
Buccinator — the trumpeter’s muscle
Deep in the cheek, forming most of its wall, is the buccinator — Latin for “trumpeter,” because it is the muscle that compresses the cheek to force air out through pursed lips, the way a trumpet player does. It has a quieter everyday job too: it holds food between the teeth during chewing, keeping it from pooling in the space between the cheek and the gums.
Origin: the alveolar processes of the maxilla and mandible, and the pterygomandibular raphe.  Insertion: blends into the orbicularis oris at the corner of the mouth.  Action: compresses the cheek against the teeth (chewing, whistling, blowing air out under pressure).
Sternocleidomastoid — the head-turner
Turn your head sharply to the right. On the left side of your neck, a thick strap of muscle stands out from the top of your sternum to behind your ear. That is the sternocleidomastoid (SCM). Its long name is a clue: sterno- (sternum) + cleido- (clavicle) + mastoid (the mastoid process behind the ear). Two origins, one insertion, one easy-to-remember pull. When one side contracts, it rotates the head to the opposite side and tilts the head toward the same shoulder. When both sides contract together, they flex the neck forward (tucks chin to chest).
Origin: the manubrium of the sternum and the medial third of the clavicle.  Insertion: the mastoid process of the temporal bone.  Action: unilaterally, rotates head to the opposite side and tilts toward the same shoulder; bilaterally, flexes the neck.
Why head and neck muscles matter even outside this module
Five of these eight muscles — orbicularis oculi, orbicularis oris, frontalis, zygomaticus major, and buccinator — are muscles of facial expression, a category of about 20 small skeletal muscles in the face that attach skin to skin (or skin to bone) rather than bone to bone. They are responsible for everything a face does that is not chewing: smiling, frowning, raising eyebrows, squinting, kissing, whistling. The masseter and temporalis are muscles of mastication, responsible for chewing. The sternocleidomastoid is in a third category — muscles that move the head on the neck.
All of these are innervated by cranial nerves — nerves that emerge directly from the brain, not from the spinal cord. You will meet the cranial nerves in detail in Module 5. For now: when you smile, cranial nerve VII (the facial nerve) is what carried the order. When you bite, cranial nerve V (the trigeminal nerve) carried it. The cross-connection runs both ways: when the facial nerve is damaged by stroke or by Bell’s palsy, the muscles of one side of the face stop working and the smile goes lopsided. Cross-system thinking begins early.
Figure — The trigeminal nerve (cranial nerve V, muscles of mastication) and the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII, muscles of facial expression) branching across the head and face.
▶  Self-check: name the eight muscles in order, plus one action of each, without looking back
Masseter: elevates mandible (chewing). Temporalis: elevates and retracts mandible. Orbicularis oculi: closes the eyelid (blinking). Orbicularis oris: closes and rounds the lips. Frontalis: raises the eyebrows. Zygomaticus major: elevates the corner of the mouth (smiling). Buccinator: compresses the cheek. Sternocleidomastoid: rotates head to opposite side / flexes the neck.
