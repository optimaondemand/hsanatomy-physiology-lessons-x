The Sarcomere and the Sliding Filament Theory — How a Muscle Actually Shortens
Reading
📖 READING
The sarcomere — the unit of contraction
Last reading you left off at the myofibril — the long parallel rod that makes up most of a muscle fiber’s interior. A myofibril, looked at lengthwise, is divided into a long chain of identical repeating units called sarcomeres. The sarcomere is the structural and functional unit of skeletal muscle contraction. When sarcomeres get shorter, the whole muscle gets shorter. When sarcomeres get longer, the whole muscle gets longer. Everything below this paragraph is the inside of one sarcomere.
A sarcomere is bounded on each end by a dense vertical line of protein called a Z-disc (also called a Z-line). Z-disc to Z-disc is one sarcomere. Anchored to each Z-disc and reaching toward the middle of the sarcomere are the thin filaments. The thin filaments are made primarily of a protein called actin, plus two regulatory proteins (troponin and tropomyosin) that you will meet later in this reading. Filling the central region of the sarcomere are the thick filaments, made of a protein called myosin. Each thick filament is a bundle of myosin molecules with myosin heads sticking out along its length — the heads will become the cross-bridges that pull thin filaments inward.
Figure — From muscle fiber to myofibril to sarcomere: Z discs bound the sarcomere, with thin (actin) and thick (myosin) filaments overlapping between them.
The arrangement of thick and thin filaments in alternating, partially overlapping regions is what produces the striations you can see in a skeletal muscle under a microscope. The dark bands are called A-bands, and they correspond to the full length of the thick filaments (which absorb more light). The lighter bands are called I-bands, and they correspond to the regions where only thin filaments are present. In the middle of each A-band is a slightly lighter region called the H-zone — the part of the thick filaments where no thin filament has yet reached. The very center of the H-zone has a protein anchor called the M-line that holds the thick filaments in position.
Figure — The sarcomere, the region from one Z-line to the next Z-line, is the functional unit of a skeletal muscle fiber.
A useful trick for remembering: the I-band is thIn only; the H-zone is tHick only; everywhere else is both.
💡 TWO HUXLEYS, ONE THEORY
For most of the early twentieth century, anatomists could see muscle striations under a microscope but did not know what produced them or what the bands actually meant. The mainstream guess was that the proteins inside a sarcomere coiled up like springs when the muscle contracted — that the filaments themselves shortened.

In 1954, two papers in the journal Nature proposed something very different. One was written by Hugh Huxley, an electron microscopist working at Cambridge, with Jean Hanson. The other was written by Andrew Huxley (no relation), a physiologist also at Cambridge, with Rolf Niedergerke. Working independently, the two teams arrived at the same conclusion: when a muscle contracts, the filaments do not shorten. They slide past each other. Hugh Huxley had used the electron microscope to see that thick and thin filaments retained their length during contraction. Andrew Huxley had used an interference microscope to watch live muscle fibers and measured the same thing. Both papers appeared in the same issue. The model has been called the sliding filament theory ever since.

Hugh Huxley spent decades refining the details and produced the crucial 1969 paper that showed cross-bridges — the myosin heads — were the link between the two kinds of filament. Andrew Huxley won the Nobel Prize in 1963, mostly for unrelated work on nerve impulses (which you will study in Module 5, Nervous System), but his contribution here was just as foundational. Two scientists, same field, same name, same year, same idea, no relation. The sarcomere you are about to sculpt and animate in VR was decoded by them — the same Andrew Huxley you just met in Reading 4.02, working on T-tubules four years later.
A muscle gets shorter without its parts getting shorter
You just learned the parts of a sarcomere: two Z-discs at the ends, thin filaments of actin reaching in from each Z-disc, and thick filaments of myosin sitting in the middle. When a muscle contracts, the sarcomere gets shorter. But here is what is strange: the thin filaments do not get shorter, and the thick filaments do not get shorter. The Huxleys’ great insight in 1954 was that nothing inside a sarcomere actually contracts. The filaments slide past each other.
Picture two combs facing each other with their teeth interlocking. If you pull the combs toward each other, the teeth slide deeper between each other’s teeth and the two handles come closer together — even though no tooth on either comb has gotten any shorter. That is sliding filament theory in three sentences: the myosin heads on the thick filaments reach out, grab the thin filaments, and pull them toward the center of the sarcomere. The Z-discs come along for the ride, because the thin filaments are anchored to them. The sarcomere shortens. Thousands of sarcomeres in a single myofibril do this at once, and millions of myofibrils across a whole muscle do it together, and the whole muscle gets shorter.
This reading is about the machinery that makes the sliding happen: the cross-bridge cycle. It is a four-step molecular handshake between myosin and actin, repeated thousands of times per second, powered by ATP, and gated by calcium.
The cross-bridge cycle — four steps
A myosin head sticks out from the thick filament at an angle. Inside the head is a tiny pocket that binds ATP, and inside the same head is the binding site that grabs onto actin. The cycle has four stages.
Stage 1 — The head is cocked and ready
The myosin head has already split an ATP into ADP and Pi (inorganic phosphate). Both ADP and Pi are still tucked inside the pocket. The energy released by that hydrolysis is stored in the head as a kind of mechanical spring — the head is now cocked at a high angle, like the hammer of a pistol pulled back, waiting.
Stage 2 — The head binds actin (cross-bridge forms)
If a binding site on the thin filament is exposed and accessible (the calcium gate from the next section), the cocked head reaches over and locks onto it. The connection between a myosin head and an actin filament is called a cross-bridge. Each cross-bridge is a single molecular handshake.
Stage 3 — The power stroke
Binding to actin causes the head to release its Pi, and then its ADP. As those byproducts leave, the spring in the head snaps. The head rotates from its high cocked angle down to a lower angle — and because it is still locked to the thin filament, it drags the thin filament with it, toward the M-line at the center of the sarcomere. That motion is the power stroke. A single power stroke is a tiny tug — about 10 nanometers — but every myosin head along a thick filament is doing the same thing, asynchronously, hundreds of times a second, while their neighbors hang on.
Stage 4 — A new ATP releases the head
After the power stroke, the myosin head is still locked to the thin filament. To let go and reach for the next binding site, the head must bind a fresh ATP molecule. ATP binding (not hydrolysis — just binding) changes the shape of the head and releases it from actin. The head then hydrolyzes that ATP, cocks back up, and is ready to bind again, one binding-site farther along the thin filament. The cycle returns to Stage 1.
Figure — Skeletal Muscle Contraction. The myosin head binds actin at its actin-binding site, forming the cross-bridge; the power stroke pivots the head toward the sarcomere's center; ATP binds and the myosin head detaches; ATP hydrolysis cocks the head back into position.
The two roles of ATP, easy to confuse.
• ATP hydrolysis (splitting ATP into ADP + Pi) cocks the head back to its ready position.
• ATP binding (a new ATP plugging into the pocket) releases the head from actin.
When ATP runs out at death, the heads cannot release. They stay bound. The body becomes rigid: rigor mortis.
The calcium gate — what lets the handshake happen
If you re-read the cycle above carefully you will notice a gap. In Stage 2, the myosin head needs an exposed binding site on actin. But thin filaments do not just sit there waiting to be grabbed. In a resting muscle, each thin filament has two regulatory proteins wrapped around the actin: a long rope-like protein called tropomyosin that lies across the row of myosin-binding sites, and a complex called troponin that holds tropomyosin in place. With tropomyosin covering the binding sites, no myosin head can bind. The sarcomere is locked at rest.
The key that unlocks it is calcium. When an action potential reaches the T-tubules (Reading 4.02), the sarcoplasmic reticulum dumps Ca²⁺ out into the space around the thin filaments. Ca²⁺ binds to troponin. Troponin changes shape and pulls tropomyosin sideways, off the binding sites. The actin is now exposed. Any cocked myosin head in the neighborhood can bind, and the cross-bridge cycle begins.
Figure (a) — Myosin-binding sites blocked. At rest, tropomyosin lies across actin's myosin-binding sites, held in place by the troponin complex.
Figure (b) — Myosin-binding sites exposed. Ca²⁺ binds troponin, which pulls tropomyosin off the binding sites.
For as long as Ca²⁺ remains in the sarcoplasm, the cycle keeps running. When the nerve stops firing, the SR’s calcium pumps clear Ca²⁺ back into the SR within milliseconds. Without Ca²⁺, troponin lets tropomyosin slide back over the binding sites. The handshake breaks. The muscle relaxes.
🔮 CURRENT RESEARCH — DRUGS THAT TUNE MYOSIN ITSELF
The same cross-bridge cycle you just learned runs in cardiac muscle, too. In some forms of heart failure, the heart muscle is intact but the cross-bridges do not generate enough force per beat — the heart pumps weakly. The traditional treatments for this raise the calcium signal or change the nervous-system inputs to the heart, but those treatments also have heavy side effects.

Recent research has produced a new class of drugs called cardiac myosin activators — molecules that bind directly to cardiac myosin and stabilize it in a state that is more likely to form and complete a successful cross-bridge. Scientists are currently studying whether activators like omecamtiv mecarbil can improve heart function in patients without the dangerous knock-on effects of older medications. The clinical picture is mixed: some trials have shown improvement in heart-pumping measurements but no clear improvement in patient survival. The interesting thing for this lesson is the underlying logic: a half-century after the Huxleys figured out that muscle works by sliding filaments, scientists are now designing molecules that nudge a single step of the cross-bridge cycle. The basic science you just learned in this reading is, fifty years later, the design specification for new medicines.
What the contraction looks like under a microscope
If the sliding theory is correct, then during contraction:
▶ the I-band (thin filaments only) should get narrower — because thin filaments are sliding inward and the thin-only region is shrinking.
▶ the H-zone (thick filaments only) should get narrower — because the thin filaments are reaching deeper into the thick-only region.
▶ the A-band (full length of thick filaments) should not change — because thick filaments are not changing length.
▶ the distance between the two Z-discs — the length of the whole sarcomere — should get shorter.
Hugh Huxley confirmed every one of these predictions under the electron microscope. The visible behavior of the bands is the visible signature of the molecular cycle. This is what scientific theory does at its best — it makes specific, falsifiable predictions, and observed reality matches.
Figure — The Sliding Filament Model of Muscle Contraction. When a sarcomere contracts, the Z lines move closer together and the I band becomes smaller. The A band stays the same width. At full contraction, the thin and thick filaments overlap completely.

The four stages: (1) head cocked with ADP+Pi; (2) head binds actin; (3) Pi and ADP released, power stroke pulls thin filament; (4) new ATP binds, head releases.

ATP roles: hydrolysis cocks the head; binding releases the head from actin.

Calcium: unlocks tropomyosin so binding sites are available in the first place.
