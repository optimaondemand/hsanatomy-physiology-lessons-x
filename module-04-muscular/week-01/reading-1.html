Three Muscle Types — Skeletal, Smooth, and Cardiac

📖 READING
A body that moves only because muscle moves it
Last module you learned that the skeleton is a frame — a system of bones, joints, and connective tissue that gives the body its shape and protects what is inside. But a frame on its own does nothing. A skeleton on a hook in a biology classroom does not walk, swallow, breathe, or pump blood. Every motion of the human body — from a sprint to a slow blink to the squeeze that pushes a meal through the intestines — is produced by muscle tissue.
Muscle tissue has one chemical superpower: it can convert chemical energy (the energy stored in ATP) into mechanical work, in the form of a contraction. A muscle cell that is told to contract gets shorter, and as it gets shorter it pulls on whatever is attached to it. That is the only thing muscle tissue does. But because so much of the body is attached to muscle tissue — bones, organs, blood vessels, the heart itself — that one trick is enough to run almost every movement in the body.
There are three kinds of muscle tissue in the human body. They share that same chemical superpower, but they look different under the microscope, they live in different parts of the body, and they answer to different commanders. The first habit of this module is to be able to tell them apart by sight, location, and control.
Figure — The three types of muscle cell and where each is found in the body: cardiac (heart), skeletal (arm), and smooth (stomach).
Skeletal muscle — the muscle you can command
Skeletal muscle is the muscle you usually picture when you hear the word “muscle”: the biceps in your upper arm, the quadriceps in your thigh, the masseter you can feel clenching when you bite down. There are roughly 650 skeletal muscles in the human body, and together they make up about 40% of an average adult’s body mass. They attach to bone — almost always via tendons — and their job is to pull on the skeleton to move the body through space.
Skeletal muscle has four features you will use to identify it for the rest of the year.
▶ It is striated. Under a microscope, skeletal muscle cells show a precise pattern of repeating dark and light bands. The bands are the visible signature of the contractile machinery inside, which you will meet in Reading 4.03. Striations mean “this is muscle that uses organized sarcomeres.”
▶ It is voluntary. Skeletal muscle does not contract unless a motor neuron tells it to. The instruction comes from the central nervous system, ultimately from a region of the brain called the primary motor cortex (you will meet it in Module 5). You decide to clench your fist; the brain sends the order; the muscle contracts. This is the only one of the three muscle types your conscious mind has direct say over.
▶ Its cells are long, cylindrical, and multinucleated. A single skeletal muscle cell — usually called a muscle fiber — can be as long as the whole muscle. Some fibers in the sartorius muscle of your thigh are 30 centimeters long. Each fiber has dozens to hundreds of nuclei, parked just under the cell membrane.
▶ It attaches to bone almost everywhere it is found, and pulls on bone to move the skeleton. The bone markings you sketched in Module 3 — the tubercles, the epicondyles, the rough lines — exist because skeletal muscles attach there.
When this module talks about “the muscular system” without qualification, it almost always means skeletal muscle. The other two types matter, but they are usually covered as part of the systems they belong to.
Smooth muscle — the muscle of the hollow organs
Smooth muscle is the muscle of the hollow organs — the stomach, the small and large intestines, the bladder, the uterus, and the walls of every blood vessel except the smallest capillaries. It also surrounds the airways of your lungs and is found in the iris of your eye. Smooth muscle is everywhere your body needs to squeeze, push, or constrict something, without your having to think about it.
Smooth muscle’s features are nearly the opposite of skeletal muscle’s.
▶ It is non-striated, which is where it gets its name. Under a microscope the cells look smooth — no banding pattern. The contractile proteins are still there, but they are arranged in a looser, more diagonal mesh rather than the parallel rows of skeletal muscle.
▶ It is involuntary. You do not consciously command the smooth muscle that is moving your breakfast through your intestines right now — the autonomic nervous system does that, along with a long list of hormones. Smooth muscle answers to the nervous system in a different way than skeletal muscle: it can also be triggered by stretch (an intestine full of food contracts on its own), by hormones (oxytocin tells the uterus to contract in labor), and by local chemical signals.
▶ Its cells are short and spindle-shaped — pointed at both ends, fat in the middle — with a single nucleus per cell. They contract more slowly than skeletal muscle, but they can sustain a contraction for hours without tiring. The muscle in the wall of an artery is contracted right now and has been contracted, more or less continuously, every minute of your life. Imagine asking your biceps to do that.
Figure — Smooth muscle tissue, found around organs in the digestive, respiratory, and reproductive tracts and the iris of the eye. (Micrograph provided by the Regents of University of Michigan Medical School)
You will not study smooth muscle systematically in this module. You will meet it again in Modules 7 (blood vessels), 9 (airways), 10 (gut wall), 11 (bladder), and 12 (uterus). For now, the goal is to recognize it.
Cardiac muscle — the muscle found only in the heart
Cardiac muscle is the muscle of the heart, and only the heart. It does not occur anywhere else in the body. It combines features of skeletal and smooth muscle in a way no other tissue does.
▶ It is striated, like skeletal muscle. Under a microscope it shows banding — the same organized contractile machinery (you will meet sarcomeres in Reading 4.03) is at work.
▶ It is involuntary, like smooth muscle. You cannot decide to stop your heart. The heart has its own internal pacemaker (the sinoatrial node, which you will meet in Module 7) that fires roughly 60 to 100 times a minute without any outside input.
▶ Its cells are short, branched, and usually have one nucleus per cell. Cardiac cells are joined end-to-end at specialized junctions called intercalated discs, which contain both anchoring proteins and channels that let an electrical signal jump directly from one cell to the next. This is why the heart can contract as a single coordinated unit even though it is made of millions of separate cells.
▶ It does not fatigue. Cardiac muscle is packed with mitochondria — the structures that make ATP using oxygen — and it runs almost exclusively on aerobic metabolism. A typical heart beats roughly 2.5 billion times in an average lifetime and never takes a rest day.
Figure — Cardiac muscle: intercalated discs join adjacent fibers and contain gap junctions and desmosomes.
You will study cardiac muscle in detail in Module 7. The point this week is to recognize it as one of the three types and to know that it lives only in the heart.
