Skeletal Muscle Microscopic Anatomy — Inside a Single Fiber
Reading
📖 READING
A long zoom into a single muscle
In Reading 4.01 you learned the three types of muscle tissue. From here on, this module is about skeletal muscle — the kind you can command. The next step is to look inside a single skeletal muscle and zoom in until you can see, in your mind, the molecules that actually do the work of contracting.
A skeletal muscle — the biceps brachii, for instance — is itself an organ: many tissues working together. It is wrapped in a thick connective-tissue sheath called the epimysium. Inside the epimysium, the muscle is divided into bundles called fascicles, each wrapped in its own sheath, the perimysium. Inside each fascicle are hundreds to thousands of individual muscle fibers — the cells — each wrapped in a thin sheath called the endomysium. At each end of the whole muscle, all three connective-tissue layers blend together and continue past the muscle as a tendon, which anchors the muscle to bone. When the muscle contracts, the tendon pulls on the bone, and the bone moves.
Figure — The three connective tissue layers. Bundles of muscle fibers, called fascicles, are covered by the perimysium. Muscle fibers are covered by the endomysium.
Zoom one level further. A single muscle fiber is a long, cylindrical cell with hundreds of nuclei pressed against its outer edge. The cell membrane has a special name: the sarcolemma. (When you see sarco- as a prefix in this module, read it as “muscle” — it comes from the Greek for flesh.) Most of the inside of the fiber is taken up by long parallel rods called myofibrils. A single muscle fiber may contain thousands of myofibrils running its full length, packed shoulder-to-shoulder. The myofibrils are the contractile machinery — each one is itself built from a long chain of repeating contractile units. You will meet those units, and the proteins inside them, in full detail next reading. Everything else in the fiber — the nuclei, the mitochondria, the membranes — exists to support what the myofibrils do.
T-tubules and the sarcoplasmic reticulum
A muscle fiber is large. Some are 30 centimeters long, and a single fiber can be tens of micrometers wide. The contractile machinery is buried deep inside, but the contraction signal arrives at the cell membrane (the sarcolemma) at the outside. The fiber needs a way to carry the signal from the surface to every sarcomere, simultaneously, in fractions of a second. Two structures do this.
Transverse (T) tubules
T-tubules are inward extensions of the sarcolemma — tunnels that dive into the body of the cell at regular intervals along its length. When an action potential travels along the sarcolemma, it also runs into and along the T-tubules, carrying the electrical signal deep into the fiber within microseconds. Without T-tubules, the surface of the fiber would receive the signal and the inside would never know.
Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)
Wrapping each myofibril is a specialized membrane network called the sarcoplasmic reticulum. It is the muscle cell’s version of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum you have met in earlier biology, and it has one job: it stores calcium ions (Ca²⁺). The SR pumps Ca²⁺ in from the cytoplasm using ATP, so that in a resting muscle the Ca²⁺ concentration inside the SR is very high and the Ca²⁺ concentration around the sarcomeres is very low.
At regular intervals, the SR forms slightly expanded sacs called terminal cisternae that sit right next to a T-tubule. Two terminal cisternae plus one T-tubule between them form a structure called a triad. The triad is where electrical signal meets calcium release. When an action potential reaches the T-tubule, voltage-sensing proteins in the T-tubule membrane physically pull on calcium-release channels in the adjacent terminal cisternae, and the SR dumps Ca²⁺ out toward the myofibrils in a sudden flood. The Ca²⁺ flood is what triggers contraction — you will see exactly how, and meet the contractile unit it acts on, in Reading 4.03.
Figure — The T-tubule. Narrow T-tubules permit the conduction of electrical impulses. The SR functions to regulate intracellular levels of calcium. Two terminal cisternae and one T-tubule comprise a triad.
💡 FINDING THE TRIGGER POINTS
By the 1950s, electron microscopists had already photographed T-tubules and the sarcoplasmic reticulum — the structures were visible. What was not yet proven was whether those structures actually carried the signal to contract, or were simply along for the ride.

In 1958, physiologist Andrew Huxley — the same Andrew Huxley you will meet in Reading 4.03 for an entirely different discovery — teamed up with Robert Taylor to test it directly. Working with a single isolated frog muscle fiber under a microscope, they touched an extremely fine glass electrode to the outside surface of the fiber and delivered a tiny depolarizing pulse at one precise spot, then moved the electrode a short distance and repeated it, mapping the fiber millimeter by millimeter.

What they found was not a smooth gradient. Contraction happened only in a narrow local patch directly beneath the electrode, and only when the electrode landed on specific, evenly spaced points along the fiber’s length — the same spacing at which T-tubules had been seen entering the cell. Touch the electrode between those points, and nothing happened locally. The experiment showed, functionally, what the electron micrographs could only show structurally: the T-tubule openings are the entry points where a surface signal is carried into the depths of the fiber. Structure and function, confirmed by two completely different methods, agreeing with each other.
