Muscle Metabolism — Energy, Fatigue, and Fiber Types

A contracting muscle is an ATP machine
Every cross-bridge cycle costs one ATP. A single skeletal muscle fiber can run a few cross-bridge cycles in a second; a contracting whole muscle runs them by the trillion. ATP is the only currency the contractile machinery accepts — calcium opens the gate, but ATP powers the actual handshake. A muscle that runs out of ATP cannot relax (the heads stay stuck to actin), and it cannot fire again until ATP is restored. The way muscles get and replace ATP — their metabolism — is the topic of this lesson.
A resting muscle has only a small amount of ATP stored inside it — enough for about two seconds of activity. That is not enough to sprint across a room, much less run a kilometer. Muscle cells maintain that small ATP pool by making new ATP at exactly the rate they spend it. Three different systems make ATP, each one suited to a different intensity and duration of work.
System 1 — Creatine phosphate, the “ten-second battery”
Inside every muscle fiber is a high-energy molecule called creatine phosphate (CP, also called phosphocreatine). When ATP runs low, an enzyme called creatine kinase transfers the phosphate from creatine phosphate directly onto ADP, producing fresh ATP. This is called direct phosphorylation. It is the fastest way the cell can make ATP, because the phosphate is right there — no oxygen, no glucose, no metabolism required.
The catch is that the creatine phosphate supply is small. A maximally contracting muscle exhausts its creatine phosphate in about 10 to 15 seconds. That is the energy system powering the first part of a sprint, a single heavy lift, or a sudden jump. After that, the next system has to take over.
Figure (a) — Direct phosphorylation. Coupled reaction of creatine phosphate (CP) and ADP. Oxygen use: none. Products: 1 ATP per CP. Duration of energy provision: about 15 seconds.
System 2 — Anaerobic glycolysis, the “burns-but-no-air” system
If a muscle keeps contracting hard for more than ~15 seconds, it switches to anaerobic glycolysis. Glycolysis is the breakdown of glucose into pyruvate, and it produces a small amount of ATP per glucose molecule — 2 ATPs, to be exact. The word anaerobic means “without oxygen”: this pathway does not require O₂ to run. Under low-oxygen conditions, the pyruvate produced by glycolysis is converted into lactate (also called lactic acid), which builds up in the muscle.
Anaerobic glycolysis can sustain hard activity for about 30 to 60 seconds. It is much slower than creatine phosphate but it can keep going longer. The price is the lactate buildup, which contributes (along with other ionic changes) to the burning feeling of a muscle that is approaching exhaustion. The lactate itself eventually drifts into the blood, travels to the liver, and is recycled back into glucose — but that takes hours.
Figure (b) — Anaerobic pathway. Glycolysis and lactic acid formation from glucose. Oxygen use: none. Products: 2 ATP per glucose, lactic acid. Duration of energy provision: 40 seconds or slightly more.
System 3 — Aerobic respiration, the “all-day” system
For activities lasting more than about a minute — everything from a brisk walk to a marathon — the muscle relies on aerobic respiration. This is the same metabolic process you have learned in biology class. Glucose (and other fuels) are broken down in the mitochondria, and oxygen is used as the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain. The yield is large: 30 to 32 ATPs per glucose molecule, compared to 2 from anaerobic glycolysis.
Aerobic respiration is slower to ramp up than the first two systems — the mitochondria have to fire up, oxygen delivery has to increase — but it can run for hours, as long as fuel and oxygen keep arriving. This is the system that fuels everything from sitting still (your muscles are still doing some tonic work) to a long-distance run.
Figure (c) — Aerobic pathway. Aerobic cellular respiration of glucose, pyruvic acid, fatty acids, and amino acids. Oxygen use: required. Products: 32 ATP per glucose, CO₂, H₂O. Duration of energy provision: hours.
Figure — Muscle Metabolism. (a) Creatine phosphate provides ATP for about 15 seconds. (b) Glycolysis converts glucose to pyruvate; without oxygen, pyruvate is converted to lactic acid. (c) Aerobic respiration provides approximately 95% of the ATP needed for resting or moderately active muscles.
Muscle fatigue — what it actually is
A muscle that “burns” and stops working is not damaged. Fatigue is the muscle protecting itself. The exact causes are still being researched, but several mechanisms are well known.
▶ Ion imbalance. Repeated action potentials cause K⁺ to leak out of the muscle fibers and Na⁺ to leak in. The fiber’s resting potential changes, and action potentials become harder to fire.
▶ Glycogen depletion. Muscle cells store glucose as glycogen. After a long workout the stores can run out — the "wall" that marathon runners hit.
▶ Lactate / pH change. Heavy anaerobic activity drops the pH of the muscle fiber, which interferes with the enzymes of the cross-bridge cycle.
▶ Inorganic phosphate (Pi) buildup. Each cross-bridge releases a Pi. Accumulated Pi inside a fast-firing fiber interferes with myosin’s ability to bind actin.
The good news: fatigue is reversible. Rest, fluid, fuel, and oxygen restore the system within minutes (light fatigue) or hours (severe). The bad news: a muscle that is fatigued contracts weakly, which is why athletes train to push the onset of fatigue, not to ignore it.
Figure — The relative contribution of each energy system over time. As the ATP store and ATP-PC system fade, the lactic acid system takes over, and the aerobic system slowly rises to sustain overall performance.
Three fiber types — not all skeletal muscle is the same
Not every muscle fiber in your body is wired for the same job. Anatomists classify skeletal muscle fibers into three types based on speed of contraction and how they make ATP.

Every muscle in your body contains a mix of all three types, but the ratio differs. The soleus (the postural calf muscle) is mostly Type I. A sprinter’s gastrocnemius is enriched in Type IIx. Training can shift the ratio modestly — especially Type IIa ↔ Type IIx — but the broad split is largely genetic. This is one reason that some people are naturally suited to sprinting and others to endurance.
Not all muscle fatigue is temporary, though. In a group of genetic conditions called mitochondrial myopathies, the mitochondria themselves are defective — often from a mutation in mitochondrial DNA — and cannot generate ATP fast enough to meet a fiber’s demand. Nothing is wrong with the sarcomeres or the NMJ; the fuel supply itself is broken. Because Type I fibers depend almost entirely on aerobic respiration, they are hit earliest and hardest, producing exercise intolerance and fatigue far out of proportion to the activity — and because the mitochondria do not repair themselves with a few minutes of rest, this fatigue does not resolve the way ordinary fatigue does.
▶  Self-check: order the three ATP systems by how fast they produce ATP and how long they can run
Fastest, shortest: creatine phosphate (10–15 sec). Middle: anaerobic glycolysis (30–60 sec). Slowest, longest: aerobic respiration (minutes to hours). Each system takes over as the previous one runs out, like a relay handing off a baton.
