Exercise Physiology — Hypertrophy, Atrophy, and Use
Reading
📖 READING
A muscle that is used differently becomes a different muscle
You continue with the most observable physiological fact about skeletal muscle: it adapts to what you do with it. A muscle that is repeatedly asked to lift heavy loads gets larger and stronger. A muscle that is repeatedly asked to keep contracting for long periods becomes more endurance-capable. A muscle that is not used at all shrinks and loses force.
This is the principle behind all athletic training, all rehabilitation from injury, and every physical-therapy plan ever written. Muscle responds to the demand placed on it. The biology of how the response works is the topic of this reading.
Hypertrophy — muscles get bigger, but cells do not multiply
When a muscle grows larger through training, the growth is called hypertrophy. The surprising fact about hypertrophy is that the number of muscle fibers does not change. A muscle that grows bigger does not grow new cells. Instead, each existing muscle fiber becomes thicker — more myofibrils per cell, more sarcomeres per myofibril. The contractile machinery gets denser, and the fiber pulls harder.
The trigger is mechanical load. When a muscle is asked to lift a weight close to its maximum, the strain damages microscopic structures inside the fibers. The damage activates a small population of stem-cell-like cells living on the surface of each muscle fiber called satellite cells. Satellite cells fuse with the damaged fiber, donate their nuclei, and the fiber uses the extra nuclear material to synthesize more contractile protein. The result, over weeks and months, is a visibly larger and stronger muscle.
This is why heavy-resistance training (a few sets of low-rep heavy lifts) produces hypertrophy and high-rep, low-weight training does not, in the same way. The muscle has to be challenged near its limit to trigger the satellite-cell response.

Endurance training — same cells, more equipment
A different kind of training — long-duration, lower-intensity activity like running or cycling — produces a different set of changes. The fibers do not necessarily get bigger. Instead, the cells become better equipped for aerobic work:
▶ More mitochondria per fiber — more sites for aerobic ATP production.
▶ More capillaries woven around each fiber — better delivery of O₂ and fuel and faster removal of CO₂ and waste.
▶ More myoglobin inside the fiber — better O₂ storage, and more red color.
▶ Some Type IIx fibers convert to Type IIa — not toward Type I (you cannot easily make slow fibers slower), but toward more aerobic-capable fast fibers.
An endurance-trained athlete can run a longer distance at a faster pace than an untrained person, not because their muscles are bigger, but because their muscles can keep producing ATP aerobically for longer without fatigue.
Atrophy — the muscle that is not used
The opposite of hypertrophy is atrophy — the loss of muscle mass. Atrophy happens in three main settings:
▶ Disuse atrophy: muscles shrink when they are not loaded. Wearing a cast for six weeks — the limb inside emerges visibly thinner. Bedrest after surgery costs muscle mass at about 1% per day. Spaceflight does the same, in microgravity, even with exercise countermeasures.
▶ Denervation atrophy: muscles shrink when their motor neuron is destroyed. The fiber receives no contraction signal at all, and within months it has lost most of its contractile protein. This is what happens to muscles paralyzed by spinal cord injury — and, as you will see next lesson, to muscles whose motor neurons are attacked by disease.
▶ Age-related atrophy (sarcopenia): everyone loses muscle as they age, gradually, even with continued use. We will explore this in the research callout below.
The mechanism is largely the reverse of hypertrophy: protein synthesis slows, protein breakdown speeds up, the fibers shrink. Disuse atrophy is largely reversible once normal use returns. Denervation atrophy is largely irreversible unless the nerve regrows. Age-related atrophy can be slowed but not stopped.

🔮 CURRENT RESEARCH — SARCOPENIA AND THE AGING MUSCLE
Beginning some time in the fourth decade of life and accelerating after about age 60, healthy adults begin to lose muscle mass at roughly 1% per year, and muscle strength at an even faster rate. The condition is called sarcopenia — from the Greek sarx (flesh) and penia (loss). It is one of the most consistent and least-understood features of human aging. By age 80, the average person has lost 30–40% of the muscle mass they had at age 30, and almost half of the strength.

Scientists are currently exploring the causes. A few factors are well documented: there is a gradual loss of motor neurons in the spinal cord (so some muscle fibers lose their nerve supply and atrophy); circulating levels of anabolic hormones (testosterone, growth hormone, IGF-1) decline; satellite cells respond less robustly to training; and chronic low-grade inflammation interferes with protein synthesis. There may also be intrinsic mitochondrial decline that has not yet been fully mapped.

The strongest evidence-based intervention so far is the most straightforward: resistance training, even in people in their 80s and 90s, produces meaningful gains in muscle mass and strength. Combining resistance training with adequate dietary protein appears to help further. Recent research is exploring drugs that target specific pathways involved in muscle atrophy (such as myostatin inhibitors), but no medication has yet matched what consistent resistance training does on its own. The clinical interest matters: sarcopenia is one of the largest contributors to falls, hip fractures, and loss of independence in older adults. The same fibers you sculpted this module are what carry a person through the second half of life.
Use, adaptation, and what comes next
A muscle that has been asked, over and over, to lift, run, walk, breathe, chew, and stand — the muscle gets better at exactly that. Form follows function, in real time, across years of use. The molecular machinery (the cross-bridge cycle) is the same in every fiber from the marathon runner to the eight-year-old. The way it has been used and trained is what makes the difference. But adaptation has a dark mirror: what happens when the machinery itself breaks, whether from disease, injury, or poisoning? That is the subject of the last lesson of this module.
▶  Self-check: name three changes that resistance training produces in muscle and three different changes that endurance training produces
Resistance training: hypertrophy (thicker fibers); more myofibrils and sarcomeres per fiber; satellite-cell-driven protein synthesis; strength increase. Endurance training: more mitochondria; more capillaries; more myoglobin; Type IIx → Type IIa shift; better aerobic ATP production. Different stimuli, different molecular responses, different visible outcomes.
