📖 READING
When the machinery breaks
Every reading this module has described a system that works. This one describes what happens when it does not. Muscle can fail at any point in the chain you have spent three weeks learning: at the neuromuscular junction, inside the fiber itself, or at the level of the nerve signal that was supposed to arrive in the first place. Each condition below fails at a different point in that chain, and knowing where each one strikes is what lets you predict what it will look like.
Myasthenia gravis — the immune system attacks the motor end plate
In myasthenia gravis, the immune system produces antibodies that attack and destroy the nicotinic ACh receptors on the motor end plate you learned about in Reading 4.04. With fewer working receptors, less of the ACh released by the axon terminal ever produces an end-plate potential large enough to trigger a muscle action potential. The result is muscle weakness that gets worse with repeated use (as ACh at the junction is used up faster than the damaged receptor population can respond) and improves with rest. The eye muscles are often affected first — drooping eyelids and double vision are classic early signs — because those muscles fire almost continuously and have little reserve capacity.
Because myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune disease — the immune system attacking the body’s own tissue — it is treated with drugs that block acetylcholinesterase (leaving more ACh in the cleft to compete for the remaining receptors) and with medications that calm the immune response itself. You will study the immune system and antibodies in detail later this year; myasthenia gravis is a preview of how deeply that system reaches into every other one.
Figure — Normal neuromuscular junction vs. myasthenia gravis. Anti-AChR antibodies bind ACh receptors, triggering endocytosis and degradation and reducing muscle contraction.
Figure — Myasthenia gravis: acetylcholine antibodies block receptors at the neuromuscular junction, reducing muscle contraction and producing the drooping, weakened facial muscles typical of the disease.
Etymology: Greek myo- (muscle) + asthenos (without strength) + Latin gravis (heavy, severe) — “severe muscle weakness.”
Rhabdomyolysis — the fiber itself breaks down
Where myasthenia gravis attacks the signal, rhabdomyolysis destroys the muscle fiber directly. Extreme exertion, a crush injury, certain toxins, or severe overheating can rupture the sarcolemma of muscle fibers on a large scale. The contents of the dying fibers — including the oxygen-storing protein myoglobin — spill into the bloodstream. Myoglobin is small enough to pass into the kidneys, where in large quantities it can clog the delicate filtering structures and cause acute kidney injury. Dark, tea-colored urine is one of the warning signs clinicians watch for.
Rhabdomyolysis is treated as a medical emergency: aggressive IV fluids to flush the myoglobin through the kidneys before it can do lasting damage. It is a direct, structural failure — a reminder that the sarcolemma you learned about in Reading 4.02 is not just a passive wrapper. It is what keeps a muscle fiber’s contents where they belong.
Figure — Rhabdomyolysis: toxic muscular content, including myoglobin, leaking from damaged muscle fiber into the bloodstream. (The Hughston Foundation, Inc.)
Figure — The pathophysiology of rhabdomyolysis: myoglobin released from damaged muscle travels to the kidney, where it can obstruct the distal tubule and cause acute kidney injury.
Etymology: Greek rhabdos (rod) + mys (muscle) + lysis (loosening, dissolution) — “dissolution of striated muscle.”
Tetanus — the failure is upstream, in the nervous system
Tetanus is caused by a toxin (tetanospasmin) produced by the bacterium Clostridium tetani, which thrives in the low-oxygen environment of a deep puncture wound — the reason a rusty nail is the classic warning story, though the rust itself is not the danger; the depth and dirt of the wound are. The toxin travels to the spinal cord and blocks the release of inhibitory neurotransmitters that normally keep motor neurons in check. Without that inhibition, motor neurons fire uncontrollably, and the muscles they command lock into sustained, rigid contraction. The classic early sign is trismus, or “lockjaw” — the jaw muscles are small and fire frequently, so they are often affected first.
Notice something different about tetanus compared to the first two conditions: the muscle fiber and the neuromuscular junction are both completely normal. The failure is entirely upstream, in the spinal cord itself. This is why the tetanus vaccine (a toxoid, a deactivated version of the toxin) works by training your immune system to neutralize the toxin before it ever reaches the spinal cord — it is prevention aimed at the nervous system, not the muscle. You will meet inhibitory neurotransmission properly in Module 5, which starts right after this one; tetanus is the reason it matters.
Figure — Tetanus: a contaminated wound allows entry of Clostridium tetani bacteria, which release tetanus neurotoxin, producing severe hyperextension and spasticity.
Figure — Generalized tetanus: Clostridium tetani enters through a wound, and its toxin travels via peripheral nerve terminals to the spinal cord and brainstem, producing spasm of the jaw, facial, and neck muscles and, in advanced disease, complete tetanic spasm. (Source: Netter's Infectious Disease)
Etymology: Greek tetanos (tension, stretching) — the name describes the sustained, rigid contraction the toxin produces.
Chemical exposure — when the off-switch is disabled
Recall from Reading 4.04 that acetylcholinesterase is the enzyme that breaks down ACh in the synaptic cleft — the “off-switch” that lets a muscle relax between signals. Sarin and related nerve agents work by permanently disabling acetylcholinesterase. With no way to clear ACh from the cleft, every receptor on the motor end plate stays continuously stimulated. Instead of paralysis from a lack of signal, this is paralysis from a signal that never stops: muscles lock into sustained contraction, a pattern called flexion paralysis. Because the diaphragm is skeletal muscle too, the same mechanism that locks a fist closed can lock the breathing muscles, which is what makes nerve agent exposure lethal. The antidote drugs used in an exposure work by re-activating or protecting acetylcholinesterase — restoring the off-switch the poison disabled.
Two more categories worth knowing by name
Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the insulating myelin sheath around neurons in the brain and spinal cord, slowing or blocking the nerve signals that muscles depend on. Unlike myasthenia gravis, the target is the nerve fiber itself, not the neuromuscular junction. You will study myelin and demyelination in detail in Module 5.
Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a genetic disease of the muscle fiber itself. Inside every healthy skeletal muscle fiber is a structural protein called dystrophin, part of a complex that anchors the contractile machinery (the sarcomeres you drew in Lesson 4.03) to the cell membrane and the surrounding connective tissue. When dystrophin is broken, the membrane develops microscopic tears every time the muscle contracts. Over years, the damage accumulates faster than the muscle can repair, and the muscle fibers die. The gene for dystrophin is on the X chromosome; when a boy inherits a mutated copy, he develops DMD — progressive muscle weakness starting in early childhood.
Figure — Healthy muscle: dystrophin anchors the plasma membrane's protein complex to F-actin. In DMD, dystrophin is absent, and the complex disconnects from the cytoskeleton.
Figure — Healthy muscle tissue compared to Duchenne muscle tissue, showing fat and fibrotic tissue replacing dying muscle fibers.
🔮 CURRENT RESEARCH — GENE THERAPY FOR MUSCULAR DYSTROPHY
The dystrophin gene is the largest gene in the human genome, which has made it very difficult to replace using traditional gene therapy techniques. Scientists are currently exploring several different approaches. Exon-skipping drugs nudge the cellular machinery to skip past the broken part of the gene and read the rest, producing a shortened but partly functional dystrophin. A more direct approach uses an engineered virus (an adeno-associated virus, or AAV) to deliver a smaller version of the dystrophin gene called micro-dystrophin into muscle cells. Recent research has produced AAV-based gene therapies (one was conditionally approved by the FDA in 2023) that demonstrably increase dystrophin in patients’ muscles. Whether they translate into long-term clinical benefit is still being studied. The work draws directly on what you learned about muscle ultrastructure in Week 1: dystrophin keeps the sarcomeres anchored to the sarcolemma, and a single missing protein can dismantle a whole life of muscular function.
Where each condition strikes
▶ The nerve signal itself: tetanus (spinal cord), MS (myelin).
▶ The neuromuscular junction: myasthenia gravis (receptors destroyed), sarin (off-switch disabled).
▶ The muscle fiber itself: rhabdomyolysis (structural rupture), DMD (structural protein missing).
▶  Self-check: for each of the five named conditions, name the exact point in the contraction chain where it fails
Myasthenia gravis: ACh receptors on the motor end plate destroyed. Rhabdomyolysis: the muscle fiber membrane ruptures. Tetanus: inhibitory neurotransmission in the spinal cord is blocked. Sarin: acetylcholinesterase disabled, ACh never cleared. DMD: the dystrophin anchor between sarcomere and sarcolemma is missing.
