Muscles of the Pelvis and Lower Limb
Reading
📖 READING
The muscles that hold you up and move you forward
The lower limb does two things the upper limb does not: it supports your body weight, and it moves you through space. The muscles that drive walking, running, jumping, and standing are larger and more powerful than the muscles of the arms. This reading covers the major movers around the hip, the knee, and the ankle — seven muscles or groups in total. The architecture is the same as the upper limb: antagonists in front and behind every joint.
Figure — Leg muscles, anterior, lateral, and posterior views.
Gluteus maximus — the powerful hip extender
The gluteus maximus is the largest and most superficial of the gluteal muscles. It is the muscle that gives the buttock its shape. Its main job is to extend the hip joint — in other words, to pull the thigh backward. This is the motion you use when climbing stairs, sprinting, or rising from a deep squat. Walking on level ground does not require much from the gluteus maximus; running and climbing do.
Origin: posterior surface of the ilium (above the posterior gluteal line), the sacrum, and the coccyx.  Insertion: the iliotibial tract (a thick fibrous band on the lateral thigh) and the gluteal tuberosity of the femur.  Action: extends the thigh at the hip (powerful in climbing and sprinting); laterally rotates the thigh.
Iliopsoas — the hip flexor gluteus maximus answers to
If gluteus maximus pulls the thigh backward, something has to pull it forward again — that is the job of the iliopsoas, a combined muscle formed from two separate muscles (the psoas major, running down from the lumbar vertebrae, and the iliacus, running down from the inside of the ilium) that merge into a single tendon before crossing the front of the hip joint. It is the strongest flexor of the hip — the muscle you use to lift your knee toward your chest, to climb stairs, and to bring the leg forward with each stride of walking or running.
Because the iliopsoas crosses the front of the hip and attaches deep to the lumbar spine, a chronically shortened iliopsoas (common in people who sit for long hours) can tilt the pelvis forward and contribute to lower back strain — a direct link between a hip flexor and the erector spinae group you traced two lessons ago.
Origin: psoas major — transverse processes and bodies of T12–L5; iliacus — the iliac fossa on the inside of the ilium.  Insertion: lesser trochanter of the femur.  Action: flexes the thigh at the hip; the strongest of the hip flexors.
Quadriceps femoris — four heads, one tendon, one job
The quadriceps femoris (often just “the quadriceps” or “the quads”) is a group of four muscles on the front of the thigh. They all converge into a single common tendon that crosses the knee. The patella (kneecap) sits inside this tendon — technically it is a sesamoid bone embedded in the tendon — and the tendon continues past the patella as the patellar ligament, which attaches to the tibial tuberosity. When the quadriceps contracts, the patella is pulled upward and the patellar ligament pulls the tibia forward, extending the knee.
The four heads are named for their location and shape:
▶ Rectus femoris — the middle muscle of the four, the only one that crosses both the hip and the knee. Its origin is on the anterior inferior iliac spine, so it also flexes the hip — a second flexor working alongside the iliopsoas.
▶ Vastus lateralis — the lateral (outside) muscle.
▶ Vastus medialis — the medial (inside) muscle.
▶ Vastus intermedius — the deepest, between the rectus femoris and the femur.
Origin: rectus femoris — anterior inferior iliac spine; the three vasti — shaft of the femur.  Insertion: tibial tuberosity, via the patellar tendon (with the patella embedded).  Action: extends the knee; rectus femoris also flexes the hip.
Figure — The quadriceps femoris: rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, and vastus intermedius (not visible, deep to rectus femoris).
Hamstrings — the antagonists of the quadriceps
On the back of the thigh sit the three hamstring muscles. They are the antagonists of the quadriceps at the knee. When you flex your knee — bend the lower leg back toward the buttock — the hamstrings are pulling. They also assist the gluteus maximus in extending the hip. Most of the hamstrings originate on the ischial tuberosity of the pelvis, which is the bone you can feel pressing into a hard chair when you sit forward.
The three named hamstrings are: biceps femoris (lateral; two heads — long head from the ischium, short head from the femur), semitendinosus (medial, ends in a long thin tendon), and semimembranosus (medial, ends in a flat membranous tendon).
Origin (most): ischial tuberosity of the os coxa.  Insertion: biceps femoris on the head of the fibula; semitendinosus and semimembranosus on the medial side of the proximal tibia.  Action: flex the knee; extend the thigh at the hip.
Figure — The hamstring muscles: biceps femoris, semitendinosus, and semimembranosus.
Tibialis anterior — the muscle that keeps your toes from dragging
Run your fingers down the front, outer edge of your shin and you will feel the tibialis anterior, a long muscle lying right against the tibia. Its job is dorsiflexion — pulling the top of the foot up toward the shin — the motion that lifts your toes clear of the ground during the swing phase of every step you take walking or running. Without it, the front of the foot would drag with every stride.
A “fun” fact with a real clinical consequence: when the tibialis anterior is overused — a sudden jump in running mileage is the classic trigger — it can become irritated where it rubs against its surrounding connective-tissue sheath, causing the pain along the shin known as shin splints. The muscle you are about to trace is the one most runners mean when they complain about that pain.
Origin: lateral condyle and proximal shaft of the tibia.  Insertion: the medial cuneiform and the base of the first metatarsal (the top of the foot).  Action: dorsiflexes and inverts the foot.
Gastrocnemius — the calf muscle you can see
The gastrocnemius is the superficial muscle of the calf. Two heads at the top (from the medial and lateral condyles of the femur) merge into a single belly that converges with the deeper soleus into the Achilles tendon — the thick rope-like tendon you can feel at the back of your ankle. Because the gastrocnemius crosses both the knee and the ankle, it has two actions: it plantar flexes the foot (points the toes downward), and it weakly flexes the knee. It is the direct antagonist of the tibialis anterior at the ankle.
Origin: medial and lateral condyles of the femur.  Insertion: calcaneus (heel bone), via the Achilles tendon.  Action: plantar flexes the foot; weakly flexes the knee.
Figure — The gastrocnemius, the superficial calf muscle, anterior and posterior views.
Soleus — the deep calf muscle that just plantar flexes
Beneath the gastrocnemius is the soleus. Its origin is on the tibia and fibula (below the knee), so unlike the gastrocnemius, the soleus crosses only the ankle, not the knee. This means it can plantar flex the foot regardless of knee position — which is critical for standing. The soleus is also the muscle most responsible for the postural plantar flexion that keeps you balanced upright; while you stand, your soleus is firing tonically, much like the erector spinae of your back.
Origin: proximal tibia and fibula.  Insertion: calcaneus, via the Achilles tendon.  Action: plantar flexes the foot.
Figure — The triceps surae: the gastrocnemius (superficial) and soleus (deep), both inserting via the Achilles tendon.
The antagonist map for the lower limb
▶ Hip: gluteus maximus (extends) vs. iliopsoas + rectus femoris (flex).
▶ Knee: quadriceps (extends) vs. hamstrings (flex).
▶ Ankle: gastrocnemius + soleus (plantar flex) vs. tibialis anterior (dorsiflex).
Every joint, every motion, two-sided muscle control. This is now the third time this module has shown the pattern. The lower-limb muscles you just learned are the ones you use most heavily every day — with the right knowledge of where they live, you can also start to understand why specific motions, specific injuries, and specific kinds of training affect specific muscles.

Knee: quadriceps extend vs. hamstrings flex. Ankle: gastrocnemius/soleus plantar flex vs. tibialis anterior dorsiflexes.
