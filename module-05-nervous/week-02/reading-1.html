📖 READING
A three-pound organ that runs everything
Last week you met the cells of the nervous system and how they signal. This week you meet the organ where most of those cells live and do their work. The adult human brain weighs about 1,400 grams (roughly three pounds), occupies the cranial cavity, contains roughly 86 billion neurons, and uses about 20 percent of the body’s resting energy — even though it makes up only about two percent of body mass. It is, by a wide margin, the most metabolically expensive organ in your body.
Anatomists divide the brain into four major divisions. From bottom to top, oldest to newest (in evolutionary terms): the brainstem, the cerebellum, the diencephalon, and the cerebrum. Each has a distinct shape, a distinct location, and a distinct set of jobs. This reading walks them in that order.
Figure — The four major divisions of the brain: cerebrum, brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla), diencephalon, and cerebellum.
1. The brainstem — the part you cannot live without
The brainstem sits at the base of the brain, continuous with the spinal cord. It has three regions, stacked from bottom to top: medulla oblongata, pons, and midbrain. The brainstem does two big jobs.
▶ It runs the vital life-support functions you cannot consciously control. The medulla contains the brain’s respiratory centers (which control the rhythm of breathing), the cardiac center (which adjusts heart rate), and the vasomotor center (which adjusts blood vessel diameter and blood pressure). Damage to the medulla is usually fatal within minutes because breathing stops.
▶ It is the conduit between brain and body. Every nerve fiber that runs between the spinal cord and the higher parts of the brain passes through the brainstem. Ten of the twelve pairs of cranial nerves emerge from it. A small stroke in the brainstem can knock out widely scattered functions all at once, because so many critical pathways are packed into so little space.
The brainstem also contains the reticular formation, a diffuse network of neurons running through all three regions, which controls levels of arousal and the sleep–wake cycle. Damage here can produce coma.
Figure — The brainstem's three regions — midbrain, pons, and medulla — continuous with the thalamus above and the spinal cord below.
2. The cerebellum — the “little brain” that smooths movement
The cerebellum sits behind and below the rest of the brain, tucked against the back of the brainstem. Its name means “little brain” in Latin, and that is exactly what it looks like — a miniature, deeply folded version of the cerebrum perched at the back. Despite making up only about ten percent of brain volume, it contains more than half of the brain’s neurons.
The cerebellum’s primary job is the fine-tuning of movement. The motor commands you generate in the cerebrum (“reach for that glass”) are coarse and approximate. The cerebellum compares the command with feedback from muscles, joints, and the vestibular system, and adjusts moment to moment so the movement is smooth, coordinated, and the right size. People with cerebellar damage can still move — but their movements become jerky, clumsy, poorly timed, and overshoot or undershoot the target (a sign called dysmetria). Field sobriety tests work in part because alcohol disrupts cerebellar function.
The cerebellum also contributes to balance, posture, and certain kinds of motor learning — the slow refinement of skills like throwing a ball or playing a musical instrument. It does all of this by constantly comparing intended movement against proprioception — the ongoing, largely unconscious sense of where your own body parts are and how fast they are moving, reported by receptors in muscles, tendons, and joints. Proprioception is the raw feedback signal; the cerebellum is the structure that actually uses it, moment to moment, to keep movement on target. This is also why cerebellar damage and proprioceptive loss can look similar from the outside — both produce clumsy, poorly targeted movement — even though the underlying problem is different (a broken feedback signal versus a broken structure that reads the feedback).
3. The diencephalon — thalamus and hypothalamus
The diencephalon sits at the top of the brainstem, deep inside the brain. It is the structural and functional bridge between the brainstem below and the cerebrum above. Three structures matter most here.
▶ The thalamus. The thalamus is the brain’s sensory relay station. Almost every sensory signal heading to the cerebral cortex — vision, hearing, touch, taste, pain, body position — passes through the thalamus first and is routed to the appropriate part of the cortex. (Smell is the only exception; the olfactory pathway bypasses the thalamus.) The thalamus does not just relay; it filters and modulates. When you stop noticing the feel of your clothes after a few minutes of wearing them, your thalamus has done that.
▶ The hypothalamus. The hypothalamus is the body’s master homeostatic controller. It sits just below the thalamus and is connected by a stalk (the infundibulum) to the pituitary gland. It regulates body temperature, hunger and satiety, thirst, sleep cycles, sexual behavior, emotional state, and the autonomic nervous system. Critically, it also regulates a large share of the body’s endocrine function through its direct control over the pituitary gland — often called the “master” endocrine gland because so many other glands take their orders from hormones the pituitary releases. The hypothalamus is therefore the anatomical bridge between the nervous and endocrine systems: it is a nervous-system structure that talks in hormones. You will pick this connection back up in detail in Module 6.
Figure — The diencephalon: the thalamus, and below it the hypothalamus, connected by the infundibulum to the pituitary gland.
Figure — Homeostasis controlled by the hypothalamus: body temperature, hunger and satiety, sleep-wake cycle, hormone release, autonomic function, immune response, electrolyte balance, and thirst and fluid balance.
▶ The epithalamus. The smallest and least prominent of the diencephalon’s structures, sitting above and behind the thalamus. Its most important feature by far is the pineal gland, a tiny cone-shaped structure that takes up most of the epithalamus’s volume. The pineal gland secretes melatonin, a hormone that rises in darkness and falls in light, helping set your sleep–wake cycle to the day–night cycle outside. Like the hypothalamus, the pineal gland is a piece of nervous tissue that functions as an endocrine gland — another preview of Module 6.
4. The cerebrum — the largest and most recognizable part
The cerebrum is the largest part of the human brain — the wrinkled, walnut-shaped mass that occupies most of the cranial cavity and that most people picture when they hear the word “brain.” It accounts for about 80 percent of brain mass. It is divided into a left and a right cerebral hemisphere, joined by a thick bundle of myelinated axons called the corpus callosum that lets the two hemispheres communicate.
The outer surface of the cerebrum is the cerebral cortex — a sheet of gray matter only a few millimeters thick, but folded into ridges (gyri) and grooves (sulci) that triple its surface area inside the skull. The cortex is where conscious thought, memory, language, planning, and voluntary movement live. Beneath the cortex is white matter (myelinated axons) connecting cortical regions to each other and to subcortical structures.
Each hemisphere is divided into four lobes, named after the cranial bones above them: frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital. You will meet each lobe in detail in Reading 5.06.
💡 THE PATIENT WHO COULD ONLY SAY “TAN”
Paul Broca (1824–1880) was a French surgeon and anatomist. In 1861 he was called to consult on a patient at the Bicêtre Hospital in Paris named Louis Victor Leborgne — a man who, after a series of strokes, had lost the ability to produce speech. He could understand questions and seemed mentally intact, but everything he tried to say came out as a single syllable: tan. The other patients called him “Tan.”

Leborgne died a few days after Broca saw him. Broca examined his brain at autopsy. He found a damaged area in the lower left frontal lobe, behind and above the temple, in what is now called the inferior frontal gyrus. Over the next several years Broca examined the brains of other patients with similar speech-production problems, and found similar damage in similar locations — always on the left side. He proposed something radical for the time: language production is localized to a specific patch of the cerebral cortex, almost always in the left hemisphere.

The area is now called Broca’s area. Damage to it produces Broca’s aphasia: speech becomes effortful, halting, and ungrammatical, while comprehension is largely preserved. Broca’s observation was the first solid evidence that specific cognitive functions live in specific parts of the brain — an idea now called functional localization, on which essentially all modern neuroscience rests.
A few subcortical structures worth naming
Inside each hemisphere, buried under the cortex but above the diencephalon, are clusters of gray matter that play important roles. You should know two by name.
▶ The basal nuclei (often called the basal ganglia — same thing, slightly older name) are involved in initiating and smoothing voluntary movement, alongside the cerebellum. Parkinson’s disease is caused by the death of dopamine-producing neurons in part of the basal nuclei, which is why Parkinson’s patients have the slow, halting, tremulous movements they do. Huntington’s disease damages a different population of basal-nuclei neurons and produces almost the opposite movement problem: instead of too little movement, patients get involuntary, jerky, uncontrollable movements called chorea. Parkinson’s and Huntington’s are a useful pair to hold in mind together — the same structure, damaged in different ways, can push movement too far in either direction.
▶ The hippocampus and amygdala. The hippocampus, deep in the temporal lobe, is essential for forming new long-term memories. Damage here is the structural lesion behind certain kinds of severe amnesia. The amygdala, near the hippocampus, plays a central role in emotional processing — especially fear and threat detection.
Together with the hypothalamus and a few other regions, the hippocampus and amygdala form the limbic system — the brain’s emotion and memory circuit.
Putting the four divisions in proportion
If you were to look at a human brain from the side, the cerebrum would dominate — it covers the diencephalon almost entirely from above. The cerebellum would be a smaller mass at the back and below, tucked behind the brainstem. The brainstem would be a slender extension downward, continuous with the spinal cord. The diencephalon would be hidden inside, between the cerebrum and the brainstem. This is the spatial relationship you will lay out this week.
