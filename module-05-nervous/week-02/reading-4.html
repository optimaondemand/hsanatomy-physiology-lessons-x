Cranial and Spinal Nerves — The Wiring Diagram
Reading
📖 READING
Twelve from the brain, thirty-one from the cord
The peripheral nervous system in your body is built from two kinds of nerves, distinguished only by where they emerge.
▶ Cranial nerves emerge from the brain itself, mostly from the brainstem. There are 12 pairs, numbered with Roman numerals I through XII from front to back. Most cranial nerves serve structures in the head and neck. A few have functions deeper in the body — the vagus nerve (CN X) reaches the heart, lungs, and most of the gastrointestinal tract.
▶ Spinal nerves emerge from the spinal cord, one pair at every vertebral level. There are 31 pairs total: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, and 1 coccygeal. Spinal nerves are always mixed — carrying both sensory and motor fibers — because the dorsal and ventral roots merge before the nerve exits the vertebral column.
Figure — Overview of the peripheral nervous system: 12 pairs of cranial nerves arise from the brain and brainstem, and 31 pairs of spinal nerves arise from the spinal cord.
The twelve cranial nerves
Memorize the list. Each cranial nerve has a Roman numeral, a name, and a primary function. The classic mnemonic for the names is “On Old Olympus’ Towering Tops, A Finn And German Viewed Some Hops” — one word per nerve, in order.

Three of these deserve special note. The vagus (CN X) is by far the most peripherally distributed cranial nerve — you will meet it again in Modules 6, 7, 9, and 10 as it serves the parasympathetic nervous system. The optic nerve (CN II) is, strictly speaking, not a true peripheral nerve at all — it is an outgrowth of the brain itself and is wrapped in CNS meninges. The facial nerve (CN VII) is the one that goes wrong in Bell’s palsy, in which an inflamed CN VII produces sudden one-sided facial drooping.
Figure — The branches of the twelve cranial nerves, color-coded sensory (blue) and motor (red), and the structures they innervate.
Spinal nerves and their plexuses
Spinal nerves are numbered by the spinal cord segment they emerge from: C1–C8 (cervical — eight pairs even though there are only seven cervical vertebrae), T1–T12 (thoracic), L1–L5 (lumbar), S1–S5 (sacral), and Co1 (coccygeal). Each spinal nerve exits through an opening between two adjacent vertebrae — the intervertebral foramen, which you can see on the vertebrae you sculpted in Module 3.
Figure — The spinal nerve regions: cervical (C1–C8), thoracic (T1–T12), lumbar (L1–L5), sacral (S1–S5), and coccygeal.
Figure — A spinal nerve is mixed the moment it forms: dorsal (sensory) and ventral (motor) rootlets join, and the dorsal and ventral primary rami that branch off both carry sensory and motor fibers to skin and muscle.
In the thoracic region, spinal nerves run straight outward and become the intercostal nerves between the ribs — the wiring for your intercostal muscles (Module 4) and your trunk skin sensation.
In the cervical, lumbar, and sacral regions, however, spinal nerves do not run straight out to their targets. They form complicated braided networks called plexuses, in which fibers from multiple spinal levels split, swap, and recombine before emerging as named peripheral nerves. Four major plexuses matter for this course.
▶ Cervical plexus (C1–C4). Skin and muscles of the neck. Most importantly: it gives rise to the phrenic nerve, which carries the entire motor signal to the diaphragm. (“C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive.”)
▶ Brachial plexus (C5–T1). The upper limb. Gives rise to the median, ulnar, radial, axillary, and musculocutaneous nerves — the nerves that drive every motion in your arms. Injuries to the brachial plexus (sometimes during difficult births) can produce arm paralysis.
▶ Lumbar plexus (L1–L4). The front of the thigh. Gives rise to the femoral nerve (the quadriceps, the patellar reflex you learned in Week 1) and the obturator nerve.
▶ Sacral plexus (L4–S4). The back of the thigh and the lower leg. Gives rise to the sciatic nerve — the thickest and longest nerve in the human body, running down the back of the leg, branching into the tibial and common fibular nerves at the knee. Sciatica is irritation of this nerve, usually from a herniated lumbar disc compressing one of its roots.
Figure — The four major nerve plexuses: cervical (C1–C5, head and neck), brachial (C5–T1, upper limb), lumbar (L1–L4, abdomen and lower limb), and sacral (L4–S4, pelvis and lower limb). No plexus forms at T1–T12.
Dermatomes — a map of the body in spinal segments
Every spinal nerve carries sensory information from a specific patch of skin. The map of these patches across the body is called the dermatome map. For example, C7 covers most of the middle finger, T4 covers the skin over the nipples, T10 covers the navel, L4 covers the front of the knee, S1 covers the bottom of the foot. Clinicians use dermatomes to localize spinal nerve damage: a patient with numbness on the lateral foot has likely lost S1 sensation, pointing to a problem in the lower sacral region.
A related concept is the myotome — the group of muscles innervated by a given spinal nerve. Both maps together let a clinician examine a patient and read which spinal level is involved by which functions are lost.
Figure — The dermatome map: the skin segments supplied by each spinal nerve level, front and back. Adjacent dermatomes overlap considerably in reality.
▶  Self-check: how many cranial nerve pairs, how many spinal nerve pairs in each region, and which plexus contains the phrenic nerve?
Cranial: 12 pairs. Spinal: 8 cervical + 12 thoracic + 5 lumbar + 5 sacral + 1 coccygeal = 31 pairs. Phrenic nerve: cervical plexus (C3–C5).
