Reading 5.07
The Spinal Cord — Cross-Section and Function
Reading
📖 READING
A cable about the thickness of your little finger
The spinal cord is the second half of the central nervous system. It begins where the brainstem ends — at the foramen magnum, the large hole in the base of the skull — and runs down inside the vertebral column that you built in Module 3. In an adult human, the spinal cord is about 18 inches long (45 centimeters) and roughly the diameter of your little finger. It does not run all the way to the base of the spine: in adults, the cord itself ends at about the level of the first or second lumbar vertebra. The vertebral column continues further, but the contents below the cord’s end are nerve roots floating in CSF (a structure called the cauda equina, “horse’s tail”). This is the level at which a lumbar puncture is performed — below the cord itself, where there is no risk of damaging it.
Figure — The spinal cord in context: it begins at the foramen magnum, tapers to the conus medullaris around L1–L2, and continues as the cauda equina, with lumbar puncture performed below the cord's end.
The spinal cord has two big jobs.
▶ It is the conduit for information traveling between the brain and the body. Every sensory signal from below the head climbs up the cord to reach the brain. Every motor command from the brain to a muscle below the neck descends through the cord. The cord is, anatomically, a thick bundle of ascending and descending nerve fiber tracts.
▶ It is the integration center for spinal reflexes. The reflex circuits you met in Week 1 are wired through the spinal cord. The cord can act on its own, without input from the brain, in situations where speed matters more than thought.
The cross-section — gray matter, white matter, and a butterfly
If you cut the spinal cord across (transversely) and look at the cut face, you see something striking: a central H-shaped (or butterfly-shaped) region of gray matter, surrounded by a much larger region of white matter. The geometry is reversed from the brain — in the cerebrum, gray matter is outside and white matter is inside, but in the spinal cord, gray matter is inside and white matter is outside. Both colors mean the same thing they did before: gray matter = cell bodies and dendrites and unmyelinated parts; white matter = myelinated axons.
Figure — Gray matter and white matter in the brain vs. the spinal cord: the arrangement is reversed, with the spinal cord's gray matter buried inside its white matter.
Figure — Spinal cord cross-section: the butterfly-shaped gray matter (cell bodies and synapses) surrounded by white matter (myelinated axon tracts), with the dorsal root (sensory) and ventral root (motor) forming the spinal nerve.
The gray matter has four named regions, one in each “wing” of the butterfly. In a cross-section drawing, with the front of the body at the top:
▶ Posterior (dorsal) horns — the back wings. These receive sensory information arriving from the periphery. The cell bodies of the incoming sensory neurons themselves are not in the cord; they sit in a swelling called the dorsal root ganglion just outside the cord on each side. Their axons enter the cord through the dorsal root and synapse in the posterior horn.
▶ Anterior (ventral) horns — the front wings. These contain the cell bodies of motor neurons. Their axons exit the cord through the ventral root and head out to skeletal muscles.
▶ Lateral horns. Present only in certain spinal levels (thoracic and upper lumbar). These contain autonomic motor neurons that send fibers to smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands.
▶ Central canal. At the very center of the butterfly is a tiny canal filled with CSF, continuous with the fourth ventricle of the brain above.
Figure — A labeled spinal cord cross-section: dorsal, lateral, and ventral gray horns; dorsal, lateral, and ventral white columns; dorsal root with dorsal root ganglion; and ventral root.
Figure — Sensory in, motor out: the sensory neuron (blue) enters via the dorsal root and dorsal ganglion; the motor neuron (red) exits via the ventral root to reach muscle fibers.
Just outside the cord, the dorsal root (carrying sensory) and the ventral root (carrying motor) merge into a single spinal nerve. Every spinal nerve is therefore mixed — sensory and motor — the moment it leaves the cord.
White matter tracts — the cord’s highways
The white matter around the gray is organized into bundles called tracts, each a group of axons carrying related kinds of information. You do not need to memorize every tract for this course — there are over a dozen — but you should know the two big categories.
▶ Ascending tracts carry sensory information up to the brain. The two main groups are the dorsal column-medial lemniscus pathway (fine touch, vibration, proprioception) and the spinothalamic tracts (pain, temperature, crude touch). They run in different parts of the white matter, which is why certain kinds of spinal cord injury knock out some sensations but spare others.
Figure — The dorsal column system (fine touch, proprioception) and the spinothalamic tract (pain, temperature), each a three-neuron relay from the spinal cord to the postcentral gyrus by way of the thalamus.
▶ Descending tracts carry motor commands down from the brain. The most important is the corticospinal tract, which carries voluntary movement commands from the primary motor cortex (frontal lobe, Reading 5.06) down to the motor neurons in the ventral horns. Most fibers of this tract cross at the pyramidal decussation in the medulla, which is the structural reason for the contralateral organization you learned about last reading.
Figure — The corticospinal tract: upper motor neurons descend from the precentral gyrus, cross at the decussation of the pyramids in the medulla, and synapse on lower motor neurons in the spinal cord that reach skeletal muscle.
Spinal cord injury — why the level matters
When the spinal cord is severed or severely damaged, every signal that needs to cross the level of the injury is lost. The result depends entirely on where the injury occurs.
▶ High cervical injury (C1–C4). Both arms and both legs are paralyzed (tetraplegia, sometimes still called quadriplegia). Worse, the diaphragm — controlled by spinal nerves C3, C4, C5 — may also be paralyzed, and the patient cannot breathe without a ventilator. There is an old mnemonic in medical training: “C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive.”
▶ Lower cervical injury (C5–C8). The diaphragm is preserved; some arm function may be preserved depending on exact level, but the hands and the legs are paralyzed.
▶ Thoracic injury (T1–T12). Arms are spared; the legs and lower trunk are paralyzed (paraplegia).
▶ Lumbar / sacral injury. Less complete paralysis; specific motor and sensory deficits depending on which roots are involved.
Figure — Levels of spinal cord injury: higher injuries produce more complete paralysis, from tetraplegia at C1–C8 down to preserved walking function at S1–S5.
Spinal cord neurons do not regenerate. This is one of the most stubborn problems in clinical neuroscience, and one of the reasons spinal cord injuries are often permanent. The reasons have to do with the CNS’s glial environment: oligodendrocytes (which make CNS myelin) do not support axon regrowth in the way Schwann cells (PNS myelin) do, and astrocytes form a dense “glial scar” that physically and chemically blocks axon regrowth. Researchers are exploring ways around this — cell transplants, scaffolds, neurotrophic factors — but reliable functional recovery from a transected cord is not yet possible.
▶  Self-check: what is in the dorsal horn, what is in the ventral horn, and where does the dorsal root ganglion sit?
Dorsal (posterior) horn: sensory information arriving from the periphery (the synapses where incoming sensory axons end). Ventral (anterior) horn: cell bodies of motor neurons whose axons leave to skeletal muscle. Dorsal root ganglion: just outside the spinal cord, alongside it on each side — this is where the cell bodies of the sensory neurons themselves sit.
