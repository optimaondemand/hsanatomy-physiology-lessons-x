Cranial and Spinal Nerves — The Wiring Diagram
Lesson
Module thread — Courage. Twelve nerves leave the brain and thirty-one pairs leave the cord. Every signal that reaches the heart, the diaphragm, the hand — every sensation that arrives in the cortex from below the head — passes through one of them.
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Reading 5.08 — Cranial and Spinal Nerves: The Wiring Diagram
The substantive content for this lesson lives on the paired Reading page in Canvas.
Before you start the activities below, open Reading 5.08 and read it end-to-end — especially the cranial nerve table.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name all 12 cranial nerves in order, with their primary function and whether they are sensory, motor, or mixed.
2
I will be able to state how many spinal nerve pairs there are and how many in each region.
3
I will be able to name the four major spinal plexuses, what region of the body each serves, and one major nerve from each.
4
I will be able to explain what a dermatome is and use one to localize a spinal nerve.

⚡ TODAY’S ACTIVITIES (~90 minutes total)
1
Read Reading 5.08
22 min
1
Read end-to-end. Memorize the 12 cranial nerve names (the “On Old Olympus” mnemonic helps).
2
Cranial nerve infographic + your own mnemonic
15 min
1
In your notebook, sketch a one-page infographic for the 12 cranial nerves. From the table, pick and clearly highlight: 2 purely sensory nerves (from I, II, VIII), 2 purely motor nerves (from III, IV, VI, XI, XII), and 2 mixed nerves (from V, VII, IX, X). For each of your six, note its Roman numeral, its name, and its primary function in a few words.
2
Somewhere on the page, write your own mnemonic sentence (not the “On Old Olympus” one from the reading) for remembering all 12 cranial nerves in order. It only has to make sense to you — a mnemonic you built yourself is harder to forget than one you memorized from a page.
3
Reflection: why bother with a plexus?
10 min
1
In your notebook, respond in a short paragraph: A plexus is a complicated redistribution of nerve fibers after they exit the spinal cord — fibers from several spinal levels split apart and recombine before emerging as named peripheral nerves. Why not keep all the fibers from one spinal level together in a single bundle and send them straight out to their target? Speculate one benefit the plexus design gives the body, and consider what an injury at a single spinal level would do to a limb under each design — the straight-bundle version versus the real, redistributed version.
✏️ YOUR DRAWING SESSION THIS WEEK
This session adds the PNS to the brain and spinal cord you have drawn. You will draw the cranial nerves (on a view of the underside of the brain) and the spinal nerves (on your Nervous System Map).
▶ Cranial nerves (~15 min). On a fresh page, draw the underside of the brain, using the figure and cranial nerve table in Reading 5.08 as your reference. Draw and label all 12 pairs at their brainstem origin. Pay particular attention to (a) the optic nerve (II) leaving the eye, (b) the vagus (X) heading down the neck into the thorax, and (c) the trigeminal (V) spreading across the face.
▶ Spinal nerves (~15 min). On your Nervous System Map, draw the paired spinal nerves emerging from the intervertebral foramina at each spinal level. Mark at least one nerve at each of the four major levels (cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral). At the cervical plexus, highlight and label the phrenic nerve running down toward the diaphragm. At the lumbar plexus, highlight the femoral nerve. At the sacral plexus, highlight the sciatic nerve.
Keep your pages. By the end of this lesson your pages should show the CNS (brain + cord) plus the PNS (cranial nerves + spinal nerve plexuses). This is most of the assembly for your module-end Assembled Figure — only the sensory organs remain to add in Week 3.
