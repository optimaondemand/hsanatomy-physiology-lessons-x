The Cerebrum — Cortex, Lobes, Ventricles, and Meninges
Reading
📖 READING
A folded sheet of cells, the size of a dinner napkin
Last lesson you met the four major divisions of the brain. This lesson we zoom in on the part you usually mean when you say “the brain” — the cerebrum — and look at four things in detail: its outer cortex, the four lobes it is divided into, the ventricles inside, and the meninges that wrap it.
The cerebral cortex is a sheet of gray matter only two to four millimeters thick — about the thickness of a credit card — but if you could peel it off your brain and unfold it flat, it would cover something like 2,500 square centimeters, roughly the area of a large dinner napkin. The folds (gyri) and grooves (sulci) you see on the surface of any brain image are how all that surface area fits inside a roughly basketball-sized skull. Two big sulci you should learn by name: the central sulcus, which runs across the top of the brain, and the lateral sulcus (Sylvian fissure), which runs along each side. They are the main anatomical landmarks for dividing the cortex into lobes.
Underneath the cortex is white matter — bundles of myelinated axons connecting one part of the cortex to another and connecting the cortex to subcortical structures. The cortex is where the cell bodies live and the work happens; the white matter is the wiring.
Figure — Gyri and sulci are the brain's ridges and grooves, increasing cortical surface area within the skull.
The four lobes of the cerebrum
Each cerebral hemisphere is divided into four lobes. The boundaries are partly defined by the major sulci above, and partly by convention. The lobes are named after the cranial bones they sit under (which you drew in Module 3).
▶ Frontal lobe. At the front, under the frontal bone. Contains the primary motor cortex (just in front of the central sulcus) — the source of every voluntary movement command. Just in front of that lies the prefrontal cortex, the seat of planning, judgment, decision-making, working memory, and what we tend to call personality. (The famous railway worker Phineas Gage — impaled by a tamping iron through his frontal lobe in 1848 — survived but became, by his coworkers’ account, “no longer Gage”: erratic, impulsive, unable to plan. The frontal lobe is where the person you are mostly lives.) Broca’s area — speech production, almost always on the left — sits at the bottom of the frontal lobe.
▶ Parietal lobe. Behind the frontal lobe, under the parietal bone. Contains the primary somatosensory cortex (just behind the central sulcus) — the destination of all touch, pressure, temperature, and proprioceptive signals from the body. Also handles spatial awareness and numerical reasoning.
▶ Temporal lobe. Under the temporal bone, on each side of the head, below the lateral sulcus. Contains the primary auditory cortex (hearing) and the structures critical for memory formation (the hippocampus is buried inside the medial temporal lobe). Wernicke’s area, the part of the cortex responsible for language comprehension, sits at the back of the temporal lobe (almost always on the left). Damage to Wernicke’s area produces fluent but meaningless speech — the opposite of Broca’s aphasia.
▶ Occipital lobe. At the very back, under the occipital bone. Contains the primary visual cortex. Every photon you have ever consciously seen has had its electrical echo arrive here.
Figure — The four lobes of the cerebrum: frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital, shown with the central sulcus, lateral sulcus, cerebellum, pons, and medulla oblongata.
Figure — Functional areas of the cerebral cortex: primary motor cortex, primary somatosensory cortex, primary auditory cortex, primary visual cortex, Broca's area, and Wernicke's area.
There is also a fifth, less-talked-about region called the insula, hidden inside the lateral sulcus — involved in taste, interoception (your sense of the inside of your body), and certain emotional responses. You should be aware it exists but do not need to memorize its functions for this course.
Contralateral organization — why a left-hemisphere stroke weakens the right side
The cerebral cortex is organized contralaterally: the left hemisphere controls (and senses from) the right side of the body, and vice versa. Most descending motor fibers cross to the opposite side at the bottom of the medulla, in a structure called the pyramidal decussation. Most ascending sensory fibers cross at the same level or higher. The result: when a stroke kills tissue in the left motor cortex, the right side of the body becomes weak. When it hits the left somatosensory cortex, the right side loses feeling. Almost every emergency-room neurological assessment depends on this rule.
Figure — Contralateral organization: the right hemisphere controls the left hand, and the left hemisphere controls the right hand.
The ventricular system
Buried inside the brain is a connected network of four fluid-filled cavities called ventricles. From top to bottom they are: the two large lateral ventricles (one in each hemisphere), the third ventricle in the diencephalon, and the fourth ventricle between the brainstem and cerebellum. The fourth ventricle is continuous with the central canal of the spinal cord below it.
Figure — The ventricular system: the two lateral ventricles, the third ventricle, the cerebral aqueduct, the fourth ventricle, and the central canal.
The ventricles produce and contain cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), a clear liquid that bathes both the inside (ventricles) and the outside (the space between meningeal layers) of the brain and spinal cord. CSF is produced by a tissue called the choroid plexus, lining the walls of the ventricles. It does three jobs:
▶ Mechanical buffer. The brain floats in CSF; its effective weight inside the skull drops from about 1,400 grams to about 50. Without that buffering, the brain’s own weight would crush its lower regions.
▶ Shock absorber. CSF cushions the brain against the inner wall of the skull when the head is jolted. Even so, severe concussions still happen; the buffer is real but not perfect.
▶ Chemical environment and waste clearance. CSF helps regulate ion concentrations around neurons and carries metabolic waste away from brain tissue, with this clearance largely happening during sleep.
When CSF flow is blocked — for example by a tumor or a developmental abnormality — pressure builds inside the ventricles. The condition is called hydrocephalus. In infants, whose skull bones are not yet fused, this causes the head to enlarge visibly. In adults, it causes severe headaches, vision problems, and altered consciousness. Surgical drainage, often via an implanted shunt, is the standard treatment.
The meninges — three layers of protection
Surrounding the entire CNS — both the brain and the spinal cord — are three layers of connective tissue called the meninges. Memorize them from outside to inside.
▶ Dura mater (“tough mother” in Latin). The outermost layer. Thick, fibrous, leathery — the layer a neurosurgeon has to cut through to reach the brain itself. The dura is fused to the inside of the cranium and dips into some of the deeper fissures of the brain.
▶ Arachnoid mater (“spider-like”). The middle layer. A web-like sheet that drapes over the surface of the brain. Beneath it is the subarachnoid space, which is filled with CSF and contains the major blood vessels of the brain. A “subarachnoid hemorrhage” is bleeding into this space — one of the most dangerous kinds of stroke.
▶ Pia mater (“tender mother”). The innermost layer. A thin, delicate membrane that adheres directly to the surface of the brain and spinal cord, following every gyrus and sulcus.
Figure — The meninges, from outside to inside: dura mater, arachnoid mater (with the CSF-filled subarachnoid space beneath it), and pia mater, adhering directly to the cerebral cortex.
Infections of the meninges are called meningitis. Bacterial meningitis is a true medical emergency; viral meningitis is usually less severe. Diagnosis often involves a lumbar puncture — a needle inserted between two lumbar vertebrae (you drew these in Module 3) to draw a small sample of CSF for analysis. A related but distinct condition is encephalitis — inflammation of the brain tissue itself rather than its coverings, usually from a viral infection. Because encephalitis affects the cortex directly, it produces symptoms meningitis alone does not: confusion, personality change, and seizures, on top of the fever and headache the two conditions share.
🎯 CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH — BRAIN-COMPUTER INTERFACES
Because the primary motor cortex produces movement commands as predictable electrical patterns, researchers have been exploring whether those patterns can be read directly from the cortex and used to control external devices. This is the basic idea behind a brain-computer interface (BCI): implant or wear a sensor that detects motor-cortex activity, translate the patterns into control signals, and drive a computer cursor, a robotic arm, or a paralyzed person’s own muscles.

Both academic groups (BrainGate, Stanford, the University of California, and others) and private companies (including Neuralink) have published demonstrations of patients with severe paralysis using implanted electrode arrays to type, move a cursor, or operate a robotic arm by thought. Recent research suggests that a few hundred well-placed electrodes can carry enough information to allow communication at meaningful rates. The technology is still early, however: the implants are surgically invasive, the long-term reliability of electrodes inside brain tissue is an active question, and the FDA-approved use cases are limited to specific medical conditions. Scientists are currently exploring less invasive sensors and the question of how stable the brain’s motor representations are over time. This is real research with real patients, not yet a consumer technology — but it is one of the most active areas in clinical neuroscience right now.
