The Neuron at Work — Resting Potential and the Action Potential
Reading
📖 READING
A neuron is an electrical battery the body builds and then uses
Last lesson you met the neuron at the structural level — dendrites, cell body, axon, terminals. This lesson you meet it at work. The job is simple to state and remarkable to think about: every neuron in your body maintains, at all times, a voltage difference across its cell membrane. That voltage is a tiny battery, charged by the cell’s metabolism, and the cell uses sudden, controlled discharges of that battery to send signals down its axon at speeds of up to 120 meters per second.
This reading covers three ideas: the resting membrane potential (how the battery is charged and held), the action potential (how the battery is discharged in a precise wave that runs down the axon), and the role of myelin in making that wave travel much faster than it otherwise would.
The resting membrane potential: a neuron in its waiting state
When a neuron is not actively signaling, it is not doing nothing. It is actively maintaining its battery. A microelectrode placed inside an unstimulated neuron will read a voltage of roughly −70 millivolts relative to the outside — the inside of the cell is more negative than the outside. This is the resting membrane potential, the voltage every neuron returns to when it is at rest.
Two ingredients produce it.
▶ An uneven distribution of ions. The fluid inside the neuron is rich in potassium ions (K⁺) and in large, negatively charged proteins. The fluid outside the neuron is rich in sodium ions (Na⁺) and chloride ions (Cl⁻). These concentrations are nothing like equal, and that imbalance is the source of the voltage.
▶ A pump that uses ATP to keep it that way. The sodium-potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase) is a membrane protein that uses energy from ATP to push three Na⁺ out of the cell and two K⁺ into the cell, for every cycle. It runs constantly. Without it, the gradients would slowly equalize and the neuron would lose its ability to signal. A typical neuron spends a substantial fraction of its total energy budget keeping these pumps running. (For the body as a whole, the Na⁺/K⁺ pump consumes something like 20 to 30 percent of resting metabolic energy. The nervous system is expensive to maintain.)
Figure — The sodium-potassium pump cycle: 3 Na⁺ and 1 ATP bind, ATP hydrolysis expels the sodium, then 2 K⁺ bind and are released inside the cell.
At rest, the membrane is also slightly “leaky” to potassium — a few K⁺ ions can drift out through always-open potassium leak channels. As they leak out, they take positive charge with them, leaving the inside slightly more negative. The combined effect of the unequal distribution and the leak is the −70 mV resting potential.
Figure — Resting membrane potential (≈ −70 mV): Na⁺, Cl⁻, and large negative proteins are unevenly distributed by K⁺ leak channels and the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump.
Think of the neuron as a charged spring. The pumps coil the spring. The closed channels hold it in place. The neuron is ready to release the spring — but only when the right trigger arrives.
The action potential: a controlled discharge
When a neuron is stimulated strongly enough — usually by signals arriving at its dendrites — the membrane near the axon hillock depolarizes. If the depolarization reaches roughly −55 mV, called the threshold, the neuron fires an action potential. The action potential is an all-or-nothing event: either the threshold is reached and the spike fires at full size, or it isn’t and nothing happens. There is no “weak” action potential and no “strong” one — only fired or not fired. The way the nervous system encodes intensity is by changing how many neurons fire and how often, not how hard each one fires.
When threshold is reached, a precise sequence unfolds in about one millisecond:
1. Depolarization. Voltage-gated sodium channels snap open. Sodium ions, pulled in by both the concentration gradient and the negative interior, rush in. The inside of the membrane swings from −70 mV up past 0 mV and reaches roughly +30 mV — positive on the inside for the briefest of moments.
2. Repolarization. The sodium channels close (very quickly — they inactivate). At nearly the same time, voltage-gated potassium channels open. Potassium rushes out, carrying positive charge out of the cell, and the membrane potential plunges back down toward the resting voltage.
3. Hyperpolarization & reset. The potassium channels are slow to close, and the membrane briefly overshoots to about −75 or −80 mV — the after-hyperpolarization. The Na⁺/K⁺ pumps and leak channels then restore the resting −70 mV. The neuron is ready to fire again.
Figure — The action potential, phase by phase: resting potential, depolarization, repolarization, hyperpolarization, and the return to resting potential, with the corresponding ion movements at the membrane.
An action potential propagates along the axon because each patch of membrane that depolarizes triggers the patch next to it to depolarize. The signal moves down the axon like a wave of falling dominoes — never weakening, because the energy comes from the local battery (the gradient) at every step.
Figure — Propagation of the action potential: as one patch of membrane depolarizes and repolarizes, it triggers the neighboring patch to do the same, moving the signal down the axon.
💡 THE SQUID, THE GLASS PIPETTE, AND THE FIRST EQUATIONS OF THE BRAIN
The action potential as a series of voltage changes was not discovered by reasoning about it — it was measured. In the late 1930s, the British physiologists Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley went looking for a nerve fiber large enough to slip a microelectrode into. They found the giant axon of the squid, an unusually thick nerve fiber (up to 1 mm across) that runs along the squid’s mantle and helps it jet through the water.

World War II interrupted their work — both men spent the war years on military projects, including radar — but they returned to the squid axon in 1947. Over the next several years, working at the Marine Biological Laboratory in Plymouth, England, they used pipettes finer than a human hair to record the voltage inside and outside the squid axon during a single action potential. They identified the contributions of sodium and potassium separately, derived a system of mathematical equations describing the ion movements, and showed that those equations predicted the action potential’s shape almost exactly. They published the results in a series of papers in 1952. Hodgkin and Huxley shared the 1963 Nobel Prize. Their equations — the “Hodgkin–Huxley model” — are still taught today in every neuroscience program in the world.
Refractory periods: why signals only go one way
Right after an action potential, the affected patch of membrane cannot fire again immediately — the sodium channels are inactivated and need a moment to reset. This is the absolute refractory period. A short while after that, the channels are reset but it takes a stronger-than-normal stimulus to reach threshold — the relative refractory period.
This is why an action potential travels in one direction down the axon. Once a patch has fired, the patch behind it is in the refractory period and cannot be re-triggered. So the wave moves forward, away from the cell body, toward the axon terminals — never backward. This unidirectional flow is exactly what Cajal had inferred decades earlier, before anyone could measure voltages this way.
Figure — The refractory periods of the neuron. During the absolute refractory period, no new action potential can occur; during the relative refractory period, a stronger-than-normal stimulus can trigger one. These periods keep the signal traveling in one direction.
Myelin and saltatory conduction
In Reading 5.01 you learned that most axons are wrapped in myelin, a fatty insulating sheath made by oligodendrocytes (CNS) or Schwann cells (PNS). The wrapping is broken at regular intervals by gaps called nodes of Ranvier. Voltage-gated channels are concentrated at the nodes; the myelinated stretches in between have very few channels.
Figure — Myelination in the CNS vs. the PNS: one oligodendrocyte myelinates segments of many different axons, while each Schwann cell myelinates a single segment of one axon.
The result is that an action potential, instead of traveling smoothly down an unmyelinated axon, jumps from one node of Ranvier to the next. This is called saltatory conduction, from the Latin saltare, “to leap.” It is dramatically faster. A typical large myelinated axon in the human PNS conducts at about 120 m/s; an unmyelinated axon of the same diameter conducts at about 1 m/s. The same signal travels more than a hundred times faster if it is insulated.
Figure — Saltatory conduction in a myelinated axon vs. continuous conduction in an unmyelinated axon, and how conduction velocity scales with fiber diameter for each.
This is why diseases that strip myelin away — multiple sclerosis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, certain peripheral neuropathies — cause symptoms like weakness, blurred vision, and numbness. The neurons are still intact; the signals just can’t move at normal speed any more.
The big picture: where the signal goes from here
A single neuron can fire up to about 200 action potentials per second. The signal runs down its axon to the axon terminals, where it triggers the release of chemical messengers that cross to the next cell. That crossing is the subject of Reading 5.03 — the synapse.
