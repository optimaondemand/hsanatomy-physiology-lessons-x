📖 READING
The body’s information system
In Module 4 you learned that every voluntary motion of the body comes from a skeletal muscle pulling on a bone. But muscle never moves on its own. Something has to tell it when to fire, how hard to contract, and when to stop. That something — for every voluntary motion and a thousand involuntary ones a second — is the nervous system.
The nervous system is the body’s information system. It does three things in rapid sequence, billions of times a day: sense (gather information about what is happening inside and outside the body), integrate (combine that information with memory, experience, and goals to decide what to do), and respond (send a signal to muscles, glands, or other organs to act). Every other organ system in this course depends on it for coordination. The heart will not beat properly without nervous input. The lungs will not breathe. The gut will not push food through it. You will not turn the next page of this reading without it.
The nervous system also does something no other organ system does: it gives rise to experience. You see, hear, remember, decide, hope, and fear with it. A body without a working nervous system is a body without a person inside it. This module is, more than any other, the study of who you are at the level of biology.

Two anatomical divisions: CNS and PNS
Anatomists divide the nervous system into two parts, defined entirely by location.
▶ The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and the spinal cord. The CNS is where integration happens. It is also the only part of the nervous system that is encased in bone (the cranium and the vertebral column you built in Module 3) and wrapped in protective membranes called the meninges.
▶ The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is everything else — every nerve that runs outside the brain and spinal cord. The PNS is divided into cranial nerves (12 pairs, emerging directly from the brain) and spinal nerves (31 pairs, emerging from the spinal cord between vertebrae). The PNS is the wiring that connects the CNS to every other part of the body.
A useful image: think of the CNS as the head office and the spinal cord as the main trunk line, and the PNS as the field cabling that reaches every employee. Information comes in from the field, decisions are made at the office, and instructions go back out along the same lines. Every signal in the body that crosses the boundary between the two divisions does so at a specific anatomical point — either a cranial-nerve root in the skull or a spinal-nerve root between vertebrae.
Figure — The central nervous system (brain and spinal cord, pink) and the peripheral nervous system (yellow) shown on the body.
Two functional divisions: somatic and autonomic
The PNS can also be divided by what it does, not where it lives.
▶ Somatic nervous system. The somatic division carries signals to and from skeletal muscle — the voluntary movement system you studied in Module 4. When you decide to clench your fist, that command travels out through a somatic motor nerve. When your hand touches a hot surface and you feel the heat, that information comes back through a somatic sensory nerve. Somatic activity is mostly conscious.
▶ Autonomic nervous system (ANS). The autonomic division runs everything you do not consciously control: heart rate, blood vessel diameter, gut motility, glandular secretions, the size of your pupils, the contraction of smooth muscle in every hollow organ. The ANS itself has two opposing branches — sympathetic (the fight-or-flight branch) and parasympathetic (the rest-and-digest branch) — which you will meet in Week 3.
Figure — The autonomic nervous system's two branches, parasympathetic and sympathetic, and their opposite effects on target organs.
So every nerve in the body is classifiable two ways at once: by location (CNS or PNS) and, if PNS, by function (somatic or autonomic). A typical spinal nerve carries fibers of multiple types — sensory in from the skin, somatic motor out to skeletal muscle, autonomic out to blood vessels — all bundled inside a single anatomical structure.
The neuron — the cell that does the work
Nervous tissue, which you met briefly in Module 2, is made of two cell types. The neuron is the signaling cell. The glial cell is the supporting cell. There are roughly 86 billion neurons in an adult human brain and at least as many glia. We will come back to glia in a moment; first, the neuron.
A neuron is a cell shaped for one job: receive a signal at one end, transmit it down its length, and pass it to another cell at the other end. To do this it has four parts:
▶ Dendrites are short, branching extensions that receive signals from other neurons. A neuron in your cerebral cortex may have thousands of dendrites collecting input.
▶ The cell body (soma) contains the nucleus and most organelles. It is where the inputs from all the dendrites get summed and the “fire or do not fire” decision is made — specifically, at a region of the cell body called the axon hillock.
▶ The axon is a long, slender extension that carries the signal away from the cell body. Most neurons have exactly one axon. Some axons are short (a few millimeters). Some are remarkably long — a single motor neuron axon in your sciatic nerve can run from the base of your spine to your toe, more than a meter, all on a single cell.
▶ Axon terminals are the branched endings of the axon, which form synapses with the next cell — either another neuron, a muscle fiber, or a gland cell.
Figure — Structure of a neuron: cell body (soma), dendrites, axon hillock, axon, myelin sheath, node of Ranvier, and axon terminal.
Most axons in the body are wrapped in myelin, a fatty insulating sheath made by glial cells. Myelin lets electrical signals jump rapidly down the axon (you will see how in Reading 5.02). The wrapping is interrupted at regular gaps called nodes of Ranvier. White matter in the brain and spinal cord gets its color from the high density of myelinated axons running through it; gray matter is mostly unmyelinated cell bodies and dendrites.
Figure — Gray matter (mostly neuron cell bodies) and white matter (mostly myelinated axons) in a coronal brain slice.
💡 THE NEURON DOCTRINE
Santiago Ramón y Cajal (1852–1934) was a Spanish anatomist who did most of his work at his desk in Madrid with a microscope and a set of homemade pencils. In the 1880s the prevailing view of the nervous system was that it was a single continuous network — a kind of fused web in which signals traveled freely through interconnected protoplasm. Cajal stained brain tissue with a silver method he had refined from a technique invented by an Italian rival, Camillo Golgi, and drew, with extraordinary patience, what he saw.

What he saw was not a web. He saw distinct, separate cells — each with its own cell body, its own dendrites, its own axon — that came close to one another but did not actually fuse. From this he proposed the neuron doctrine: the nervous system is built from discrete cells (neurons) that communicate across tiny gaps. The signal moves in one direction within a cell — in through the dendrites, out through the axon — and a chemical messenger bridges the gap to the next cell. He was right about all of it. The doctrine is the foundation on which the rest of this module is built. He shared the 1906 Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine with Golgi — who, throughout the ceremony and afterwards, continued to insist that Cajal was wrong.
Glia — the supporting cast that is not optional
For most of the twentieth century, glial cells were treated as the connective tissue of the brain — the supporting cells that held the neurons in place. We now know they do far more. Without glia, neurons cannot survive, cannot signal, and cannot recover from injury.
There are six major types of glial cell, four in the CNS and two in the PNS. You do not need to memorize all six this week. Know these four:
▶ Astrocytes (CNS) are star-shaped cells that regulate the chemical environment around neurons, recycle neurotransmitters, and form a major part of the blood-brain barrier — the selective wall that keeps most substances in the bloodstream from entering brain tissue.
▶ Oligodendrocytes (CNS) make the myelin sheath in the brain and spinal cord. One oligodendrocyte can wrap segments of myelin around dozens of different axons at once.
▶ Schwann cells (PNS) make the myelin sheath in peripheral nerves. Unlike oligodendrocytes, each Schwann cell wraps just one short segment of one axon. Schwann cells also play a role in helping peripheral nerves regenerate after injury — one of the reasons cutting a finger nerve can heal while damage in the CNS often cannot.
▶ Microglia (CNS) are the immune cells of the brain. They patrol the brain tissue, clear debris from dying cells, and respond to injury or infection.
Diseases like multiple sclerosis attack oligodendrocytes (and the myelin they make), demonstrating just how essential these “supporting” cells are: when the myelin goes, signaling slows down, and the symptoms of MS — weakness, blurred vision, numbness — are what happens to a nervous system whose insulation is failing.
A road map for this module
Module 5 has three weeks. This week we stay at the cellular level: how a single neuron generates a signal, how that signal crosses to the next cell, and how a few neurons strung together can produce a reflex. Next week we move to the gross anatomy of the brain and spinal cord. Week 3 covers the autonomic nervous system and the sensory organs (eye, ear) that feed information in. Every week you will sculpt something new onto the figure you have built across Modules 3 and 4, until by the end of Module 5 you have a body with a complete nervous system layered on top of bone and muscle.
