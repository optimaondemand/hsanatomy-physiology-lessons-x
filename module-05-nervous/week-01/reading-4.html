Reflex Arcs — Survival Without Thinking
Reading
📖 READING
Pulling your hand off a hot stove before you know it is hot
Touch a hot stove. Your hand jerks back. By the time you feel the heat — really feel it, consciously — your hand is already gone. The withdrawal happens in roughly 200 milliseconds. The conscious sensation of pain doesn’t arrive at your cortex for another 100 to 200 milliseconds after that. You moved before you knew anything was wrong.
This is not magic. It is a reflex arc — the simplest functional circuit in your nervous system. A reflex arc takes a sensory signal, runs it into the spinal cord, and routes it to a motor neuron without ever sending it up to the brain. The brain finds out later. The point is that some decisions are too important to wait on.
The five components of a reflex arc
Every reflex, no matter how simple, has the same five components. Memorize them in this order:
▶ 1. Sensory receptor. A specialized cell or nerve ending that detects a stimulus — heat, pressure, stretch, pH, vibration, pain. (You will meet many of these in Week 3.)
▶ 2. Sensory neuron (afferent). Carries the signal from the receptor inward, toward the spinal cord. “Afferent” comes from the Latin afferre, “to carry toward” — toward the CNS.
▶ 3. Integration center. Inside the spinal cord (or sometimes the brainstem). This is where the decision is made. It may be as simple as one synapse directly onto a motor neuron, or it may involve one or more interneurons in between.
▶ 4. Motor neuron (efferent). Carries the “do this” signal out of the spinal cord toward the effector. “Efferent” comes from efferre, “to carry away” — away from the CNS.
▶ 5. Effector. The structure that responds — usually a skeletal muscle (which contracts) or a gland (which secretes). For most reflex arcs in your body, the effector is skeletal muscle.
Notice what is missing: the brain. A reflex arc does not require conscious awareness, and the brain’s involvement is optional and slow. The brain often gets informed of the reflex by a separate (ascending) pathway, but by the time the brain knows, the action is done.
Figure — The reflex arc: receptor → sensory (afferent) neuron → interneuron in the spinal cord → motor (efferent) neuron → effector.
Monosynaptic vs polysynaptic reflexes
Reflex arcs come in two basic complexities.
▶ Monosynaptic. One synapse total: the sensory neuron synapses directly onto a motor neuron. The classic example is the patellar reflex — the knee-jerk every doctor has elicited with a small rubber hammer. Tapping the patellar tendon stretches the quadriceps. Stretch receptors in the muscle fire, sensory neurons carry the signal to the spinal cord (at level L2–L4), and the signal jumps directly to motor neurons that fire the quadriceps, jerking the lower leg forward. One synapse. About 50 ms total.
▶ Polysynaptic. One or more interneurons between the sensory and motor neurons. The classic example is the withdrawal reflex — pulling your hand off the hot stove. Pain receptors in the fingertip fire, sensory neurons carry the signal to the spinal cord, and an interneuron there routes the signal to multiple motor neurons that fire the flexor muscles of the arm (biceps brachii, brachialis), pulling the hand back. The withdrawal reflex usually also involves a second interneuron that inhibits the extensors of the same arm (triceps brachii) so they don’t fight the contraction — a pairing called reciprocal inhibition: whenever a flexor is told to contract, its antagonist extensor is simultaneously told to relax, so the two muscles don’t work against each other. Polysynaptic reflexes can also coordinate the other side of the body — the crossed extensor reflex simultaneously stiffens the opposite leg when you yank one foot off a tack, so you don’t fall over.
Figure — Monosynaptic reflex (patellar reflex): one synapse, sensory neuron directly to motor neuron. Polysynaptic reflex (withdrawal reflex): one or more interneurons process the signal, including an inhibitory interneuron to the antagonist muscle.
Three reflexes worth knowing
You will refer back to these three reflexes for the rest of the year. Read each carefully.
Withdrawal reflex (polysynaptic, somatic)
Stimulus: a painful or damaging touch (heat, cut, sharp pressure) on the skin. Receptors: nociceptors (pain receptors). Path: sensory neuron → spinal cord → one or more interneurons → motor neuron to flexor muscle. Effect: flexor muscles contract, joint flexes, the body part jerks away from the stimulus. Survival purpose: limit damage from the offending stimulus before the brain has time to think about it. Speed: ~150–250 ms.
Figure — The withdrawal reflex: sensory receptors detect the painful stimulus, the sensory neuron carries the signal to the spinal cord, an interneuron routes it to the motor neuron, and the hand withdraws.
Patellar reflex (monosynaptic, somatic)
Stimulus: a tap on the patellar tendon, just below the kneecap. Receptors: muscle spindles inside the quadriceps that detect stretch. Path: sensory neuron → spinal cord at L2–L4 → motor neuron back to the quadriceps. Effect: quadriceps contract, lower leg kicks forward. Survival purpose: in everyday life, this reflex (called the stretch reflex when not elicited with a hammer) keeps your posture stable. When you wobble forward, your calves stretch, the same reflex fires, and your calf muscles brace you. Doctors use the patellar reflex to test the integrity of the L2–L4 spinal cord segments and the femoral nerve.
Figure — The patellar (knee-jerk) reflex illustrates a monosynaptic stretch reflex and reciprocal inhibition of the antagonist hamstring muscle.
Pupillary light reflex (polysynaptic, autonomic)
Stimulus: a bright light shining into one eye. Receptors: photoreceptors in the retina. Path: optic nerve → brainstem (not the spinal cord this time) → cranial nerve III → pupillary constrictor muscle. Effect: both pupils constrict (the consensual response — light in one eye constricts both pupils). Survival purpose: limit how much light hits the retina, protecting the photoreceptors from over-saturation. EMTs and neurologists check the pupillary light reflex to evaluate brainstem function in unconscious patients — a fixed, dilated pupil after head injury is a red flag for serious brainstem damage.
What the brain does after the reflex
A reflex does not happen in isolation. While the spinal cord is firing the motor response, the same sensory signal is also climbing up the spinal cord toward the brain along separate (ascending) pathways. About 100 to 200 ms later it reaches the cerebral cortex, and that is when you become consciously aware of what happened. The brain then does several things the reflex did not do: it identifies the cause (“the kettle was hot”), files the memory (“don’t touch that kettle again”), and may modify future behavior. The reflex saved you. The brain figures out what to do about it.
The brain can also modify reflexes after the fact. A trained boxer can suppress the blink reflex when expecting a punch. A pianist can suppress the withdrawal reflex when their finger slips into a hot light. These are not failures of the reflex — they are descending signals from the cortex to the spinal cord that inhibit the reflex circuit. The brain has veto power, but it is slow.
Why this matters: the diagnostic value of reflexes
Because reflexes have such well-known anatomical pathways, neurologists and emergency physicians use them constantly to test whether specific parts of the nervous system are working. A missing patellar reflex points to a problem in the L2–L4 segments, the femoral nerve, or the quadriceps. A hyper-active reflex points to a problem with descending inhibition from the brain (which normally damps reflexes a bit). A missing pupillary reflex on one side after a head injury points to brainstem compromise. Knowing the wiring lets the clinician read the symptoms back to the broken part.
