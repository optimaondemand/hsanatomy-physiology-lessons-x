The Synapse — How One Neuron Talks to the Next
Reading
📖 READING
The gap between cells — and why it matters
When Cajal proposed the neuron doctrine in the 1880s, he claimed that the nervous system was built of separate cells — not a fused web. That meant a signal traveling down an axon had to cross a gap to reach the next cell. He could not see the gap with his microscope. He inferred it. We now know it is real, it is real-named, and it is small — about 20 to 40 nanometers wide. That tiny space is the synaptic cleft, and the whole junction (the end of one neuron, the cleft, and the start of the next cell) is called a synapse.
Synapses are where the nervous system does most of its actual computing. An action potential is binary — on or off. But the strength, timing, and chemistry of a synapse can vary in many directions. The neuron on the receiving end may have thousands of synapses with different partners; it adds up the inputs and decides whether to fire. Learning happens by strengthening some synapses and weakening others. Drugs, hormones, and neurotoxins exert most of their effects by changing what happens at synapses. This reading covers what one looks like, what happens at one, and why this is the place to understand if you want to understand how the nervous system actually works.
Anatomy of a chemical synapse
There are two kinds of synapse — electrical and chemical. Electrical synapses are rare in humans; we will mention them briefly later. Almost every synapse in your nervous system is a chemical synapse, so that is what we describe in detail.
Figure — (a) A chemical synapse releases neurotransmitter across the cleft; (b) an electrical synapse passes current directly through gap junction channels.
A chemical synapse has three parts.
▶ The presynaptic terminal. This is the swelling at the end of an axon terminal of the sending neuron. Inside it are dozens of small membrane-bound sacs called synaptic vesicles, each one packed with thousands of molecules of a chemical messenger called a neurotransmitter. The terminal also contains voltage-gated calcium channels in its membrane and a large supply of mitochondria (vesicle recycling is energetically expensive).
▶ The synaptic cleft. The gap. Twenty to forty nanometers wide — small enough that you would need an electron microscope to see it. Filled with extracellular fluid plus a thin scaffolding of proteins that hold the two membranes in precise alignment.
▶ The postsynaptic membrane. The receiving side. On a neuron-to-neuron synapse, this is usually a dendrite or the cell body of the next neuron. On a neuron-to-muscle synapse (the neuromuscular junction you met in Module 4), it is the motor end plate on the muscle fiber. The postsynaptic membrane is studded with neurotransmitter receptors — protein molecules shaped to bind a specific neurotransmitter.
Figure — A chemical synapse in detail: the presynaptic terminal (vesicles, mitochondria, voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, SNARE proteins) and the postsynaptic membrane (receptors and ion channels).
What happens during a synaptic event
When the action potential travels down the axon and reaches the presynaptic terminal, a sequence unfolds in about one to two milliseconds.
1. Calcium rushes in. The arriving depolarization opens voltage-gated calcium channels in the terminal membrane. Calcium ions (Ca²⁺), abundant outside the cell, flood into the terminal.
2. Vesicles fuse and release. The Ca²⁺ binds to specialized proteins on the synaptic vesicles, triggering vesicle fusion with the presynaptic membrane. The vesicles dump their neurotransmitter into the cleft. Each vesicle is a small “quantal” release; a single action potential may release many vesicles at once.
3. Neurotransmitter crosses the cleft. The neurotransmitter molecules diffuse across the cleft — a journey of microseconds because the distance is so short.
4. Receptor binding. The neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane. The binding opens (or closes) ion channels on the postsynaptic cell, changing its membrane potential.
5. Signal is cleared. The neurotransmitter is removed from the cleft — usually by enzymatic breakdown (acetylcholinesterase breaks down ACh in the neuromuscular junction within milliseconds), reuptake into the presynaptic terminal, or diffusion away. Without this clearance step, the synapse would stay activated and the signal would lose its precision.
Figure — The five steps of synaptic transmission: action potential arrives, calcium enters, neurotransmitter is released, it binds receptors, and the signal ends.
Figure — How neurotransmitter signals end: enzymatic breakdown (e.g., acetylcholinesterase), reuptake into the presynaptic terminal, or diffusion away from the synapse.
This is why a chemical synapse is one-directional: only the presynaptic side has vesicles, and only the postsynaptic side has receptors. The signal can only flow one way.
💡 THE NAME “SYNAPSE”
The word synapse was coined by the British physiologist Charles Sherrington (1857–1952) in 1897. Sherrington spent his career working out the integrative behavior of the nervous system — how spinal reflexes are constructed from the simultaneous excitation and inhibition of many neurons at once. He needed a name for the special junction between two neurons that Cajal had inferred and electrophysiologists were beginning to characterize. He took the Greek word synapsis, meaning “clasping together,” and proposed it as a technical term. It has been the word ever since.

Sherrington also coined motor unit (a motor neuron and all the muscle fibers it controls) and proprioception (the body’s sense of itself in space). He shared the 1932 Nobel Prize with Edgar Adrian. His 1906 book The Integrative Action of the Nervous System is one of those rare scientific works that is still readable a century later. The central insight he repeated again and again is the one we keep returning to: the nervous system does not just transmit signals, it integrates them — and the place where that integration happens is at synapses.
Excitatory vs inhibitory: not every synapse fires the next cell
Receptor binding can have two opposite effects on the postsynaptic cell.
▶ An excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP) is a small depolarization — it brings the postsynaptic cell closer to threshold. The neurotransmitter glutamate, the most common excitatory transmitter in the human CNS, produces EPSPs by opening Na⁺ channels.
▶ An inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP) is a small hyperpolarization — it pushes the postsynaptic cell farther from threshold. The neurotransmitter GABA, the most common inhibitory transmitter in the human CNS, produces IPSPs by opening Cl⁻ channels.
Figure — Excitatory (glutamate, Na⁺) synapses depolarize the postsynaptic cell; inhibitory (GABA, Cl⁻) synapses hyperpolarize it.
A typical neuron receives thousands of synaptic inputs at any moment — some excitatory, some inhibitory. The cell adds them up at the axon hillock. If the net depolarization at threshold is reached, the cell fires an action potential. If the inhibitory inputs cancel out the excitatory inputs, it stays silent. This summation is the closest the nervous system gets to “deciding”: thousands of small votes, weighted by the strength of each synapse, summed in one place, judged against one number.
Figure — Summation at a neuron: excitatory and inhibitory inputs are added together at the axon hillock, and an action potential fires only if the total reaches threshold.
Neurotransmitters you should know by name
There are more than a hundred chemicals that act as neurotransmitters in the human body. You need to know six by name and function.
Neurotransmitter Main role(s)
Acetylcholine (ACh)Neuromuscular junction (excites skeletal muscle); many CNS roles in attention and arousal.
GlutamateMain excitatory transmitter in CNS; central to learning and memory.
GABAMain inhibitory transmitter in CNS; target of many anti-anxiety drugs.
DopamineReward, motivation, motor control; Parkinson’s disease is dopamine loss.
SerotoninMood, sleep, appetite; target of SSRIs.
NorepinephrineArousal, alertness, sympathetic nervous system (Week 3).
Why this matters clinically
Most psychiatric medications, most neurotoxins, and most recreational drugs work at synapses. Botulinum toxin (Botox), which you met in Module 4, prevents ACh release at neuromuscular junctions. SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) used for depression and anxiety block the reuptake of serotonin, keeping it active longer in the cleft. Cocaine and amphetamines increase dopamine signaling. Benzodiazepines (anti-anxiety drugs) enhance GABA inhibition, and general anesthetics used in surgery work on the same principle — enhancing GABA-mediated inhibition broadly enough across the brain to suppress consciousness. Opioids (such as morphine and fentanyl) bind receptors that inhibit pain-signaling synapses and, at high doses, synapses in the brainstem that drive breathing — which is why an opioid overdose is fatal: breathing simply stops. Naloxone (Narcan) works by displacing opioid molecules from those same receptors, reversing the overdose within minutes. Once you understand what a synapse is and how it works, the mechanism behind these drug classes is no longer mysterious — it is just chemistry happening at a very specific location.
Figure — Drug targets at the synapse: Botox blocks release, acetylcholinesterase breaks down ACh, Naloxone blocks opioid receptors, SSRIs block reuptake, and benzodiazepines enhance inhibitory (GABA) signaling.
