The Eye and Vision — Sensory Organs I

📖 READING
A camera made of living tissue
Up to now we have focused on the nervous system itself — cells, brain, cord, autonomic wiring. The last three lessons of this module turn outward to the sensory organs, the specialized structures that gather information from the world and feed it into the nervous system. This lesson covers the eye. Next lesson, the ear and the chemical senses. The lesson after that ties everything together.
The eye is a biological camera. Light from the world passes through a transparent front window, gets focused by an adjustable lens onto a curved sheet of photoreceptors at the back, and is converted into a stream of nerve impulses that the brain reconstructs as vision. Each step in that chain is built from specific anatomical structures you can draw by name.
Figure — The human eye: light enters through the cornea and pupil, is focused by the lens onto the retina, and rod and cone photoreceptors convert it into nerve signals carried by the optic nerve.
External anatomy — the accessory structures
Each eyeball sits inside a bony socket called the orbit, formed by seven cranial and facial bones (you drew them in Module 3). Several accessory structures protect and move the eye.
▶ Eyelids and eyelashes. Mechanical protection. The blink reflex sweeps a layer of fluid across the cornea every few seconds.
▶ Lacrimal gland (tear gland). Sits above the lateral corner of each eye. Produces tears that bathe the eye, drain across the cornea, and flow into the nasal cavity through the nasolacrimal duct — which is why crying gives you a runny nose.
▶ Six extraocular muscles. Skeletal muscles that move the eyeball: the four rectus muscles (superior, inferior, medial, lateral) and the two oblique muscles (superior, inferior). They are controlled by three cranial nerves: III (most of them), IV (superior oblique only), and VI (lateral rectus only). When you read that mnemonic LR6 SO4 R3 in a neurology text, this is what it means.
The three layers of the eyeball
The eyeball itself is built from three concentric layers, called tunics.
▶ Fibrous tunic (outer). The tough, protective outer layer. Mostly the white sclera (the “whites of the eyes”), with the transparent cornea covering the front. The cornea is the eye’s first lens — it does about two-thirds of the eye’s total focusing, and it does it without changing shape.
▶ Vascular tunic (middle), also called the uvea. Has three parts. The choroid is a heavily pigmented, vascular layer that lines most of the back of the eye, absorbs stray light, and supplies blood to the retina. The ciliary body contains smooth muscle that changes the shape of the lens (accommodation) and produces the fluid that fills the front of the eye. The iris is the colored disc at the front, with a hole in the middle (the pupil). It contains smooth muscle that constricts and dilates the pupil to control how much light enters.
▶ Neural tunic (inner), also called the retina. The thin innermost layer that lines the back of the eyeball. This is where light is converted to nerve signals. See below.
The lens and the chambers
Behind the iris sits the lens, a transparent, flexible biconvex structure held in place by suspensory ligaments attached to the ciliary body. The lens does the eye’s fine focusing — accommodation. When the ciliary muscle contracts, the lens becomes more rounded and focuses light from nearby objects. When the ciliary muscle relaxes, the lens flattens and focuses light from distant objects. As we age, the lens becomes less flexible (the condition called presbyopia), which is why most people need reading glasses in their forties or fifties.
The interior of the eye is divided into two compartments. In front of the lens are the small anterior and posterior chambers, filled with watery aqueous humor. Behind the lens, filling most of the eyeball, is a much larger cavity filled with gel-like vitreous humor. Both substances are transparent so light can pass through them on its way to the retina. Aqueous humor is constantly produced and drained; if drainage is impaired, pressure inside the eye rises — the condition called glaucoma, a leading cause of blindness worldwide.
Figure — Accommodation: for distant vision the ciliary muscles relax and the lens flattens; for near vision the ciliary muscles contract, the suspensory ligaments relax, and the lens rounds and thickens.
The retina and how light becomes nerve signal
The retina is the eye’s photoreceptive surface. It contains two types of photoreceptor cells.
▶ Rods are extremely light-sensitive but do not detect color. They are responsible for night vision and for detecting motion in your peripheral vision. There are roughly 90 million rods per retina.
▶ Cones are less light-sensitive but detect color and produce sharp vision. There are three types — sensitive to long (red), medium (green), and short (blue) wavelengths — and the combination of their signals lets you see the millions of colors a human eye can discriminate. There are about 6 million cones per retina, packed densely at one central spot called the fovea.
Figure — Rods vs. cones: night vision, no color, very light-sensitive, peripheral retina, low acuity vs. daylight vision, color vision, less light-sensitive, concentrated at the fovea, high acuity.
When light strikes a rod or cone, it triggers a biochemical cascade that changes the cell’s membrane potential. (The mechanism — involving a pigment called rhodopsin in rods and three opsins in cones — is one of the most thoroughly studied signaling cascades in biology, but you do not need to memorize the chemistry for this course.) The photoreceptor passes its signal to a chain of cells in the retina (bipolar cells, then ganglion cells), and the axons of the ganglion cells gather into a bundle that exits the back of the eyeball as the optic nerve (cranial nerve II). The point where the optic nerve leaves the retina has no photoreceptors — this is the blind spot in each eye. You don’t notice it because the brain fills in the missing region from context and from the other eye.
From the optic nerve, signals travel back through the optic chiasm (where the nerves from the two eyes partially cross) to the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, and from there to the primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe — the destination you learned about in Week 2.
Figure — The path of light: cornea → aqueous humor → pupil → lens → vitreous humor → retina, where rods and cones convert it to nerve signals carried by the optic nerve to the brain.
🎯 CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH — RESTORING VISION
In certain forms of inherited blindness — retinitis pigmentosa and age-related macular degeneration — the photoreceptors die or stop functioning, while the rest of the retina (bipolar and ganglion cells) and the optic nerve often remain intact. Researchers have been exploring two strategies for restoring vision in these patients.

Retinal prosthetics are implanted electrode arrays placed on or near the retina that stimulate the surviving ganglion cells directly with electrical pulses, bypassing the dead photoreceptors. Some such implants have received regulatory approval and have been used in patients, with results that are presently modest — patients can perceive light, motion, and large shapes, but not the detail required to read or recognize faces. Newer, higher-resolution implants are in active development.

Gene therapy has had more recent clinical impact. A gene therapy called voretigene neparvovec (Luxturna) was approved in the United States for a specific form of inherited retinal dystrophy caused by mutations in the RPE65 gene. It uses a viral vector to deliver a working copy of the gene to retinal cells. Recent research suggests that for some patients with this specific mutation, the therapy meaningfully improves low-light vision. Scientists are currently exploring gene therapies for other inherited retinal diseases, and the field is changing rapidly — the answers will look different by the time today’s high schoolers are practicing medicine.
The pupillary light reflex revisited
Recall the pupillary light reflex from Week 1. You can now see its full anatomical path: light hits the retina, ganglion cells send the signal up the optic nerve to a structure in the midbrain (not all the way to the cortex), which signals back via the oculomotor nerve (CN III, parasympathetic outflow) to the iris constrictor muscle, and the pupil constricts. Because the signal connects to both eyes’ midbrain structures, the response is consensual: light in one eye constricts both pupils.
Figure — The pupillary light reflex pathway: the afferent pathway carries the signal from the retina via the optic nerve to the pretectal nucleus; the efferent pathway carries it back via the oculomotor nerve's parasympathetic fibers and the ciliary ganglion to both eyes' iris constrictor muscles.
▶  Self-check: name the three tunics of the eyeball, the two types of photoreceptor, and the cranial nerve that carries vision out
Tunics: fibrous (sclera + cornea), vascular / uvea (choroid + ciliary body + iris), neural (retina). Photoreceptors: rods (light-sensitive, no color) and cones (less sensitive, three color types). Vision out: optic nerve, cranial nerve II.
