📖 READING
The sense that covers the whole body
The four special senses you just met — vision, hearing, taste, smell — each have a dedicated organ and a dedicated cranial nerve. The fifth and most diffuse sensory system is the one most people forget to count: somatosensation, the sense of the body itself. It does not have one organ. It is distributed across every square centimeter of your skin and through every muscle, tendon, and joint. It tells you, second by second, what is touching you, what temperature it is, whether it hurts, and exactly where every part of your body is in space. It is the closing of the nervous-system loop — the sense that lets you know your own body well enough to act in the world.
The five submodalities of somatosensation
Somatosensation is not one sense but a family of five, each carried by its own kind of receptor.
▶ Touch and pressure — detected by several types of mechanoreceptors in the skin. Some respond best to light touch (Meissner’s corpuscles), some to deep pressure or vibration (Pacinian corpuscles), some to sustained pressure (Merkel discs), some to stretch (Ruffini endings). The receptors are most dense in the fingertips, lips, and tongue — the regions you use to explore the world.
▶ Temperature — detected by thermoreceptors. There are separate “warm” and “cold” receptors, each responding to a different temperature range. They do not measure absolute temperature accurately — they fire most when the skin temperature changes. (This is why a pool that felt cold when you first jumped in feels neutral five minutes later.)
▶ Pain — detected by nociceptors, free nerve endings that respond to potentially damaging stimuli (mechanical injury, extreme temperatures, certain chemicals). Pain signals travel along two kinds of fiber: fast, sharp pain along myelinated A-delta fibers, and slower, dull aching pain along unmyelinated C fibers. This is why a stubbed toe hurts twice — a fast sharp pang, then a slower throbbing ache.
▶ Proprioception — the sense of where your body parts are in space. Detected by muscle spindles (which sense muscle stretch — the same receptors that drove the patellar reflex in Week 1), Golgi tendon organs (which sense tendon tension), and joint receptors. You can close your eyes, raise one hand, and accurately touch your nose because your proprioceptors are constantly reporting your hand’s position. People who lose proprioception — from certain rare neurological diseases — have to use vision to control every movement and can only walk by watching their feet.
▶ Itch — once thought to be a kind of mild pain, but now known to be its own sensation with its own dedicated receptors and its own neural pathway. The reason scratching feels good is that the touch and mild-pain signals from scratching activate inhibitory interneurons in the spinal cord that briefly suppress the itch signal.
Figure — The submodalities of somatosensation and their primary receptors: Meissner's corpuscle and Merkel disc (touch/pressure), Pacinian corpuscle (vibration), thermoreceptors (temperature), nociceptors (pain), and muscle spindle (proprioception).
The somatosensory pathway
A typical touch signal from your fingertip takes the following journey, which combines almost everything you have learned this module:
▶ Receptor in the fingertip skin fires.
▶ Sensory neuron axon travels up the arm in a peripheral nerve (median or ulnar), into the cervical spinal cord through the dorsal root, with its cell body in the dorsal root ganglion.
▶ White matter tract — the signal ascends through the dorsal column of the spinal cord (for fine touch and proprioception) or the spinothalamic tract (for pain and temperature).
▶ Crossing point — somewhere along the way (in the medulla for the dorsal column, in the spinal cord itself for the spinothalamic), the signal crosses to the opposite side of the body. This is why your right hand projects to your left brain.
▶ Thalamus — the signal is relayed through the thalamus (specifically the ventral posterior nucleus).
▶ Primary somatosensory cortex in the parietal lobe — the destination. From here, the signal is processed and may activate motor commands or be stored as part of an experience.
Every named structure in that sentence is something you have built or drawn in the last three weeks. This path is the closing demonstration of what this module has been about.
Figure — The somatosensory pathway, shown for both major routes: first-order neuron → crossing point (medulla or spinal cord) → second-order neuron → thalamus → third-order neuron → primary somatosensory cortex.
The somatosensory homunculus
The primary somatosensory cortex is not laid out by body part in proportional sizes. It is laid out by sensory density: body regions with more receptors get more cortical real estate. The lips and the fingertips take up a huge fraction of the somatosensory cortex; the trunk, calves, and back take up very little. A diagram of this cortex made by “drawing” the body where its cortex sits is called a somatosensory homunculus, and it looks bizarre — an oversized face and hands attached to a tiny torso. The motor cortex, just in front of the central sulcus, has its own homunculus organized by motor density (also with disproportionately large hands and lips, the body parts that produce the most varied voluntary movements).
Figure — The sensory homunculus and motor homunculus: both are drawn in proportion to cortical territory, not actual body size, giving the hands, lips, and face their exaggerated scale.
Brain-body integration: the closing picture
Step back, finally, and look at what you have built this module. A whole nervous system: a brain with four major divisions and four cortical lobes; ventricles full of CSF; three meningeal layers; a spinal cord with its butterfly of gray matter and its tracts of white; 12 cranial nerves and 31 pairs of spinal nerves emerging into a sprawling peripheral net; a sympathetic chain and a vagus that handle every involuntary control loop; an eye and an ear with their dedicated cranial nerves; and an entire body surface that pours sensory information back up through cord and thalamus to a striped cortex.
All of it is connected. The motor cortex sends commands down through the corticospinal tract, crosses at the medulla, exits the ventral horn, joins a peripheral nerve, and arrives at the very same skeletal muscles you drew in Module 4 — making them contract. The pain receptors in the skin send signals up through the spinothalamic tract to the same cerebral cortex that decides what to do about them. The hypothalamus, which you met in Week 2, controls the autonomic system you met this week — and through that system it adjusts every other organ system the body has, all of them at once, ten times per second, every second of your life.
Figure — The nervous system's integrated pathways: every sense organ feeds through peripheral nerves, the spinal cord, the brainstem, and the thalamus to the cerebral cortex, which in turn drives somatic and autonomic motor output back to the body.
This is the level of detail at which a doctor diagnoses a stroke from a numb hand and a missing patellar reflex. It is the level at which a neuroscientist designs a brain-computer interface. It is the level at which you understand what is happening when you reach for a glass of water, recognize a friend’s face across a room, or pull your hand off a hot stove without thinking. You started this module knowing the body has a nervous system. You finish it knowing what one looks like, layer by layer, signal by signal.
In Module 6 you will meet the body’s second-fastest control system — the endocrine system — and learn how it works alongside the nervous system to maintain everything Walter Cannon called homeostasis.
