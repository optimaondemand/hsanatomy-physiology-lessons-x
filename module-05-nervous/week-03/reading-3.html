The Ear, Taste, and Smell — Sensory Organs II

📖 READING
Three more channels into the brain
Last reading covered vision. This reading covers the three other “special senses” that have their own cranial nerves: hearing and balance (both in the ear, both via CN VIII), taste (via CN VII and IX), and smell (via CN I). The ear gets most of the attention because it is the most anatomically complex.
The ear — three regions in a row
The ear is built in three regions, lined up from outside to inside: outer ear, middle ear, and inner ear. Sound entering the ear must pass through all three; balance happens in the inner ear only.
Figure — The ear's three regions: outer ear (pinna, ear canal), middle ear (eardrum, malleus, incus, stapes), and inner ear (semicircular canals, cochlea, vestibular and auditory nerves).
Outer ear
The visible pinna (also called the auricle) and the external auditory (ear) canal together make up the outer ear. The pinna funnels sound waves into the canal. The canal directs them to a thin membrane called the tympanic membrane (the eardrum) at its inner end. Sound waves vibrate this membrane.
Middle ear
Behind the tympanic membrane is a small air-filled cavity, the middle ear. Inside it sit the three auditory ossicles — the smallest bones in the human body — arranged in a chain: malleus (hammer), incus (anvil), and stapes (stirrup). They mechanically transmit the vibrations from the tympanic membrane to a second membrane (the oval window) at the entrance to the inner ear, amplifying the vibration in the process. The middle ear is connected to the back of the throat by the auditory (eustachian) tube, which equalizes air pressure on either side of the eardrum — the “popping” sensation you feel on an airplane is this tube opening.
Inner ear
The inner ear is a complex set of fluid-filled cavities and tubes carved into the temporal bone — collectively called the bony labyrinth. It contains two functionally distinct structures.
▶ The cochlea is the snail-shell-shaped structure for hearing. When the stapes pushes on the oval window, it sends a wave through the fluid inside the cochlea. The wave bends tiny hair cells that line a structure inside the cochlea called the organ of Corti. The bending opens ion channels in the hair cells, which fire and signal up to the cochlear branch of CN VIII. Different parts of the cochlea respond to different sound frequencies — high frequencies near the base, low frequencies at the apex — which is how the ear distinguishes pitch.
Figure — The path of a sound wave: the tympanic membrane vibrates, the ossicles amplify and transmit the vibration to the oval window, pressure waves move through the cochlea, and hair cells in the organ of Corti signal the cochlear branch of CN VIII.
▶ The vestibular apparatus is the part of the inner ear responsible for balance and head-position sense. It includes the three semicircular canals (one for each plane of head rotation) and two small chambers called the utricle and saccule (for sensing head tilt and linear acceleration). All four structures contain fluid and hair cells; head motion moves the fluid, bends the hair cells, and sends signals up the vestibular branch of CN VIII. This is why some people get motion sickness — conflicting signals from the eyes (which say “not moving”) and the vestibular system (which says “moving”) confuse the brain.
Figure — The vestibular apparatus: semicircular canals detect rotation, the utricle detects horizontal acceleration, and the saccule detects vertical acceleration and gravity, each by bending hair cells embedded in a gel-like structure.
🎯 CONTEMPORARY RESEARCH — THE COCHLEAR IMPLANT
In sensorineural hearing loss, the hair cells of the cochlea are damaged or destroyed while the auditory nerve itself remains intact. A traditional hearing aid only amplifies sound — it does not help if the hair cells themselves are gone. The cochlear implant, developed over several decades starting in the 1960s and refined ever since, takes a different approach: it bypasses the hair cells entirely.

A modern cochlear implant has an external microphone and speech processor (worn behind the ear) and an internal electrode array surgically inserted directly into the cochlea. The processor analyzes incoming sound and stimulates different parts of the electrode array depending on the frequency content, exploiting the same tonotopic map the natural cochlea uses. The auditory nerve fires in response to the electrical pulses, and the brain learns to interpret the patterns as sound.

Cochlear implants have been used in hundreds of thousands of patients worldwide, including children born profoundly deaf. The results vary widely — many users acquire functional speech understanding, though the sound quality is reportedly mechanical compared to natural hearing. Recent research suggests that earlier implantation in children correlates with better speech and language outcomes. The technology is mature enough that it is no longer experimental, but research continues on improving signal processing, reducing surgical invasiveness, and combining electrical with residual acoustic hearing in patients with partial cochlear function.
Taste — the chemical sense in the mouth
Taste happens at taste buds, small clusters of receptor cells found mostly on the tongue but also on the soft palate, throat, and upper esophagus. Each taste bud responds to chemicals dissolved in saliva. Five primary tastes are recognized:
▶ Sweet — signals sugars and other energy-rich molecules.
▶ Salty — signals sodium and other ions.
▶ Sour — signals acids (low pH).
▶ Bitter — signals many alkaloid compounds, which are often (though not always) toxic. This is an old protective sense.
▶ Umami — the savory, meaty taste of glutamate and a few related amino-acid compounds. Officially recognized as the fifth basic taste in the early 1900s.
Taste signals from the front two-thirds of the tongue travel up the facial nerve (CN VII). Signals from the back third travel up the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX). Both routes converge in the solitary nucleus of the brainstem and from there reach the thalamus and the gustatory cortex (in the insula and parts of the frontal lobe). What you experience as “flavor” is mostly not taste — it is a combination of taste, smell, temperature, texture, and even pain (the “hot” of a chili pepper is the activation of pain receptors by capsaicin, not a taste).
Figure — Olfaction (odorant molecules → olfactory epithelium → olfactory bulb) and gustation (sapid molecules → taste buds on the tongue's papillae) both feed into the brain alongside trigeminal nerve branches from the face and mouth.
Smell — the only sense that skips the thalamus
Smell happens at the olfactory epithelium, a small patch of receptor cells in the roof of the nasal cavity. The receptor cells are themselves neurons — one of the only places in the body where neurons are continuously replaced from a population of stem cells. Each receptor cell expresses one of about 400 different odorant receptor types in humans. When a molecule of an odorant binds to a receptor, that cell fires.
Olfactory neuron axons pass up through the tiny holes in the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone (you drew this in Module 3) and synapse in the olfactory bulbs on the underside of the brain. From there, the signal travels directly to several regions of the cortex and the limbic system — without first passing through the thalamus. Smell is the only sense for which this is true, and it is one of the reasons certain smells produce such immediate, vivid emotional and memory responses: the olfactory signal reaches the amygdala and hippocampus (memory and emotion structures) on a very short route.
Loss of smell (anosmia) is a common neurological symptom. Sudden loss of smell after a head injury suggests damage to the olfactory neurons as they pass through the cribriform plate. Loss of smell over time is one of the earliest symptoms of Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease, sometimes appearing years before the more familiar symptoms.
