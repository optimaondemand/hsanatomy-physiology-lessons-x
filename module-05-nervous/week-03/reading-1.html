The Autonomic Nervous System — Fight or Flight, Rest or Digest

📖 READING
The nervous system you do not command
Most of what your nervous system does, you never notice. You did not consciously decide to speed up your heart when you stood up. You did not consciously decide to start digesting breakfast. You are not deciding, right now, how dilated your pupils should be for the light level around you, or what diameter the blood vessels in your fingers should hold. Yet all of these decisions are happening at this moment in your body, made by the autonomic nervous system (ANS) — the involuntary division of the PNS that you met in Reading 5.01.
The ANS controls smooth muscle (in blood vessels, the gut, the airways, the bladder, the iris), cardiac muscle, and glands (salivary, sweat, digestive, adrenal). It does not control skeletal muscle — that is the somatic division’s job. The ANS is organized in two opposing branches that work together to keep the body running at the right setting for what it’s doing.

Sympathetic — the “fight or flight” branch
The sympathetic nervous system is the body’s emergency response system. It prepares the body for action: running, fighting, lifting, surviving. When you are startled, frightened, or exerting yourself, sympathetic activity surges, and your body shifts into a configuration designed for short bursts of intense activity.
Anatomically, sympathetic neurons emerge from the spinal cord at the thoracic and upper lumbar levels (T1 through L2) — the “thoracolumbar” outflow. They synapse first in a chain of ganglia running alongside the spinal column (the sympathetic chain ganglia) before sending second-order fibers out to target organs. The chemical messengers are mostly norepinephrine at the target tissue, and acetylcholine at the ganglion. The adrenal medulla — which is essentially a modified sympathetic ganglion — dumps epinephrine directly into the bloodstream when activated, amplifying the body-wide response.
Sympathetic activation does these things at once:
▶ Heart rate and contractility increase. Blood pumps faster and harder.
▶ Blood vessels redistribute blood. Vessels to skeletal muscle dilate; vessels to skin and gut constrict. Blood is shunted to where it’s needed for fight or flight.
▶ Pupils dilate. More light enters; visual acuity in low light improves.
▶ Airways dilate. Bronchodilation lets more air reach the lungs. (This is why epinephrine treats acute asthma attacks.)
▶ Sweating increases. Cooling for the work to come.
▶ Digestion slows or stops. Gut motility decreases; digestive secretions decrease. (You are not absorbing breakfast in the middle of a fight.)
▶ Liver releases glucose. Energy stores mobilize for the muscles.
💡 THE PHYSIOLOGIST WHO COINED “FIGHT OR FLIGHT”
Walter Cannon (1871–1945) was an American physiologist who spent much of his career at Harvard Medical School working out how the body maintains stability in the face of physical and emotional stress. In a 1915 book called Bodily Changes in Pain, Hunger, Fear and Rage, he described the coordinated set of physiological changes that occur when an animal is suddenly threatened — heart rate up, pupils dilated, blood diverted to muscles, digestion suspended — and coined the now-familiar phrase “fight or flight.”

Twenty years later, in 1932, Cannon proposed a related and even more sweeping idea: homeostasis. He argued that all the body’s self-regulating mechanisms exist to maintain a relatively constant internal environment in the face of changing external conditions. Body temperature stays near 37 °C even when the outside air is 0 °C or 40 °C; blood pH stays within a narrow range; blood glucose, blood pressure, and water balance are all defended within tight limits. The mechanisms that defend these set points run mostly through the ANS and the endocrine system (Module 6). Cannon’s framing is the way every textbook still teaches physiology today. When you read “homeostasis” in this course or any other, you are reading Walter Cannon’s vocabulary.
Parasympathetic — the “rest and digest” branch
The parasympathetic nervous system does the opposite. It dominates when the body is calm, safe, fed, and not under threat — resting, digesting, conserving energy, rebuilding tissue. Most of the time, most of your organs are under parasympathetic control.
Anatomically, parasympathetic neurons emerge from a different set of CNS regions: certain cranial nerves (especially CN III, VII, IX, and X) and the sacral spinal cord (S2–S4). This is the “craniosacral” outflow. The vagus nerve (CN X) is the workhorse here — it carries about 75% of all parasympathetic fibers and reaches all the way down to the colon. The chemical messenger at both the ganglia and the target tissue is acetylcholine.
Parasympathetic activation does the opposite of sympathetic activation:
▶ Heart rate slows.
▶ Pupils constrict.
▶ Airways constrict.
▶ Digestion ramps up — gut motility increases, salivary and digestive secretions flow, bladder contracts.
▶ Glands release saliva, tears, mucus.

Dual innervation and tonic balance
Most organs are innervated by both branches at once. Your heart receives both sympathetic input (which speeds it up) and parasympathetic input (which slows it down). At any moment, your actual heart rate is the balance of these two opposing signals. Doctors talk about “sympathetic tone” and “parasympathetic tone” the way an audio engineer talks about the levels on a mixing board — they are continuous, not on-or-off, and what matters is the ratio.
A few systems are not so balanced. Most blood vessels in the body receive only sympathetic innervation; their diameter is controlled by changing the level of sympathetic tone (more tone = constricted; less tone = dilated). Sweat glands are also sympathetic-only, even though the neurotransmitter they use is acetylcholine (one of the few exceptions to the “sympathetic = norepinephrine” rule).
Why this matters clinically
Many common medications work by modifying autonomic activity. Beta-blockers for hypertension and heart-failure block sympathetic effects on the heart. Albuterol (an inhaler for asthma) selectively activates the sympathetic receptors that dilate airways. Atropine blocks parasympathetic acetylcholine receptors, used to dilate pupils for eye exams or to speed a dangerously slow heart. Antidepressants that affect norepinephrine work in part by changing sympathetic signaling.
When the autonomic system itself is damaged — in conditions like autonomic neuropathy, which sometimes accompanies long-standing diabetes — patients can have unstable blood pressure, gastric paralysis, bladder dysfunction, and abnormal sweating. The system you never notice is the system you cannot afford to lose.
